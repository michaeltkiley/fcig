# What financial conditions indexes are for: five bodies of work (11 Nov 2026)

Tags: [read] = the repository's literature notes record a read of the text (file named); [abstract] = abstract or working-paper version only; [secondhand] = from the pasted 7 Nov summary or memory, not checked. Sources: `experimental/path_fci/lit/` (`lit_hatzius.md`, `lit_neutral_fci_framing.md`, `lit_nfci_indexes_weights.md`, `lit_transmission_caballero_simsek.md`). Nothing here was re-read in this session; quotes are those in the notes.

**1. Hatzius, Hooper, Mishkin, Schoenholtz and Watson (2010), NBER WP 16150 / U.S. Monetary Policy Forum [read].** Purpose: summarize "the information about the future state of the economy contained in current financial variables", so that an index can (a) serve as one guide to the effective stance of policy "after taking into account all the other factors", like a Taylor rule (examples: the 2004-06 conundrum, late 2008 at the zero bound), and (b) isolate exogenous financial shocks from the endogenous reflection of past activity, hence the purge of each indicator on current and lagged GDP growth and inflation. They separate two families (weighted sums with weights from effects on GDP; principal components) and two readings (effect on growth, zero neutral for growth; tighter or looser than a base period). Their index is evaluated by predictive regressions of GDP, payrolls, production and unemployment 2 and 4 quarters ahead. Caveats they give: no micro-foundations, Lucas critique, instability over time. Dudley's comments in the same volume tie the equilibrium real funds rate to financial conditions (higher stock prices call for a higher funds rate).

**2. Brave and Butters (2011, 2012), Chicago Fed NFCI/ANFCI [read: FAQ, IJCB 2012; the 2011 text not read].** Purpose: monitor the financial system in real time, summarizing 105 weekly, monthly and quarterly indicators of risk, credit and leverage in one number (standard deviations from the sample average, positive = tighter than average); the FAQ attributes forecasting of GDP and investment two to four quarters ahead to the 2011 paper, and the 2012 paper separates "financial conditions" from "financial stress". Weights come from a mixed-frequency dynamic factor model (systemic and dynamic importance, not effect on activity). The ANFCI removes the part explained by activity and inflation, so a positive value means tighter than the macro conditions would suggest. Mostly spreads and volatility; the policy rate and the dollar have almost no weight.

**3. Ajello, Cavallo, Favara, Peterman, Schindler and Sinha (2023), FCI-G [read: FEDS Note].** Purpose: express financial conditions as their contribution to GDP growth over the next year, for communication and staff analysis ("a reading of 1 means a drag of about 100 basis points on GDP growth over the next year"). Seven variables (funds rate, 10-year yield, mortgage rate, BBB yield, stock prices, house prices, dollar); weights are FRB/US dynamic multipliers (difference of the four-quarter-ahead and the current multipliers) applied to changes, so the index is a flow built from cumulated past changes; zero means the cumulated effect on growth sums to zero (neutral for growth, not a neutral level). It avoids the problem that levels trend or drift.

**4. Adrian, Boyarchenko and Giannone (2019), "Vulnerable Growth," AER [abstract, NY Fed Staff Report 794; AER text not read].** Purpose of the financial conditions index in the paper: to show that conditions shift the whole conditional distribution of GDP growth, not only its mean. Deteriorating conditions raise conditional volatility and lower the conditional mean; the lower quantiles of growth vary with conditions while the upper quantiles are stable, so downside risk rises as conditions tighten (growth-at-risk). [secondhand: the conditioning variable is the Chicago Fed NFCI.] Implication for index design: an index is judged by what it says about downside risk, and the mean-only VAR of Note A does not address it.

**5. Caballero and Simsek, with Caravello (2024-26) [read: "Financial Conditions Targeting" (28 Sep 2026 version) and "FCI-plot" (26 Mar 2026); "FCI-star" read in the 7 Nov work as the January 2026 PDF].** Purposes: (a) policy communication and a policy target: conditions as the object a central bank steers when markets are noisy or "opinionated" and may misread policy ("FCI-plot", "Financial Conditions Targeting"; FCI-G is the observable index); (b) a neutral level: FCI* is the level of a financial conditions index consistent with output at potential, estimated in a Laubach-Williams-type model on FCI-G (1990Q2-2024Q4), the analogue of r*; in US data r* co-moves with the equity premium and FCI* does not, and FCI gaps give real-time guidance on the stance (for example 2022) [read intro and abstract only].

**What the five have in common, and what Note A has to supply for Note B.** All treat financial conditions as a bridge between policy and the economy: an index tells (i) the stance of policy beyond the policy rate, (ii) what is coming for activity (mean or tail), or (iii) what to communicate or target. They differ on whether the index must be exogenous (Hatzius et al., ANFCI purge), forward-looking (FCI-G), a stress monitor (NFCI) or neutral-relative (FCI*). Note B uses the market index as the channel through which a policy surprise reaches unemployment and prices, relative to r*; so what Note A has to establish is that the index (a) measures the policy-relevant part of conditions in comparable units, (b) is not an arbitrary choice among indexes, (c) carries what the literature asks of an index (information about activity, in flow form; the stance relative to neutral), and (d) behaves sensibly when measured relative to r*.
