\begin{abstract}
How much do financial conditions add to the stance of monetary policy beyond the policy rate? I measure conditions in policy units, as the move in the two-year yield that would bring the same change, and relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. The weights on the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar come from their responses to monetary policy news in announcement windows. Over 1976--2026 the index explains unemployment a year ahead beyond the real funds rate relative to $\rs$, and the funds-rate gap adds nothing to the index. The information is in the 12-month change of the index and, outside 2008--15, not in its level; it is absent for core inflation. In 2004--06 the index read \bl{abs:st:ep0:diff:point} points looser than the funds-rate gap, with an interval below zero. The 2022--24 reading, \bl{abs:st:ep1:diff:point} points, depends on the end date. The index complements the Chicago Fed's NFCI, which carries the longer horizons and recession risk. Removing what lagged unemployment and inflation explain cuts its unemployment response by \bl{pg:fall:full} to \bl{pg:fall:rec} percent. Put on a common basis (nominal, real, relative to $\rs$), the main indexes agree on direction and differ by about one point in level. The results for unemployment hold on 1996--2026, with smaller gains; those for payrolls do not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Is monetary policy tight or loose? The policy rate does not answer by itself. Policy works through long yields, stock prices and the dollar, and these respond to much else, so the same funds rate can be more or less restrictive at different times. An index of financial conditions has long been proposed as a guide to the stance of policy, a counterpart of the Taylor rule \citep{hatzius2010}, and policymakers describe policy this way. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}. The indexes do not always agree. In March 2024 the Chair said that ``there are many different financial conditions indicators, and you can kind of, you know, see different answers to that question'' \citep{powell2024}.

I ask how much financial conditions add to the stance of policy beyond the policy rate, and whether the difference says anything about the economy ahead.

They add, and the addition is informative about unemployment. I measure financial conditions with a market index in policy units: a one-point move in the index is the change in conditions that a policy move raising the two-year yield by one percentage point brings. The index and the funds rate are both measured relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. Over 1976--2026, adding the index to the real funds rate relative to $\rs$ raises the adjusted $R^2$ of the change in the unemployment gap a year ahead by \bl{st:du:flow:gain} ($t=$ \bl{st:du:flow:t}), while adding the funds-rate gap to the index adds \bl{st:du:flow:gainff}. The information is in the index's 12-month change, the form of the Board's FCI-G, and not in its level, except when 2008--15 is excluded. The index adds nothing for core inflation. In 2004--06 the index read \bl{abs:st:ep0:diff:point} points looser than the funds-rate gap, with an interval below zero; in 2022--24 the reading is \bl{abs:st:ep1:diff:point} points looser and depends on the end date (Figure~\ref{fig:wedge}).

The index complements the Chicago Fed's NFCI instead of replacing it: the NFCI carries the longer horizons and recession risk, and the market index carries the next six to twelve months. Between \bl{pg:fall:full} and \bl{pg:fall:rec} percent of the index's unemployment response is activity that lagged unemployment and inflation explain. The unemployment results hold on 1996--2026, the sample of the weights, with smaller gains, and the payroll results do not. Put on a common basis, the major indexes agree on direction and differ by about one point in level.

The approach has three features. First, I build the index and put it on a common basis. Its weights are the responses of the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the real dollar to monetary policy news in narrow windows around FOMC announcements, combined to recover the news as precisely as possible. A two-variable VAR with the unemployment gap gives it a level and a flow. I measure every index in three steps, nominal rates, real rates and gaps to $\rs$, and at each step I compare like with like: my nominal index with nominal versions of the Goldman Sachs index, FCI-G and an index weighted with FRB/US, my real index with real versions, and my $\rs$ index with $\rs$ versions. Second, I compare the index with the real funds rate relative to $\rs$ and ask whether it adds for unemployment, payrolls and inflation, in sample and out of sample, with the uncertainty of the weights and of $\rs$ in the statements about episodes. Finally, I ask whether the flow is only activity reflected back, how it relates to the NFCI, whether the neutral-rate step and the weights matter for outcomes, and whether the results hold on 1996--2026 and with data as known at the time.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{The stance of policy.} The first purpose of a financial conditions index in \citet{hatzius2010} is to serve as one guide to the effective stance of policy after taking into account all other factors, as a Taylor rule does; their examples are the conundrum of 2004--06, when conditions stayed easy as the funds rate rose, and late 2008, when conditions were restrictive at a zero funds rate. \citet{dudley2010} adds that higher stock prices and faster productivity growth raise the equilibrium rate, so that the same funds rate is less restrictive. I measure the wedge between an index and the funds-rate gap in common units and test whether it matters.

\textbf{Units and flows.} \citet{ajello2023} express conditions as their drag on GDP growth over the next year, with weights from FRB/US multipliers applied to changes, so that FCI-G is a flow with zero neutral for growth. \citet{caballero2026} define FCI$^*$, the level of FCI-G consistent with output at potential, as the analogue of $\rs$. I express indexes in policy units, give the market index a flow of the FCI-G form, and measure rates relative to $\rs$; I do not estimate a neutral level for the index itself.

\textbf{Stress, risk and the tail.} The Chicago Fed's NFCI and ANFCI summarize 105 indicators of risk, credit and leverage by a dynamic factor model \citep{brave2011}; \citet{adrian2019} show that conditions shift the lower quantiles of growth. I treat them as a different kind of index, set the market index beside the NFCI, and report the lower tail in sample only.

\textbf{Weights and comparisons.} Weights come from effects on output \citep{dudley2000,hatzius2018,ajello2023}, from principal components \citep{brave2011} or, as here, from event-study regressions \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005,bernanke2005,nakamura2018,bauer2023}. \citet{aramonte2013} compare twelve indexes by correlations of standardized series, \citet{arnaut2024} estimate the response of the Goldman Sachs index to policy surprises, and \citet{sack2023} relate FCI-G to policy-induced changes in the two-year yield. I put the indexes on one basis and ask what the market index adds to the stance, and I report the sampling error of its weights.

\textbf{The neutral rate.} What matters is the rate relative to the neutral rate, which fell from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}. I test whether measuring the inputs relative to $\rs$ improves what the index says about the economy and report the answer as it is.

The first difference is the source of the weights. The Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G take them from models of how each variable affects activity, the NFCI from the data's principal components, and my market index from how each variable responds to policy. The second is the reading. A level index says where conditions stand relative to a base period, as the Goldman Sachs index and the NFCI do. A growth index says what conditions imply for activity: FCI-G measures the contribution of financial conditions to GDP growth over the next year, and zero means that the effect of current and past changes adds nothing to growth and takes nothing from it. Zero in FCI-G is therefore neutral for growth, which is a different idea from a neutral level of conditions. FCI-G is built from changes and has no level. The third is the treatment of rates. The Goldman Sachs index uses nominal rates and the nominal dollar, so it trends over long spans \citep{hatzius2010}. FCI-G uses nominal rates as well.

Units differ too. I convert the Goldman Sachs index and the NFCI and ANFCI to the market index's units by their own responses to the same policy news: the Goldman Sachs index rises by \bl{resp:gs} points per unit of policy news, the NFCI by \bl{resp:nfci} standard deviations and the ANFCI by \bl{resp:anfci}. FCI-G stays in percentage points of GDP growth and is compared through correlations and through its own scale.

\subsection{The Market Index: Weights from Policy News}\label{sec:market}
\textbf{Policy news.} At each FOMC announcement I measure policy news with the factor of \citet{nakamura2018}. It is the first principal component of the moves in five short-term interest-rate futures in a narrow window around the announcement, scaled so that one unit moves the two-year Treasury yield by one percentage point. The data are the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}, ``monetary events'' windows: \bl{n:events} announcements from \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}.

\textbf{Responses and weights.} In each window the 10-year yield, the equity valuation and the real dollar move by a response times the policy news plus a part that has nothing to do with it. The windows reveal the response $\psi_j$ of each variable $j$. I choose the weights that recover the policy news most precisely from the three variables together: $w\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$, where $\Sigma$ is the covariance of the parts that are not policy news, normalized so that $w'\psi=1$ (one-factor generalized least squares). A policy move that raises the two-year yield by one percentage point therefore raises the index by exactly one unit, and the index is measured in points of the two-year yield.

Table~\ref{tab:weights} gives the results. A policy move that raises the two-year yield one point raises the 10-year yield by \bl{psi:ten} points, lowers equity valuation by \bl{abs:psi:eq} percent and raises the real dollar by \bl{psi:usd} percent. Each variable carries a substantial part of the policy move: the shares are \bl{share:ten} for the 10-year yield, \bl{share:eq} for equity valuation and \bl{share:usd} for the dollar. Applied to the demeaned levels of the 10-year yield, the equity valuation multiple (100 times the log of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio, CAPE) and the real broad dollar, the weights give the index.

\input{../replication/report/tab_weights}

\textbf{What the weights assume.} Consider a rise in stock prices. If it comes from news about earnings, it has little to do with policy, and the index treats it as if it did, because it enters with the same weight as a rise that policy caused. If every movement in the three variables came from policy, the index would equal the cumulated policy-induced change in the two-year yield. The weights say what the variables do when policy moves, not what causes their movements at other times. An announcement can also reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and then the responses mix policy and information \citep{nakamura2018}. I measure the responses to the news as the markets receive it, and I set the information effect aside. The index is a measure of the policy-equivalent tightening in financial conditions. It is not a forecast of activity, and Section~\ref{sec:var} gives it dynamics separately.

\subsection{The Index Weighted with FRB/US}\label{sec:frbus}
The main alternative to market weights is to weight each variable by its effect on activity in a macroeconomic model, as FCI-G does. For a comparison of levels I build such an index from FRB/US \citep{brayton2014}. Each variable's weight is its effect on GDP after four quarters of a persistent unit shift, divided by the effect of a persistent one percentage point rise in the federal funds rate (\bl{frbus:yardstick} percent of GDP), so the index is in funds-rate points. It covers the policy rate, the 10-year yield, the mortgage and BBB spreads, equity valuation and the dollar, and in one version house prices. I also give it a flow in the form of FCI-G.

Table~\ref{tab:frbus} compares the two sets of weights on the three common variables. They agree on the 10-year yield, where the FRB/US weight is \bl{ratio:ten} times the market weight. The model puts less weight on equity valuation (\bl{ratio:eq} times) and more on the dollar (\bl{ratio:usd} times). The two indexes are close on a pure policy move. The FRB/US weights price the response of the three variables to a unit of policy news at \bl{spsi:market} funds-rate points, against exactly one point for the market index. They differ on movements that policy did not cause. The model gives a fall in equity prices its estimated causal effect on output, and the market index treats it as policy-equivalent. Because the two weight vectors are not proportional, the gap between the indexes is a weighted sum of non-policy movements in equity valuation and the dollar.

\section{A Common Basis}\label{sec:steps}
The comparison runs in three steps, and each step answers a problem of the one before.

\subsection{Nominal Rates}\label{sec:nominal}
The first step uses rates as published. Every index is then a function of nominal yields, and the Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G also use the nominal broad dollar. A nominal index treats a 10-year yield of 8 percent alike in 1981 and in 2000. That is reasonable only if inflation expectations and the neutral real rate are stable, and neither was. Expected inflation over the next year averaged \bl{einf:1981} percent in 1981 and is \bl{einf:latest} percent today, and the Laubach--Williams estimate of $\rs$ averaged \bl{rstar:lw:pre} percent over 1990--2007 and \bl{rstar:lw:post} percent over 2009--2016 (Figure~\ref{fig:infl}). The nominal dollar poses a further problem for the Goldman Sachs index. Its broad index rises from \bl{usd:nom:1973} in 1973 to \bl{usd:nom:latest} today, mostly from inflation differentials, and this drags the Goldman Sachs index away from the others before 1990 (Figure~\ref{fig:levels}, top panel).

\subsection{Real Rates}\label{sec:real}
The second step measures each rate less expected inflation at a matched horizon: the policy rate less one-year expected inflation, and longer rates less ten-year expected inflation. The dollar is the real broad index in every index, and in FCI-G stock and house prices are deflated by the CPI. This step removes the inflation trend. The nominal and real market indexes are almost the same over 1990--2026, with a correlation of \bl{step:nom:real:ninety}, because inflation expectations were stable then. The real step matters before 1990. Nominal FCI-G shows a tightening in mid-1974 (\bl{nominal:fcig:seventyfour}) that the real version does not (about zero), because nominal rates rose with expected inflation.

\subsection{Gaps to the Neutral Rate}\label{sec:rstarstep}
The third step measures each real rate relative to $\rs$: the policy rate, the 10-year yield, and in FCI-G the BBB and mortgage yields. Equity valuation enters through the earnings yield $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$, which is also an implicit real rate, as the gap $E/P-\rs$. I convert each index's equity weight at the sample-average earnings yield, so that every index keeps its weight on CAPE. Spreads over the 10-year yield are unchanged, because $\rs$ cancels. The baseline uses the one-sided, full-sample Laubach--Williams estimate, and Kiley's estimate is a check. Each variable is measured relative to its 1973--2026 average, so $\rs$ matters through its movements over time.

\subsection{What Each Step Changes}\label{sec:whatsteps}
Figure~\ref{fig:marketsteps} shows the market index at each step. The real step changes little after 1990, and the $\rs$ step changes a lot: the correlation of the real and $\rs$ versions over 1990--2026 is only \bl{step:real:rstar:ninety}. With $\rs$ low after 2008, the step to $\rs$ raises the 2009--2016 average of each index: by \bl{shift:mkt:gfc} points for the market index, \bl{shift:gs:gfc} for the Goldman Sachs index and \bl{shift:frbus:gfc} for the FRB/US index. Relative to the 1990--2026 average, the market index averages \bl{rstar:mkt:gfc} at the $\rs$ step against \bl{real:mkt:gfc} in the real version and \bl{nominal:mkt:gfc} in the nominal one, so conditions in 2009--2016 look tighter than average rather than easier. The four level indexes (the market index, the Goldman Sachs index and the FRB/US index with and without house prices) are closer at each step: their average range over 1990--2026 falls from \bl{nominal:range:ninety} points (nominal) to \bl{real:range:ninety} (real) and \bl{rstar:range:ninety} (relative to $\rs$).

\section{Level and Flow}\label{sec:var}
FCI-G measures a flow, and the other indexes measure a level. I give the market index both. The level is the index of Section~\ref{sec:market}. The flow comes from the relationship between the index and unemployment.

\subsection{The Response of Unemployment}\label{sec:varresp}
I estimate a monthly VAR in the unemployment gap (the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate) and the market index, from January 1973 to September 2026, with 12 lags and monthly dummies for 2020. Unemployment is ordered first, so it responds to the index only with a lag. I repeat the estimation for each step of the construction. Table~\ref{tab:var} and Figure~\ref{fig:var} give the results.

The index helps predict unemployment at every step (in the $\rs$ baseline the Granger statistic is \bl{var:rstar:p12:granger:F}, $p$ \bl{var:rstar:p12:granger:p}). A one-unit tightening raises the unemployment gap gradually. In the baseline the response reaches \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12} percentage point after 12 months, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12:lo90} to \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12:hi90}. With an Okun coefficient of two this is about \bl{okun:phi12} percent of output. I call the 12-month response the yardstick of the index, $\Phi(12)$. With four lags the response is smaller (\bl{var:rstar:p4:phi12}) and levels off earlier. The baseline has 12 lags; Section~\ref{sec:rob} reports four.

\subsection{The Forward Impetus}\label{sec:fi}
With the response profile $\Phi(m)$ I compute what the changes in the index over the past year imply for the next 12 months, in the way FCI-G does with FRB/US multipliers:
\[
\text{FI}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{11}\frac{\Phi(m+12)-\Phi(m)}{\Phi(12)}\,\Delta M(t-m).
\]
Here $M$ is the market index. A value of 1 means that the past year's changes raise unemployment over the next year by as much as a permanent one-unit tightening does in its first year. I call this the forward impetus. Figure~\ref{fig:levelflow} shows the level and the flow in the baseline. The flow correlates \bl{rstar:fi:fcig} with the $\rs$ version of FCI-G, and the level only \bl{rstar:fcig:level}, because the two answer different questions.

\section{The Stance beyond the Policy Rate}\label{sec:stance}
\subsection{The Funds-Rate Gap and the Wedge}\label{sec:wedge}
In this section and the next, the flow of an index is its 12-month change; the forward impetus of Section~\ref{sec:fi} is a weighted version of it.

The stance of policy read from the policy rate is the funds-rate gap: the effective federal funds rate less one-year expected inflation less $\rs$. The stance read from financial conditions is the market index relative to $\rs$. Each is measured relative to its 1973--2026 mean. They differ in units, two-year-yield points for the index and funds-rate points for the gap, and the comparison treats a point of each as a point of policy. The wedge is the index less the gap. A policy move that raises the two-year yield raises both. A rise in the dollar or a fall in equity valuation that policy did not cause raises only the index, and the wedge is the part of conditions that the funds rate does not carry. Levels of the two correlate \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:level:full} over 1976--2026 and their 12-month changes \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:flow:full}; they are \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:level:p0406} and \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:flow:p0406} in 2004--06, when the funds rate rose and conditions did not follow.

\subsection{Does the Wedge Matter for the Economy Ahead?}\label{sec:stancetest}
Table~\ref{tab:stance} adds the index to the funds-rate gap in projections of the change in the unemployment gap, payroll growth and core inflation 12 months ahead.

\input{../replication/report/tab_stance}

\textbf{Unemployment.} The funds-rate gap alone explains little (the $t$-statistic of its 12-month change is \bl{st:du:flow:tff}), and the index alone explains much (\bl{st:du:flow:tidx}). Given the gap, the index raises the adjusted $R^2$ by \bl{st:du:flow:gain} in the flow form and by \bl{st:du:mixed:gain} when the gap enters as a level; given the index, the gap adds \bl{st:du:flow:gainff} and \bl{st:du:mixed:gainff}. In levels the index adds \bl{st:du:level:gain} ($t=$ \bl{st:du:level:t}). The result holds for the Goldman Sachs index (gain \bl{st:du:gs:gain}) and the FRB/US index (\bl{st:du:frbus:gain}), and the $t$-statistic of the market index rises with the step, from \bl{st:du:nominal:t} in nominal terms to \bl{st:du:real:t} in real terms and \bl{st:du:flow:t} relative to $\rs$.

\textbf{Out of sample.} Recursive forecasts from 1995 have root mean squared errors of \bl{st:du:flow:rmse} (flow) and \bl{st:du:mixed:rmse} (mixed) times those of the gap alone, with Clark--West statistics of \bl{st:du:flow:cw} and \bl{st:du:mixed:cw}. The primary family has nine tests, three outcomes in three forms; five reject at the 10 percent level before adjustment, and one survives the Holm correction (the mixed form for unemployment, adjusted $p=$ \bl{st:prim:mixed:holm}). The out-of-sample gains are modest.

\textbf{Payrolls and inflation.} For payrolls the in-sample gain is \bl{st:pay:flow:gain} in the flow form and the out-of-sample statistic is \bl{st:pay:flow:cw}; on 1996--2026 the payroll flow is not distinguishable from zero (Section~\ref{sec:sample96}). For core inflation the index adds nothing (gain \bl{st:pi:flow:gain}). The claim is about unemployment.

\textbf{Which periods.} In 2009--15, when the policy rate was at its lower bound, the index adds in sample ($t=$ \bl{st:du:zlb:t}, \bl{st:du:zlb:n} months) and not significantly out of sample (Clark--West \bl{st:du:zlb:cw} in the flow form and \bl{st:du:zlb:cwm} in the mixed form). In 2022--26 the sample is about three independent years and the coefficient on the index has the opposite sign ($t=$ \bl{st:du:post:t}): the tightening of 2022--23 was not followed by higher unemployment.

\subsection{The Flow, Not the Level}\label{sec:levelflow}

\subsection{The NFCI}\label{sec:nfci}
The NFCI and ANFCI beat the common-basis indexes for unemployment at long horizons and for recessions. They are a different index: a level index of credit, risk and leverage, mostly spreads. The three variables of the market index explain \bl{nfci:decomp:level:nfci:3:vars:R2} of the NFCI's level, and \bl{nfci:decomp:level:nfci:3:vars:plus:spread:R2} when the Baa--10-year spread is added. At 24 months only the NFCI remains (\bl{nf:du24:nfci:t}, against \bl{nf:du24:mkt:t}), and for recessions the NFCI has $t=$ \bl{nf:rec1212:nfci:t} against \bl{nf:rec1212:mkt:t}. The two are complements: the market index is a policy-channel flow in policy units that carries the next six to twelve months, and the NFCI carries the longer horizons and recession risk. It is not a stress or recession monitor.

\subsection{Is the Flow Only Activity Reflected Back?}\label{sec:purge}
Asset prices respond to expected activity, and \citet{hatzius2010} purge each indicator of current and lagged growth and inflation so that the index reflects exogenous conditions; the ANFCI does the same. I regress each input on the unemployment gap and core inflation (current and 12 lags), on the full sample and recursively, and rebuild the index from the residuals with the same weights. Table~\ref{tab:purge} gives the VAR response. The 12-month response of the market index falls from \bl{pg:market:unpurged} to \bl{pg:market:full} with the full-sample purge and \bl{pg:market:recursive} with the recursive purge, and every interval stays above zero; the Goldman Sachs index is similar. Purging is not identification, and it removes too much when asset prices rightly respond to expected activity. The flow carries both a channel and an anticipation component, and for payrolls it does not survive the purge.

\subsection{The Neutral Rate and the Weights}\label{sec:rstarweights}
\textbf{The neutral rate.} The $\rs$ step makes the level of the index mean the same thing across regimes to a degree. At a zero unemployment gap the index level in 2009--19 is \bl{abs:nu:nominalma} lower than in 1990--2007 in nominal terms and \bl{abs:nu:realma} lower in real terms, and \bl{nu:rstarma} higher relative to $\rs$ (Laubach--Williams) and \bl{nu:rstar:kileyma} higher with the estimate of \citet{kiley2020} (Table~\ref{tab:neutrality}). The step reverses and shrinks the post-2008 drift but does not make the unemployment response constant across periods (Section~\ref{sec:sample96}), and the unit restriction it imposes on $\rs$ cannot be tested at this power. For outcomes, the $\rs$ versions have lower out-of-sample error than the nominal versions in \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:ratio:below1} of \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:tests} comparisons across seven index families, five outcomes and horizons; \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:dm:sig10:better} Diebold--Mariano tests favor $\rs$ and \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:dm:sig10:worse} the nominal version at the 10 percent level, none survives the Holm correction at 5 percent, and the mean error ratio is \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:mean:rmse:ratio}. The tests share data, so the counts are descriptive. For unemployment the in-sample gain of the $\rs$ version over the nominal version is \bl{ld:p96:dr2} on 1996--2026 (interval \bl{ld:p96:lo} to \bl{ld:p96:hi}) and \bl{ld:pre96:dr2} before 1996. The outcomes lean toward the $\rs$ basis without proving it; it rests on the argument of \citet{dudley2010} and \citet{caballero2026} that the rate relative to neutral is what matters.

\textbf{The weights.} No alternative weights beat the market weights. Replacing them with the FRB/US weights, the first principal component, equal weights or weights fitted to activity changes the mean squared error by between \bl{wt:level:min} and \bl{wt:level:max} percent in levels and between \bl{wt:change:min} and \bl{wt:change:max} percent in 12-month changes. Of \bl{w:rstar:n:primary:tests} paired tests, \bl{w:rstar:n:raw:sig10} reject at 10 percent before adjustment and \bl{w:rstar:n:holm:sig10} after, so no alternative is significantly better and the market weights are not significantly better either. Weights fitted to activity are too imprecise to rank any set. Outcomes cannot settle the weights question.

\subsection{Levels}\label{sec:levels}
\textbf{The market index and the Goldman Sachs index agree once both are real.} With real rates or gaps to $\rs$ the two correlate \bl{rstar:mkt:gs} in levels and \bl{rstar:mkt:gs:chg} in 12-month changes. In nominal terms the level correlation is \bl{nominal:mkt:gs}, against \bl{nominal:mkt:frbus} with the FRB/US index, and the Goldman Sachs index is the one that uses the nominal dollar at that step.

\textbf{The market index and the FRB/US index agree at every step in changes, and less in levels since 1990.} The correlation over 1974--2026 is \bl{nominal:mkt:frbus} in nominal terms, \bl{real:mkt:frbus} in real terms and \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus} relative to $\rs$. The 1970s and early 1980s, when all indexes swung widely, dominate these figures; since 1990 the correlations are \bl{nominal:mkt:frbus:ninety}, \bl{real:mkt:frbus:ninety} and \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:ninety}, and with the Goldman Sachs index \bl{rstar:mkt:gs:ninety} at the $\rs$ step. Correlations of persistent levels overstate agreement, and the 12-month changes (\bl{rstar:mkt:gs:chg} and \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:chg}) are the better guide to whether the indexes move together. Adding house prices to the FRB/US index does not change this (\bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:hp}). Both indexes use the real dollar at every step, so the common basis moves the Goldman Sachs index and leaves them where they were. The level correlation with the FRB/US index falls slightly with each step, and the correlation of 12-month changes is \bl{nominal:mkt:frbus:chg}, \bl{real:mkt:frbus:chg} and \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:chg}.

\textbf{The ANFCI is a different object.} Its correlation with the market index is \bl{rstar:mkt:anfci} from 1990. The ANFCI measures stress, mostly spreads and volatility, rather than the price of credit and risk.

\textbf{The distance between the indexes falls with each step, and stays near a point since 1990.} The average range of the four level indexes, each in its own units (two-year-yield points or funds-rate points), is \bl{nominal:range:long} points over 1974--2026 in nominal terms, \bl{real:range:long} in real terms and \bl{rstar:range:long} relative to $\rs$. Over 1990--2026 it is \bl{nominal:range:ninety}, \bl{real:range:ninety} and \bl{rstar:range:ninety}. In \bl{rstar:share:range:gt1:ninety} percent of the months since 1990 the indexes differ by more than one point. Correlations measure whether the indexes move together. The range measures whether they say the same thing about the level of conditions, and it is the more demanding test. It depends on the units: the market index's standard deviation is \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:sd} times that of the FRB/US index, so part of the range is a difference of scale, and the Goldman Sachs conversion is itself estimated (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). All indexes share the same base period, so the range measures dispersion around each index's own average and not a difference in average level.

\subsection{Flows}\label{sec:flows}
The market index's forward impetus correlates \bl{nominal:fi:fcig} with FCI-G at the nominal step, \bl{real:fi:fcig} at the real step and \bl{rstar:fi:fcig} at the $\rs$ step. Against the published FCI-G the correlation is \bl{nominal:fi:pub} over 1990--2026, and my replication of FCI-G tracks the published series at \bl{nominal:fcig:pub}. The flow built from FRB/US multipliers agrees with FCI-G more closely (\bl{rstar:fcig:frbus:gi} at the $\rs$ step). The market index's flow comes from a small VAR, not from a structural model, and it agrees with both: its correlation with the FRB/US growth impetus is \bl{rstar:fi:frbus:gi}.

\subsection{Where They Differ and How Precisely the Weights Are Estimated}\label{sec:wheresummary}
The widest gaps between the market index and the FRB/US index are in 1977--80 and in 1998--2001, and equity valuation accounts for most of them; today the market index reads \bl{rstar:latest:market} and the FRB/US index \bl{rstar:latest:frbus}. The weights are imprecise: the 90 percent interval of the weight on the 10-year yield runs from \bl{wb:ten:lo} to \bl{wb:ten:hi}, and of the market index's reading for September 2026 from \bl{bt:rstar:latest:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:latest:hi}, which covers the Goldman Sachs reading (\bl{rstar:latest:gs}) and nearly reaches the FRB/US reading. The sampling error of the weights is of the same size as the differences between the readings. Appendix~\ref{app:differ} gives the episodes, the groups behind today's readings and the bootstrap.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
Three points follow for reading the stance of policy.

\textbf{Financial conditions add to the policy rate, through changes.} The funds-rate gap tells little about unemployment a year ahead once the index is in, and the index tells much once the gap is in. What it tells is in how conditions have moved over the year, in policy units and relative to $\rs$, not in where they stand. In 2004--06 and in 2022--24 the index read more than a point looser than the gap, and in September 2026 the wedge is \bl{st:today:wedge}. These are readings of the stance with intervals, and the stance from the policy rate alone misses them.

\textbf{The index is a channel measure, not a forecaster or a stress monitor.} It carries the next six to twelve months of unemployment, in a form that survives purging of lagged activity at a third to a half of its size. It does not carry inflation, payrolls on 1996--2026 or recession risk, and the NFCI carries the longer horizons. Out-of-sample gains are modest.

\textbf{The neutral rate changes the readings.} In nominal terms the market index reads \bl{nominal:latest:market} points for September 2026, relative to $\rs$ \bl{rstar:latest:market}, and the step reverses the picture after 2008. The indexes agree on direction (12-month changes correlate \bl{rstar:mkt:gs:chg} with the Goldman Sachs index and \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus:chg} with the FRB/US index) and differ in level by about \bl{rstar:range:long} point on average, of the order of the sampling error of the market index's weights. Outcomes lean toward the $\rs$ basis, and the policy-news weights are not beaten.

\textbf{What the results do not show.} There is no ground truth for financial conditions. Zero is the 1973--2026 average of each variable, not a neutral level of conditions. The index measures the policy-equivalent tightening and treats movements that policy did not cause as if it had; the unemployment response is the claim, and the index carries both a channel and an anticipation component.
\FloatBarrier

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Financial conditions add to the stance of policy beyond the policy rate. Measured in policy units and relative to the neutral rate, the market index explains unemployment a year ahead beyond the real funds rate relative to $\rs$, through its 12-month change, and not inflation. It complements the NFCI, which carries the longer horizons and recession risk, and it holds on 1996--2026 at smaller size. The wedge between the index and the funds-rate gap was about \bl{abs:st:ep0:diff:point} points in 2004--06 and \bl{abs:st:ep1:diff:point} points in 2022--24. On a common basis the indexes agree on direction and differ by about a point in level, and the neutral rate changes the reading most. The most durable finding is the wedge and what it says for unemployment, and the answer to what a statement about the stance should say is a range, relative to $\rs$.

\section{Construction and Estimation Details}\label{app:details}
\textbf{Data.} All series are monthly averages from January 1973 to September 2026, from the public sources in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The BBB spread is Moody's Baa yield less the 10-year yield. Months in which the CPI and unemployment releases are missing (October 2025) are interpolated. The unemployment gap is the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate, placed in the middle month of each quarter and interpolated. Expected inflation is the Cleveland Fed's estimate at one and ten years from 1982 and the Livingston Survey's 12-month CPI expectation before, shifted to the Cleveland level by the mean difference over 1982--84 and blended over 1982. CAPE is Shiller's series spliced over 1990--92 in logs to the monthly average of a daily series. The real broad dollar is spliced in 2006 in logs, as are the nominal dollar and the S\&P 500. Case--Shiller home prices before 1987 are Shiller's series.

\textbf{Market index.} Policy news is the first principal component of MP1, MP2, ED2, ED3 and ED4 in the monetary-event windows, scaled by its regression on the two-year yield change so that one unit moves the two-year yield by one percentage point. Unscheduled events with no change in MP1 and March--April 2020 are dropped. Each of the 10-year yield change, the S\&P 500 return and the dollar (DXY) return in the window is regressed on the factor. The residual covariance $\Sigma$ of the three regressions gives $w=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$. The S\&P 500 and DXY weights apply to 100 times the log of CAPE and of the real broad dollar, as percent changes in the window and in the index levels agree to first order.

\textbf{Goldman Sachs index.} The weights are the published ones: policy rate 4.4, 10-year yield 45.1, BBB spread 39.6, equity valuation $-4.9$ and dollar 6.0 percent, applied to the demeaned inputs. The conversion to the market index's units is the inverse of the response of a daily replication of the index to policy news, measured from the day before to the day after each announcement because its dollar input is a noon series (\bl{resp:gs} points per unit of news, standard error \bl{se:resp:gs}).

\textbf{FCI-G.} I use the published multipliers of the Board's release. Each of the seven variables enters as the change over three months of its three-month average (rates, in points), 100 times the three-month log change (stock and house prices) or 100 times the log change of the three-month average (the dollar), with the multipliers of the first four quarters for the one-year lookback and the first twelve for the three-year lookback. This replication tracks the published series at \bl{nominal:fcig:pub} in monthly levels from 1990. At the $\rs$ step the stock term is the change in the earnings-yield gap.

\textbf{FRB/US index.} The weights are the cumulative GDP responses at four quarters to a persistent unit shift in each variable, simulated in FRB/US with VAR-based expectations, divided by the response to a persistent one percentage point rise in the funds rate. The weight on the policy rate is its direct effect with expectations of future rates held fixed. House prices enter as 100 times the log of the price-to-rent ratio relative to its trailing 15-year average. The growth impetus applies the differences of the quarterly responses at horizons $m/3+4$ and $m/3$ to the changes of the last 12 months, the form of FCI-G.

\textbf{NFCI and ANFCI.} The weekly index at the last observation of each month; the conversion is the response of the weekly change to the sum of policy news in the week (Friday-dated weeks; heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-robust standard errors with four lags).

\textbf{Gaps to $\rs$.} For the policy rate, the 10-year yield and the Baa and mortgage yields, the input is the real rate less $\rs$. Equity valuation becomes $-(100/\overline{E/P})(E/P-\rs)$, where $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$ and $\overline{E/P}$ is its 1973--2026 mean, so that a one-point rise of CAPE (in logs) has the same weight as before when $E/P$ is at its mean. $\rs$ is the one-sided, full-sample estimate (New York Fed's Laubach--Williams estimate, data through 2026Q2) placed in the middle month of each quarter.

\textbf{VAR and bootstrap.} The VAR has a constant, the 12 monthly dummies for 2020 and $p$ lags of the unemployment gap and the index. Because unemployment is first, the response of unemployment to an innovation that raises the index by one unit on impact is the moving-average coefficient $\Phi(h)$ and does not depend on the residual covariance. The bootstrap resamples blocks of 12 consecutive residual pairs, simulates the VAR recursively from the actual initial values with the dummies fixed, and re-estimates; bands are percentiles of the \bl{boot:reps} draws. The same draws give the intervals of the flow's correlation with FCI-G. The weights bootstrap resamples announcements with replacement and recomputes the policy-news factor and the weights each time. Draws use the seed in \texttt{lib/config.py}.

\subsection{How Precisely Is the Market Index Estimated?}\label{sec:precision}
The market index's weights come from regressions on \bl{n:events} announcements, and they are estimated with error. I resample the announcements with replacement \bl{boot:reps} times, recompute the policy-news factor and the weights each time, and recompute the index and its comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:boot}).

The weights are imprecise. The weight on the 10-year yield has a 90 percent interval from \bl{wb:ten:lo} to \bl{wb:ten:hi} around \bl{w:ten}. The interval for equity valuation runs from \bl{wb:eq:lo} to \bl{wb:eq:hi}, and for the dollar from \bl{wb:usd:lo} to \bl{wb:usd:hi}. The FRB/US weights on all three lie inside these intervals, the equity weight near the edge: it is lower in absolute value than \bl{frw:eq:pct} percent of the bootstrap draws, about 14 percent in a two-sided test. The data do not reject that the model weights equal the market weights. This is a statement about power, not a finding that they are equal, and it treats the FRB/US weights and the VAR as fixed.

The index is better determined than its weights, and its correlations with the others are stable. The correlation with the Goldman Sachs index is \bl{rstar:mkt:gs}, with a 5th to 95th percentile range from \bl{bt:rstar:corr:gs:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:corr:gs:hi}, and with the FRB/US index \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus}, from \bl{bt:rstar:corr:frbus:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:corr:frbus:hi}. The level of the reading is not stable. The September 2026 reading relative to $\rs$ is \bl{rstar:latest:market}, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{bt:rstar:latest:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:latest:hi}. The Goldman Sachs reading (\bl{rstar:latest:gs}) lies inside that interval. The FRB/US reading (\bl{rstar:latest:frbus}) lies just above it, and the 90 percent interval for the market index minus the FRB/US index runs from \bl{gap:frbus:lo} to \bl{gap:frbus:hi}. The sampling error of the weights is therefore of the same size as the differences between the readings, and the Goldman Sachs and FRB/US readings lie on opposite sides of the 1973--2026 average.

\subsection{The Sample: 1996--2026}\label{sec:sample96}
The weights come from announcements since 1996, so the sample of 1996--2026 is the one on which the index is estimated, and the sample of Note B. I re-estimate the VAR and the regressions from 1996. The response of unemployment after 12 months is \bl{ck:a12:phi} (\bl{ck:a12:lo} to \bl{ck:a12:hi}) on 1973--2026, and \bl{ck:b12:phi} (\bl{ck:b12:lo} to \bl{ck:b12:hi}) with 12 lags and \bl{ck:b4:phi} (\bl{ck:b4:lo} to \bl{ck:b4:hi}) with four lags on 1996--2026: it does not change sign, and the interval is about 1.6 times as wide. \subsection{Weights That Move}\label{sec:drift}
The weights define the unit, so I hold them fixed and show how they would drift. Re-estimating on an expanding window of announcements from 2006 keeps the 10-year and dollar weights inside the 90 percent band of the full-sample estimate at every date after 2010; the equity weight is outside at \bl{exp:post2010:n:out:eq} dates in 2011--14, by at most \bl{exp:post2010:min:eq} against a band edge of \bl{base:lo:eq} (Figure~\ref{fig:weightpath}). \subsection{Limits of the Design}\label{sec:limits}
The $\rs$ step is the largest effect in the paper, and the outcomes lean toward it without proving it. The uncertainty of $\rs$ is not in the intervals, and $\rs$ is not neutral to outcomes, because the Laubach--Williams model uses output, inflation and unemployment. The weights come from announcements since 1996 and are applied from 1973; they assume that the responses reflect policy rather than information effects \citep{nakamura2018}, and if policy came to target financial conditions the responses could change, the Lucas-critique concern that \citet{hatzius2010} raise for all indexes. The bootstrap treats the FRB/US and Goldman Sachs weights, the Goldman Sachs conversion and the VAR as fixed. The sample has five recessions, two out of sample, and 12-month outcomes overlap, so the counts of tests are descriptive. The VAR is linear and describes the mean. The Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G are my replications, and the FRB/US index is my construction at one horizon. The readings for September 2026 use $\rs$ through 2026Q2 and interpolate October 2025.
