\begin{abstract}
When can a movement in financial conditions be read as a movement in monetary policy? Two independent calibrations answer. Announcement windows give weights on the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar from their responses to policy news. The FRB/US model gives weights from their effects on GDP. They agree on the combination of prices that moves around an FOMC announcement, if the movements are assumed to persist: the model's weights price the typical response at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} of the market index's one, and at \bl{ag:bp:hl4} with a half-life of four quarters. They disagree on other combinations. For the same dollar and equity movements the model gives the dollar \bl{ag:h4:rr} times the relative effect that the event study does (interval \bl{ag:h4:rr:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:rr:hi}), and the contrast does not depend on how long the movements are assumed to last. The difference between the two readings is \bl{dz:ev:ratio} of the market move on announcements and \bl{dz:12m:ratio} in 12-month changes, where it exceeds a quarter point in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months, and in the equity boom of 1998--2001 it comes mostly from the equity weights. Whether it matters for the outlook cannot be established. In the main test composition has no detectable footprint on unemployment a year ahead, an outlook difference of \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point with an interval from \bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}, although it predicts professional forecasters' errors with the sign opposite to the model's reading, and equity valuation alone carries most of the content of either index. Both indexes predict unemployment through their 12-month changes, and the Chicago Fed's NFCI carries the longer horizons.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Consider two tightenings of financial conditions with the long yield unchanged. In one, equity valuations fall by 5 log points. In the other, the dollar appreciates by 3.9 log points. An index weighted by how asset prices respond to monetary policy news rates the two alike: each is worth about \bl{ag:ex:eq:mkt} points of the two-year yield. The FRB/US model, which weights asset prices by their effects on spending, rates the dollar movement \bl{ag:h4:fr} times as high as the equity movement (Table~\ref{tab:example}). Both weightings price the typical response of asset prices to a policy surprise almost identically, \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} against one. When can a movement in financial conditions be read as a movement in monetary policy, and when does it matter that two readings differ?

Policymakers read the stance of policy from financial conditions. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}, and in March 2024 that ``there are many different financial conditions indicators, and you can kind of, you know, see different answers to that question'' \citep{powell2024}. An index serves as a guide to the stance of policy only to the extent that a point of the index means a point of policy.

The two weightings answer different questions. The event study starts with a policy surprise and measures how asset prices move; it favors the assets that recover the surprise most precisely. The macro model starts with asset-price movements and measures how spending responds; it favors the assets with the largest effects on spending. There is no general reason for the two criteria to agree. They agree on the movements that policy news produces by construction, because the market weights are normalized to price those movements at one, and the model's weights price them at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} (interval \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi}). Whether they agree on other movements is an empirical question.

I find that they disagree on composition and that the disagreement is not small, and I cannot show that it matters for the outlook. For the same movements the model gives the dollar \bl{ag:h4:rr} times the relative effect that the event study does (interval \bl{ag:h4:rr:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:rr:hi}), and the contrast stays between \bl{ag:mp:hl8} and \bl{ag:mp:hl2} when the movements are assumed to decay with half-lives of 8 or 2 quarters. On held-out announcements the difference between the two readings is \bl{dz:ev:ratio} of the market move and never exceeds a quarter point. In 12-month changes since 1996 it is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} of the market move, exceeds a quarter point in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months, and runs for about \bl{dz:12m:run} months at a time (Figure~\ref{fig:D}). Equity weights account for most of the gap between the two indexes in 1998--2001, but not in 2004--06. The question of whether the difference changes the outlook has a flat answer: the composition term has a coefficient of \bl{cm:du:main:c} for the change in unemployment a year ahead, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{cm:du:main:lo} to \bl{cm:du:main:hi}. That is an outlook difference between the 5th and 95th percentile of composition of \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point (\bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}), which does not rule out a difference of a size that policymakers would care about and does not establish one. The test cannot separate a coefficient of zero on the composition term from a coefficient equal to the one on the index change, which is what the model's weights imply. Equity valuation alone raises the adjusted $R^2$ by \bl{cm:eq:gain}, against \bl{cm:mkt:gain} for the market index, and the sign of the composition term in the unemployment forecast errors of professional forecasters is opposite to the model's reading, so the evidence does not favor the model's composition.

The approach has three features. First, I put the two weightings on one scale. A point of the market index is the change in conditions that a policy move raising the two-year yield by one percentage point brings; I divide the FRB/US weights by the response they assign to the same move, so that both read one on a conventional surprise, and I report that response with its interval before normalizing. Second, I measure the difference between the readings on announcements and on ordinary movements, and I account for it by the weights, the persistence of the paths, and the contributions that only the FRB/US index has. Finally, I ask whether the difference matters: whether composition predicts unemployment and GDP beyond the index change, whether equity valuation alone does as well, and whether composition predicts what professional forecasters got wrong. Alongside I report what both indexes say about unemployment through their changes, how the market index relates to the NFCI, whether the results hold on 1996--2026, and how the neutral rate changes the readings.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Weights and their meaning.} Indexes of financial conditions differ mainly in the source of their weights \citep{hatzius2010}: effects on output from a model \citep{dudley2000,hatzius2018,ajello2023}, principal components \citep{brave2011}, or event-study regressions \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005,bernanke2005,nakamura2018,bauer2023}. The older literature on monetary conditions indexes recognized that asset-price weights depend on the shock and the dynamics, and that their policy meaning is not invariant \citep{eika1996,batini2002}. I quantify that difficulty with two observed sets of U.S. weights and ask where it matters.

\textbf{The closest comparisons.} \citet{arnaut2024} estimate how an existing index responds to policy surprises, \citet{sack2023} relate FCI-G to policy-induced changes in the two-year yield, and \citet{ajello2023} translate movements into a growth impulse with model multipliers. \citet{bletzinger2026} build a U.S. macro-finance index in units comparable to a policy rate and compare it with the Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G, finding divergences driven by equities and the strongest tightening in 2022--24. I ask how a policy-news weighting and a macro weighting read the same movements. \citet{boehm2025} show that announcements also move equities and exchange rates along a dimension orthogonal to yield changes, so a movement that departs from the conventional pattern can come from the announcement itself, which the event-study weights treat as noise.

\textbf{Information about activity.} \citet{hatzius2010} build an index conditional on past activity and inflation, \citet{aramonte2013} show that comparisons among indexes depend on the crisis in the sample, and \citet{lombardi2025} separate safe yields from priced risk and find that risk predicts activity more strongly. \citet{adrian2019} show that conditions shift the lower quantiles of growth, and \citet{brave2011} build an index of risk, credit and leverage. I report what the market index adds beside the NFCI, and why the two answer different horizons.

\textbf{The neutral rate.} What matters is the rate relative to the neutral rate, which fell from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}, and \citet{caballero2026} define FCI$^*$, the level of financial conditions consistent with output at potential. I measure the inputs relative to $\rs$, report that the outcomes lean toward it without proving it, and do not estimate a neutral level for the index.

The first difference is the source of the weights. The Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G take them from models of how each variable affects activity, the NFCI from the data's principal components, and my market index from how each variable responds to policy. The second is the reading. A level index says where conditions stand relative to a base period, as the Goldman Sachs index and the NFCI do. A growth index says what conditions imply for activity: FCI-G measures the contribution of financial conditions to GDP growth over the next year, and zero means that the effect of current and past changes adds nothing to growth and takes nothing from it. Zero in FCI-G is therefore neutral for growth, which is a different idea from a neutral level of conditions. FCI-G is built from changes and has no level. The third is the treatment of rates. The Goldman Sachs index uses nominal rates and the nominal dollar, so it trends over long spans \citep{hatzius2010}. FCI-G uses nominal rates as well.

Units differ too. I convert the Goldman Sachs index and the NFCI and ANFCI to the market index's units by their own responses to the same policy news: the Goldman Sachs index rises by \bl{resp:gs} points per unit of policy news, the NFCI by \bl{resp:nfci} standard deviations and the ANFCI by \bl{resp:anfci}. FCI-G stays in percentage points of GDP growth and is compared through correlations and through its own scale.

\subsection{The Market Index: Weights from Policy News}\label{sec:market}
\textbf{Policy news.} At each FOMC announcement I measure policy news with the factor of \citet{nakamura2018}. It is the first principal component of the moves in five short-term interest-rate futures in a narrow window around the announcement, scaled so that one unit moves the two-year Treasury yield by one percentage point. The data are the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}, ``monetary events'' windows: \bl{n:events} announcements from \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}.

\textbf{Responses and weights.} In each window the 10-year yield, the equity valuation and the real dollar move by a response times the policy news plus a part that has nothing to do with it. The windows reveal the response $\psi_j$ of each variable $j$. I choose the weights that recover the policy news most precisely from the three variables together: $w\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$, where $\Sigma$ is the covariance of the parts that are not policy news, normalized so that $w'\psi=1$ (one-factor generalized least squares). A policy move that raises the two-year yield by one percentage point therefore raises the index by exactly one unit, and the index is measured in points of the two-year yield.

Table~\ref{tab:weights} gives the results. A policy move that raises the two-year yield one point raises the 10-year yield by \bl{psi:ten} points, lowers equity valuation by \bl{abs:psi:eq} percent and raises the real dollar by \bl{psi:usd} percent. Each variable carries a substantial part of the policy move: the shares are \bl{share:ten} for the 10-year yield, \bl{share:eq} for equity valuation and \bl{share:usd} for the dollar. Applied to the demeaned levels of the 10-year yield, the equity valuation multiple (100 times the log of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio, CAPE) and the real broad dollar, the weights give the index.

\input{../replication/report/tab_weights}

\textbf{What the weights assume.} Consider a rise in stock prices. If it comes from news about earnings, it has little to do with policy, and the index treats it as if it did, because it enters with the same weight as a rise that policy caused. If every movement in the three variables came from policy, the index would equal the cumulated policy-induced change in the two-year yield. The weights say what the variables do when policy moves, not what causes their movements at other times. An announcement can also reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and then the responses mix policy and information \citep{nakamura2018}. I measure the responses to the news as the markets receive it, and I set the information effect aside. The index is a measure of the policy-equivalent tightening in financial conditions. It is not a forecast of activity, and Section~\ref{sec:var} gives it dynamics separately.

\subsection{The Index Weighted with FRB/US}\label{sec:frbus}
The main alternative to market weights is to weight each variable by its effect on activity in a macroeconomic model, as FCI-G does. For a comparison of levels I build such an index from FRB/US \citep{brayton2014}. Each variable's weight is its effect on GDP after four quarters of a persistent unit shift, divided by the effect of a persistent one percentage point rise in the federal funds rate (\bl{frbus:yardstick} percent of GDP), so the index is in funds-rate points. It covers the policy rate, the 10-year yield, the mortgage and BBB spreads, equity valuation and the dollar, and in one version house prices. I also give it a flow in the form of FCI-G.

Table~\ref{tab:frbus} compares the two sets of weights on the three common variables. They agree on the 10-year yield, where the FRB/US weight is \bl{ratio:ten} times the market weight. The model puts less weight on equity valuation (\bl{ratio:eq} times) and more on the dollar (\bl{ratio:usd} times). The two indexes are close on a pure policy move. The FRB/US weights price the response of the three variables to a unit of policy news at \bl{spsi:market} funds-rate points, against exactly one point for the market index. They differ on movements that policy did not cause. The model gives a fall in equity prices its estimated causal effect on output, and the market index treats it as policy-equivalent. Because the two weight vectors are not proportional, the gap between the indexes is a weighted sum of non-policy movements in equity valuation and the dollar.

\subsection{What Agreement Requires}\label{sec:requires}
Let $\psi$ be the responses of the three variables to a unit of policy news, $w_M$ the market weights (so that $w_M'\psi=1$), and $b_h$ the FRB/US weights for GDP at horizon $h$ in funds-rate points. The model prices the typical response at $b_h'\psi$, which I report in its own units first, and the normalized weights $w_{B,h}=b_h/(b_h'\psi)$ put both indexes on one scale. For a vector of financial movements $x$ the difference between the readings is
\[
D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x.
\]
By construction $D_h(\psi s)=0$ for any conventional surprise $s$. Agreement on other movements requires the difference in weights to have little exposure to the combinations of asset prices that occur. The weights can differ appreciably and give almost the same reading in practice, if the movements that occur resemble policy news, and two highly correlated indexes can disagree during a sustained equity revaluation. Table~\ref{tab:example} gives the arithmetic for two movements with the same market reading.

\input{../replication/report/tab_example}

The comparison confounds composition with persistence. A persistent dollar appreciation in FRB/US and the dollar's response to policy news are different experiments, so a difference in weights could reflect the initial composition of the movement or the assumed path that follows. Section~\ref{sec:persist} separates them. Appendix~\ref{app:ma} gives the VAR version and the conditions under which the market weights recover the spending weights up to scale.

\section{When Do the Two Readings Agree?}\label{sec:agree}
\subsection{On Announcements}\label{sec:onannounce}
The FRB/US weights price the response of the three variables to a unit of policy news at \bl{ag:h2:bpsi}, \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} and \bl{ag:h8:bpsi} funds-rate points at two, four and eight quarters, with intervals around \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi} at four quarters (Table~\ref{tab:agree}). The market index prices it at one, so agreement is not rejected at any horizon. The agreement is specific to persistent paths. On the 253 announcements, with the weights re-estimated without the event being evaluated, the difference between the two readings has a mean absolute size of \bl{dz:ev:mean} against a mean absolute market move of \bl{dz:ev:move}, a ratio of \bl{dz:ev:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:ev:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:ev:ratio:hi}), and the 90th percentile is \bl{dz:ev:p90}. On announcements the two readings are close.

\input{../replication/report/tab_agree}

\subsection{On Ordinary Movements}\label{sec:ordinary}
Outside announcements the movements of asset prices have a different mix. Table~\ref{tab:dsize} and Figure~\ref{fig:D} give the difference for monthly and 12-month changes. In monthly changes since 1996 it is \bl{dz:1m:ratio} of the market move. In 12-month changes it is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:12m:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:12m:ratio:hi}) with a mean absolute value of \bl{dz:12m:mean}; it exceeds 0.25 in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months (interval \bl{dz:12m:sh25:lo} to \bl{dz:12m:sh25:hi}) and 0.5 in \bl{dz:12m:sh50} percent, runs above 0.25 for \bl{dz:12m:run} months on average and for \bl{dz:12m:maxrun} at the longest. The difference is negatively correlated with the market move (\bl{dz:12m:corr}): when the market index tightens sharply, the FRB/US reading of the same movements tightens less. Someone tracking whether conditions tightened would reach much the same conclusion with either reading, and someone asking how much they tightened or eased would sometimes not.

\input{../replication/report/tab_dsize}

\subsection{Composition or Persistence?}\label{sec:persist}
The FRB/US dollar-to-equity ratio is \bl{ag:h2:fr}, \bl{ag:h4:fr} and \bl{ag:h8:fr} at two, four and eight quarters, against \bl{ag:mkt:ratio} for the market weights (interval \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:lo} to \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:hi}). The ratio of ratios at four quarters is \bl{ag:h4:rr} (\bl{ag:h4:rr:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:rr:hi}); at two quarters its interval includes one. The contrast does not come from the assumed persistence. Pricing the same movements with a common decaying path, with half-lives of 8, 4 and 2 quarters, gives dollar-to-equity ratios of \bl{ag:mp:hl8}, \bl{ag:mp:hl4} and \bl{ag:mp:hl2} at four quarters, against \bl{ag:h4:fr} for the persistent paths, and different half-lives for the two assets move the ratio between \bl{ag:mp:min} and \bl{ag:mp:max}, never near one. Decay changes the scale of the typical response, not the contrast: the agreement on announcements is for persistent paths. The data give no support for short half-lives (the half-lives of the levels of the three variables since 1996 are 20 to 26 quarters, and the equity response to policy news does not decay within 20 trading days). The contrast is a difference in the effects on GDP per unit of each asset.

\subsection{What Accounts for the Difference}\label{sec:accounting}
Table~\ref{tab:bridge} builds a bridge from the market index to the FRB/US index. Both are measured on the same inputs, dates and centering; this section is about the level of the difference, and Section~\ref{sec:epi} about $D$, the difference in 12-month changes of the three common assets. Replacing the market weights on the three common assets by the normalized FRB/US weights accounts for \bl{br:lvl:w:eq} percent of the variance of the difference in levels through equity weights and \bl{br:lvl:w:usd} percent through dollar weights; for 12-month changes the shares are \bl{br:chg:w:eq} and \bl{br:chg:w:usd} percent, and the mortgage spread, which only the FRB/US index has, accounts for \bl{br:chg:spread:mortgage}. The policy rate contributes almost nothing. The intervals are wide because the market weights are imprecise.

\input{../replication/report/tab_bridge}

Three episodes show how this works. In 1998--2001 the FRB/US index read \bl{br:ep1998:gap} points tighter than the market index (interval \bl{br:ep1998:lo} to \bl{br:ep1998:hi}), of which the equity weights contribute \bl{br:ep1998:eq}; the market index puts more weight on valuations that were far above average. In 2004--06 the gap is \bl{br:ep2004:gap} (\bl{br:ep2004:lo} to \bl{br:ep2004:hi}): the equity weights add \bl{br:ep2004:eq} and house prices subtract \bl{abs:br:ep2004:house}, so equities do not explain it. In 2022--24 the gap in levels is \bl{br:ep2022:gap} (\bl{br:ep2022:lo} to \bl{br:ep2022:hi}), with equities (\bl{br:ep2022:eq}) and the dollar (\bl{br:ep2022:usd}) contributing about equally; the difference in 12-month changes, $D$, is near zero in the same episode (Section~\ref{sec:epi}). The attribution to equities holds in the equity boom and not elsewhere.

\section{Does the Composition of a Tightening Matter for the Outlook?}\label{sec:outlook}
\subsection{One Test}\label{sec:onetest}
Among financial movements that the market index rates as equally tight, does composition contain information about unemployment a year ahead? I regress the 12-month-ahead change in the unemployment gap on the 12-month change in the market index (relative to $r^*$) and the 12-month change in the normalized FRB/US-minus-market difference $D$ computed on the same changes, with own lags. If the FRB/US weights were right, the coefficient on $D$ would equal the coefficient on the index change. Four-quarter GDP growth is the model-facing check, since GDP is the outcome that constructs the FRB/US weights. This is the only primary test.

\subsection{The Answer}\label{sec:answer}
The coefficient on $D$ is \bl{cm:du:main:c} with a 90 percent interval from \bl{cm:du:main:lo} to \bl{cm:du:main:hi} on 1976--2026, against \bl{cm:du:main:a} on the index change (Table~\ref{tab:comp}). Moving $D$ from its 5th to its 95th percentile at the same index change shifts the year-ahead outlook by \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point, with an interval from \bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}. On 1996--2026 the coefficient is \bl{cm:du:1996p:c} (\bl{cm:du:1996p:lo} to \bl{cm:du:1996p:hi}) and excluding 2008--15 it is \bl{cm:du:ex200815:c} (\bl{cm:du:ex200815:lo} to \bl{cm:du:ex200815:hi}). The sign changes across samples, the sign is opposite to the model's reading in the main sample, and the interval is wide: it admits a difference of \bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi} percentage point and does not separate a coefficient of zero from the coefficient on the index change (\bl{cm:du:main:a}), which is what the model's weights imply. For GDP growth the coefficient is \bl{cm:gdp:main:c} (\bl{cm:gdp:main:lo} to \bl{cm:gdp:main:hi}). In no sample does adding $D$ significantly improve out-of-sample forecasts (Clark--West \bl{cm:addD:cw} on 1976--2026). The data neither support nor reject the claim that composition matters for the outlook.

\input{../replication/report/tab_comp}

\subsection{Equities Alone}\label{sec:eqalone}
Equity valuation alone raises the adjusted $R^2$ of the change in unemployment by \bl{cm:eq:gain}, the market index by \bl{cm:mkt:gain}, the FRB/US weights by \bl{cm:frbus:gain}, the 10-year yield by \bl{cm:y10:gain}, the dollar by \bl{cm:usd:gain} and the three variables entered freely by \bl{cm:free:gain}. Out of sample, equity valuation alone forecasts better than the market index (Diebold--Mariano \bl{cm:eqmkt:dm}, an error ratio of \bl{cm:eqmkt:rmse}) and the FRB/US weights worse (\bl{cm:frmkt:dm}), without correction for the number of tests. Entered freely, the coefficients are not significantly different from proportional to the market weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wm}) or to the FRB/US weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wb}); the power is low. If equities alone deliver the content, the policy-news weighting still has a use, as a statement of what a movement is equivalent to in policy, but the evidence for summarizing three assets in order to assess the outlook is weak.

\subsection{What Forecasters Missed}\label{sec:spf}
The backward-looking purge of Section~\ref{sec:purge} cannot say whether markets anticipated developments that had yet to enter lagged unemployment or inflation. I match the financial data to the information date of each Survey of Professional Forecasters release (\bl{cm:spf:n} surveys, 1990Q3--2025Q3) and ask whether the index change and $D$ predict the unemployment forecast error four quarters ahead (Table~\ref{tab:spf}). The coefficient on $D$ is \bl{cm:spf:c} (\bl{cm:spf:lo} to \bl{cm:spf:hi}, $t=$ \bl{cm:spf:t}), \bl{cm:spfx:c} excluding 2008--15 (\bl{cm:spfx:lo} to \bl{cm:spfx:hi}). Its sign is opposite to the model's reading: if the model's weights were right and forecasters had not priced the composition, $D$ would predict errors with the sign of the index change. The result is suggestive only: the outcomes overlap, the coefficients are not adjusted for their number, and 2001 and 2008--09 probably dominate.

\section{A Common Basis}\label{sec:steps}
The comparison runs in three steps, and each step answers a problem of the one before.

\subsection{Nominal Rates}\label{sec:nominal}
The first step uses rates as published. Every index is then a function of nominal yields, and the Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G also use the nominal broad dollar. A nominal index treats a 10-year yield of 8 percent alike in 1981 and in 2000. That is reasonable only if inflation expectations and the neutral real rate are stable, and neither was. Expected inflation over the next year averaged \bl{einf:1981} percent in 1981 and is \bl{einf:latest} percent today, and the Laubach--Williams estimate of $\rs$ averaged \bl{rstar:lw:pre} percent over 1990--2007 and \bl{rstar:lw:post} percent over 2009--2016 (Figure~\ref{fig:infl}). The nominal dollar poses a further problem for the Goldman Sachs index. Its broad index rises from \bl{usd:nom:1973} in 1973 to \bl{usd:nom:latest} today, mostly from inflation differentials, and this drags the Goldman Sachs index away from the others before 1990 (Figure~\ref{fig:levels}, top panel).

\subsection{Real Rates}\label{sec:real}
The second step measures each rate less expected inflation at a matched horizon: the policy rate less one-year expected inflation, and longer rates less ten-year expected inflation. The dollar is the real broad index in every index, and in FCI-G stock and house prices are deflated by the CPI. This step removes the inflation trend. The nominal and real market indexes are almost the same over 1990--2026, with a correlation of \bl{step:nom:real:ninety}, because inflation expectations were stable then. The real step matters before 1990. Nominal FCI-G shows a tightening in mid-1974 (\bl{nominal:fcig:seventyfour}) that the real version does not (about zero), because nominal rates rose with expected inflation.

\subsection{Gaps to the Neutral Rate}\label{sec:rstarstep}
The third step measures each real rate relative to $\rs$: the policy rate, the 10-year yield, and in FCI-G the BBB and mortgage yields. Equity valuation enters through the earnings yield $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$, which is also an implicit real rate, as the gap $E/P-\rs$. I convert each index's equity weight at the sample-average earnings yield, so that every index keeps its weight on CAPE. Spreads over the 10-year yield are unchanged, because $\rs$ cancels. The baseline uses the one-sided, full-sample Laubach--Williams estimate, and Kiley's estimate is a check. Each variable is measured relative to its 1973--2026 average, so $\rs$ matters through its movements over time.

\subsection{What Each Step Changes}\label{sec:whatsteps}
Figure~\ref{fig:marketsteps} shows the market index at each step. The real step changes little after 1990, and the $\rs$ step changes a lot: the correlation of the real and $\rs$ versions over 1990--2026 is only \bl{step:real:rstar:ninety}. With $\rs$ low after 2008, the step to $\rs$ raises the 2009--2016 average of each index: by \bl{shift:mkt:gfc} points for the market index, \bl{shift:gs:gfc} for the Goldman Sachs index and \bl{shift:frbus:gfc} for the FRB/US index. Relative to the 1990--2026 average, the market index averages \bl{rstar:mkt:gfc} at the $\rs$ step against \bl{real:mkt:gfc} in the real version and \bl{nominal:mkt:gfc} in the nominal one, so conditions in 2009--2016 look tighter than average rather than easier. The four level indexes (the market index, the Goldman Sachs index and the FRB/US index with and without house prices) are closer at each step: their average range over 1990--2026 falls from \bl{nominal:range:ninety} points (nominal) to \bl{real:range:ninety} (real) and \bl{rstar:range:ninety} (relative to $\rs$).

\section{Level and Flow}\label{sec:var}
FCI-G measures a flow, and the other indexes measure a level. I give the market index both. The level is the index of Section~\ref{sec:market}. The flow comes from the relationship between the index and unemployment.

\subsection{The Response of Unemployment}\label{sec:varresp}
I estimate a monthly VAR in the unemployment gap (the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate) and the market index, from January 1973 to September 2026, with 12 lags and monthly dummies for 2020. Unemployment is ordered first, so it responds to the index only with a lag. I repeat the estimation for each step of the construction. Table~\ref{tab:var} and Figure~\ref{fig:var} give the results.

The index helps predict unemployment at every step (in the $\rs$ baseline the Granger statistic is \bl{var:rstar:p12:granger:F}, $p$ \bl{var:rstar:p12:granger:p}). A one-unit tightening raises the unemployment gap gradually. In the baseline the response reaches \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12} percentage point after 12 months, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12:lo90} to \bl{var:rstar:p12:phi12:hi90}. With an Okun coefficient of two this is about \bl{okun:phi12} percent of output. I call the 12-month response the yardstick of the index, $\Phi(12)$. With four lags the response is smaller (\bl{var:rstar:p4:phi12}) and levels off earlier. The baseline has 12 lags; Section~\ref{sec:rob} reports four.

\subsection{The Forward Impetus}\label{sec:fi}
With the response profile $\Phi(m)$ I compute what the changes in the index over the past year imply for the next 12 months, in the way FCI-G does with FRB/US multipliers:
\[
\text{FI}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{11}\frac{\Phi(m+12)-\Phi(m)}{\Phi(12)}\,\Delta M(t-m).
\]
Here $M$ is the market index. A value of 1 means that the past year's changes raise unemployment over the next year by as much as a permanent one-unit tightening does in its first year. I call this the forward impetus. Figure~\ref{fig:levelflow} shows the level and the flow in the baseline. The flow correlates \bl{rstar:fi:fcig} with the $\rs$ version of FCI-G, and the level only \bl{rstar:fcig:level}, because the two answer different questions.

\section{What Financial Prices Say about the Outlook}\label{sec:prices}
The remaining sections support the answer: they report what both indexes and the NFCI say about unemployment through their changes, how the neutral-rate basis changes the readings, and whether the results hold on 1996--2026 and with data as known at the time. The first result is not specific to the market index: the Goldman Sachs and FRB/US indexes deliver essentially the same gain, so it is evidence about financial prices and not about the weighting.
\subsection{The Funds-Rate Gap}\label{sec:wedge}
In this section and the next, the flow of an index is its 12-month change; the forward impetus of Section~\ref{sec:fi} is a weighted version of it.

The stance of policy read from the policy rate is the funds-rate gap: the effective federal funds rate less one-year expected inflation less $\rs$. The stance read from financial conditions is the market index relative to $\rs$. Each is measured relative to its 1973--2026 mean. They differ in units, two-year-yield points for the index and funds-rate points for the gap, and the comparison treats a point of each as a point of policy. The wedge is the index less the gap. A policy move that raises the two-year yield raises both. A rise in the dollar or a fall in equity valuation that policy did not cause raises only the index, and the wedge is the part of conditions that the funds rate does not carry. Levels of the two correlate \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:level:full} over 1976--2026 and their 12-month changes \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:flow:full}; they are \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:level:p0406} and \bl{corr:mkt:rstar:flow:p0406} in 2004--06, when the funds rate rose and conditions did not follow.

\subsection{Does the Wedge Matter for the Economy Ahead?}\label{sec:stancetest}
Table~\ref{tab:stance} adds the index to the funds-rate gap in projections of the change in the unemployment gap, payroll growth and core inflation 12 months ahead.

\input{../replication/report/tab_stance}

\textbf{Unemployment.} The funds-rate gap alone explains little (the $t$-statistic of its 12-month change is \bl{st:du:flow:tff}), and the index alone explains much (\bl{st:du:flow:tidx}). Given the gap, the index raises the adjusted $R^2$ by \bl{st:du:flow:gain} in the flow form and by \bl{st:du:mixed:gain} when the gap enters as a level; given the index, the gap adds \bl{st:du:flow:gainff} and \bl{st:du:mixed:gainff}. In levels the index adds \bl{st:du:level:gain} ($t=$ \bl{st:du:level:t}). The result holds for the Goldman Sachs index (gain \bl{st:du:gs:gain}) and the FRB/US index (\bl{st:du:frbus:gain}), and the $t$-statistic of the market index rises with the step, from \bl{st:du:nominal:t} in nominal terms to \bl{st:du:real:t} in real terms and \bl{st:du:flow:t} relative to $\rs$.

\textbf{Out of sample.} Recursive forecasts from 1995 have root mean squared errors of \bl{st:du:flow:rmse} (flow) and \bl{st:du:mixed:rmse} (mixed) times those of the gap alone, with Clark--West statistics of \bl{st:du:flow:cw} and \bl{st:du:mixed:cw}. The primary family has nine tests, three outcomes in three forms; five reject at the 10 percent level before adjustment, and one survives the Holm correction (the mixed form for unemployment, adjusted $p=$ \bl{st:prim:mixed:holm}). The out-of-sample gains are modest.

\textbf{Payrolls and inflation.} For payrolls the in-sample gain is \bl{st:pay:flow:gain} in the flow form and the out-of-sample statistic is \bl{st:pay:flow:cw}; on 1996--2026 the payroll flow is not distinguishable from zero (Section~\ref{sec:sample96}). For core inflation the index adds nothing (gain \bl{st:pi:flow:gain}). The claim is about unemployment.

\textbf{Which periods.} In 2009--15, when the policy rate was at its lower bound, the index adds in sample ($t=$ \bl{st:du:zlb:t}, \bl{st:du:zlb:n} months) and not significantly out of sample (Clark--West \bl{st:du:zlb:cw} in the flow form and \bl{st:du:zlb:cwm} in the mixed form). In 2022--26 the sample is about three independent years and the coefficient on the index has the opposite sign ($t=$ \bl{st:du:post:t}): the tightening of 2022--23 was not followed by higher unemployment.

\subsection{The NFCI}\label{sec:nfci}
The NFCI and ANFCI beat the common-basis indexes for unemployment at long horizons and for recessions. They are a different index: a level index of credit, risk and leverage, mostly spreads. The three variables of the market index explain \bl{nfci:decomp:level:nfci:3:vars:R2} of the NFCI's level, and \bl{nfci:decomp:level:nfci:3:vars:plus:spread:R2} when the Baa--10-year spread is added. Table~\ref{tab:nfci} enters the market flow and the NFCI level together. For unemployment at 6 and 12 months both keep their coefficients ($t=$ \bl{nf:du6:mkt:t} and \bl{nf:du12:mkt:t} for the market flow, \bl{nf:du6:nfci:t} and \bl{nf:du12:nfci:t} for the NFCI). At 24 months only the NFCI remains (\bl{nf:du24:nfci:t}, against \bl{nf:du24:mkt:t}), and for recessions the NFCI has $t=$ \bl{nf:rec1212:nfci:t} against \bl{nf:rec1212:mkt:t}. The two are complements: the market index is a policy-channel flow in policy units that carries the next six to twelve months, and the NFCI carries the longer horizons and recession risk. It is not a stress or recession monitor.

\subsection{Is the Flow Only Activity Reflected Back?}\label{sec:purge}
Asset prices respond to expected activity, and \citet{hatzius2010} purge each indicator of current and lagged growth and inflation so that the index reflects exogenous conditions; the ANFCI does the same. I regress each input on the unemployment gap and core inflation (current and 12 lags), on the full sample and recursively, and rebuild the index from the residuals with the same weights. Table~\ref{tab:purge} gives the VAR response. The 12-month response of the market index falls from \bl{pg:market:unpurged} to \bl{pg:market:full} with the full-sample purge and \bl{pg:market:recursive} with the recursive purge, and every interval stays above zero; the Goldman Sachs index is similar. Purging is not identification, and it removes too much when asset prices rightly respond to expected activity. The flow carries both a channel and an anticipation component, and for payrolls it does not survive the purge.

\subsection{The Neutral Rate and the Weights}\label{sec:rstarweights}
\textbf{The neutral rate.} The $\rs$ step makes the level of the index mean the same thing across regimes to a degree. At a zero unemployment gap the index level in 2009--19 is \bl{abs:nu:nominalma} lower than in 1990--2007 in nominal terms and \bl{abs:nu:realma} lower in real terms, and \bl{nu:rstarma} higher relative to $\rs$ (Laubach--Williams) and \bl{nu:rstar:kileyma} higher with the estimate of \citet{kiley2020} (Table~\ref{tab:neutrality}). The step reverses and shrinks the post-2008 drift but does not make the unemployment response constant across periods (Section~\ref{sec:sample96}), and the unit restriction it imposes on $\rs$ cannot be tested at this power. For outcomes, the $\rs$ versions have lower out-of-sample error than the nominal versions in \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:ratio:below1} of \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:tests} comparisons across seven index families, five outcomes and horizons; \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:dm:sig10:better} Diebold--Mariano tests favor $\rs$ and \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:n:dm:sig10:worse} the nominal version at the 10 percent level, none survives the Holm correction at 5 percent, and the mean error ratio is \bl{lad:rstar:vs:nominal:all:mean:rmse:ratio}. The tests share data, so the counts are descriptive. For unemployment the in-sample gain of the $\rs$ version over the nominal version is \bl{ld:p96:dr2} on 1996--2026 (interval \bl{ld:p96:lo} to \bl{ld:p96:hi}) and \bl{ld:pre96:dr2} before 1996. The outcomes lean toward the $\rs$ basis without proving it; it rests on the argument of \citet{dudley2010} and \citet{caballero2026} that the rate relative to neutral is what matters.

\textbf{The weights.} No alternative weights beat the market weights. Replacing them with the FRB/US weights, the first principal component, equal weights or weights fitted to activity changes the mean squared error by between \bl{wt:level:min} and \bl{wt:level:max} percent in levels and between \bl{wt:change:min} and \bl{wt:change:max} percent in 12-month changes. Of \bl{w:rstar:n:primary:tests} paired tests, \bl{w:rstar:n:raw:sig10} reject at 10 percent before adjustment and \bl{w:rstar:n:holm:sig10} after, so no alternative is significantly better and the market weights are not significantly better either. Weights fitted to activity are too imprecise to rank any set. Outcomes cannot settle the weights question.

\section{Robustness and Limits}\label{sec:rob}
The results rest on one sample, one set of weights, one estimate of $\rs$ and data revised after the fact. I vary each in turn. Table~\ref{tab:checks96} gives the main checks.

\subsection{The Sample: 1996--2026}\label{sec:sample96}
The weights come from announcements since 1996, so the sample of 1996--2026 is the one on which the index is estimated, and the sample of the companion paper on monetary surprises. I re-estimate the VAR and the regressions from 1996. The response of unemployment after 12 months is \bl{ck:a12:phi} (\bl{ck:a12:lo} to \bl{ck:a12:hi}) on 1973--2026, and \bl{ck:b12:phi} (\bl{ck:b12:lo} to \bl{ck:b12:hi}) with 12 lags and \bl{ck:b4:phi} (\bl{ck:b4:lo} to \bl{ck:b4:hi}) with four lags on 1996--2026: it does not change sign, and the interval is about 1.6 times as wide. The estimate from VARs on shorter spans is \bl{vs:197389} for 1973--89, \bl{vs:19902007} for 1990--2007, \bl{vs:19962007} for 1996--2007, \bl{vs:19962019} for 1996--2019 and \bl{vs:20092026} for 2009--26. The flow has $t=$ \bl{ck:b12:tflow} for unemployment (\bl{ck:a12:tflow} on the long sample), with a gain of \bl{ck:b12:gain} in adjusted $R^2$ against \bl{ck:a12:gain}; the index adds \bl{ck:b12:stgain} to the funds-rate gap, with a Clark--West statistic of \bl{ck:b12:cw} (one recession in the out-of-sample period). The payroll flow has $t=$ \bl{ck:b12:payt} and the payroll result does not hold. The $\rs$ step helps on 1996--2026 and not before.

\subsection{Weights That Move}\label{sec:drift}
The weights define the unit, so I hold them fixed and show how they would drift. Re-estimating on an expanding window of announcements from 2006 keeps the 10-year and dollar weights inside the 90 percent band of the full-sample estimate at every date after 2010; the equity weight is outside at \bl{exp:post2010:n:out:eq} dates in 2011--14, by at most \bl{exp:post2010:min:eq} against a band edge of \bl{base:lo:eq} (Figure~\ref{fig:weightpath}). With four lags the yardstick is \bl{rb:2:phi12}. Re-estimating the weights with the two-year yield as the policy news, with announcements through 2019, or without the lower-bound years (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:wvar}) moves the September 2026 reading from \bl{rb:6:latest:market} to \bl{rb:5:latest:market}, a spread of \bl{rb:spread:weights} points, as wide as the bootstrap interval of Section~\ref{sec:precision}. The readings follow the equity weight, which is \bl{wv:two:year:w:eq}, \bl{wv:to:2019:w:eq} and \bl{wv:no:lower:bound:w:eq} against \bl{w:eq} in the baseline. With 1990--2026 as the base period the market index reads \bl{rstar:latest90:market}, the Goldman Sachs index \bl{rstar:latest90:gs} and the FRB/US index \bl{rstar:latest90:frbus}, shifts of about a point.

\subsection{Limits of the Design}\label{sec:limits}
The $\rs$ step is the largest effect in the paper, and the outcomes lean toward it without proving it. The uncertainty of $\rs$ is not in the intervals, and $\rs$ is not neutral to outcomes, because the Laubach--Williams model uses output, inflation and unemployment. The weights come from announcements since 1996 and are applied from 1973; they assume that the responses reflect policy rather than information effects \citep{nakamura2018}, and if policy came to target financial conditions the responses could change, the Lucas-critique concern that \citet{hatzius2010} raise for all indexes. The bootstrap treats the FRB/US and Goldman Sachs weights, the Goldman Sachs conversion and the VAR as fixed. The sample has five recessions, two out of sample, and 12-month outcomes overlap, so the counts of tests are descriptive. The VAR is linear and describes the mean. The Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G are my replications, and the FRB/US index is my construction at one horizon. The readings for September 2026 use $\rs$ through 2026Q2 and interpolate October 2025.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Financial conditions move like policy when they move like policy news.} The market weights and the FRB/US weights price the typical response to a policy surprise at one and \bl{ag:h4:bpsi}, and on held-out announcements the difference between the two readings is \bl{dz:ev:ratio} of the market move. A movement that resembles policy news can be read as policy with either weighting.

\textbf{Departures matter in size, not yet in consequence.} The difference is a third of the move in 12-month changes, exceeds a quarter point in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months and runs for months at a time. It is mostly the weights on equity valuation and the dollar, it does not come from assumed persistence, and in 1998--2001 it accounts for most of the gap between the indexes. What the data cannot show is that it changes the outlook: the outlook difference is \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point (\bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}), equity valuation alone carries as much information as either index, and the composition term has the opposite sign in the forecast errors. Equity valuation alone does as well as either index on what can be tested, and the policy interpretation of the market index ends where the movement departs from policy news.

\textbf{What a policymaker can say.} Read changes, not levels, and say what a movement is equivalent to in policy under each weighting, with a range of about a third of the move. For horizons beyond a year and for recession risk, the NFCI carries what the market index does not. Measuring the rate and the earnings yield relative to the neutral rate changes the readings, and the outcomes lean toward it without proving it.

\textbf{What the results do not show.} There is no ground truth for financial conditions. Zero is the 1973--2026 average of each variable, not a neutral level of conditions, and levels of the readings, including the levels of the wedge between an index and the funds-rate gap in Appendix~\ref{app:episodes}, depend on that base period. The market index treats movements that policy did not cause as if it had; it carries both a channel and an anticipation component.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Financial conditions can be read as monetary policy when they move like policy news. The event study and the FRB/US model price the typical response to a surprise almost identically, and they read the movements of announcement windows alike. They differ on composition: for the same movements the model gives the dollar about two and a half times the relative effect that the event study does, independently of the assumed persistence, and the difference is a third of the move in 12-month changes and in the equity boom of 1998--2001 comes mostly from the equity weights. I cannot show that the difference changes the outlook: composition has no detectable footprint on unemployment a year ahead in the main test, it predicts forecasters' errors with the sign opposite to the model's reading, and equity valuation alone carries most of the content of either index. The market index and the FRB/US index predict unemployment through their changes, and the NFCI carries the longer horizons. The most durable finding is the contrast in composition and how large it is; the answer to whether it matters is open.

\section{The Market Index and the FRB/US Index in One Framework}\label{app:ma}
Let financial movements be $x=\psi s+u$, where $s$ is policy news and $u$ collects everything else with covariance $\Sigma$, and let $g_h$ be the effects of a persistent unit shift in each variable on spending at horizon $h$. The FRB/US weights are proportional to $g_h$, and the market weights are $w_M\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$ (generalized least squares, normalized so that $w_M'\psi=1$). For any $x$ the difference between the normalized readings is $D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'u$, because the policy part cancels. The two readings coincide for every movement if and only if $g_h\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$: the criterion that recovers policy news precisely and the criterion that measures spending effects select the same weights only in that case. In a one-factor economy in which the movements that policy does not cause are uncorrelated with the spending effects, the market weights recover the spending weights up to scale, and the variance of the difference is $(w_{B,h}-w_M)'\Sigma(w_{B,h}-w_M)$, which is what Table~\ref{tab:dsize} estimates.

In \bl{rstar:share:range:gt1:ninety} percent of the months since 1990 the indexes differ by more than one point. Correlations measure whether the indexes move together. \section{Construction and Estimation Details}\label{app:details}
\textbf{Data.} All series are monthly averages from January 1973 to September 2026, from the public sources in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The BBB spread is Moody's Baa yield less the 10-year yield. Months in which the CPI and unemployment releases are missing (October 2025) are interpolated. The unemployment gap is the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate, placed in the middle month of each quarter and interpolated. Expected inflation is the Cleveland Fed's estimate at one and ten years from 1982 and the Livingston Survey's 12-month CPI expectation before, shifted to the Cleveland level by the mean difference over 1982--84 and blended over 1982. CAPE is Shiller's series spliced over 1990--92 in logs to the monthly average of a daily series. The real broad dollar is spliced in 2006 in logs, as are the nominal dollar and the S\&P 500. Case--Shiller home prices before 1987 are Shiller's series.

\textbf{Market index.} Policy news is the first principal component of MP1, MP2, ED2, ED3 and ED4 in the monetary-event windows, scaled by its regression on the two-year yield change so that one unit moves the two-year yield by one percentage point. Unscheduled events with no change in MP1 and March--April 2020 are dropped. Each of the 10-year yield change, the S\&P 500 return and the dollar (DXY) return in the window is regressed on the factor. The residual covariance $\Sigma$ of the three regressions gives $w=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$. The S\&P 500 and DXY weights apply to 100 times the log of CAPE and of the real broad dollar, as percent changes in the window and in the index levels agree to first order.

\textbf{Goldman Sachs index.} The weights are the published ones: policy rate 4.4, 10-year yield 45.1, BBB spread 39.6, equity valuation $-4.9$ and dollar 6.0 percent, applied to the demeaned inputs. The conversion to the market index's units is the inverse of the response of a daily replication of the index to policy news, measured from the day before to the day after each announcement because its dollar input is a noon series (\bl{resp:gs} points per unit of news, standard error \bl{se:resp:gs}).

\textbf{FCI-G.} I use the published multipliers of the Board's release. Each of the seven variables enters as the change over three months of its three-month average (rates, in points), 100 times the three-month log change (stock and house prices) or 100 times the log change of the three-month average (the dollar), with the multipliers of the first four quarters for the one-year lookback and the first twelve for the three-year lookback. This replication tracks the published series at \bl{nominal:fcig:pub} in monthly levels from 1990. At the $\rs$ step the stock term is the change in the earnings-yield gap.

\textbf{FRB/US index.} The weights are the cumulative GDP responses at four quarters to a persistent unit shift in each variable, simulated in FRB/US with VAR-based expectations, divided by the response to a persistent one percentage point rise in the funds rate. The weight on the policy rate is its direct effect with expectations of future rates held fixed. House prices enter as 100 times the log of the price-to-rent ratio relative to its trailing 15-year average. The growth impetus applies the differences of the quarterly responses at horizons $m/3+4$ and $m/3$ to the changes of the last 12 months, the form of FCI-G.

\textbf{NFCI and ANFCI.} The weekly index at the last observation of each month; the conversion is the response of the weekly change to the sum of policy news in the week (Friday-dated weeks; heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-robust standard errors with four lags).

\textbf{Gaps to $\rs$.} For the policy rate, the 10-year yield and the Baa and mortgage yields, the input is the real rate less $\rs$. Equity valuation becomes $-(100/\overline{E/P})(E/P-\rs)$, where $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$ and $\overline{E/P}$ is its 1973--2026 mean, so that a one-point rise of CAPE (in logs) has the same weight as before when $E/P$ is at its mean. $\rs$ is the one-sided, full-sample estimate (New York Fed's Laubach--Williams estimate, data through 2026Q2) placed in the middle month of each quarter.

\textbf{VAR and bootstrap.} The VAR has a constant, the 12 monthly dummies for 2020 and $p$ lags of the unemployment gap and the index. Because unemployment is first, the response of unemployment to an innovation that raises the index by one unit on impact is the moving-average coefficient $\Phi(h)$ and does not depend on the residual covariance. The bootstrap resamples blocks of 12 consecutive residual pairs, simulates the VAR recursively from the actual initial values with the dummies fixed, and re-estimates; bands are percentiles of the \bl{boot:reps} draws. The same draws give the intervals of the flow's correlation with FCI-G. The weights bootstrap resamples announcements with replacement and recomputes the policy-news factor and the weights each time. Draws use the seed in \texttt{lib/config.py}.

\subsection{How Precisely Is the Market Index Estimated?}\label{sec:precision}
The market index's weights come from regressions on \bl{n:events} announcements, and they are estimated with error. I resample the announcements with replacement \bl{boot:reps} times, recompute the policy-news factor and the weights each time, and recompute the index and its comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:boot}).

\input{../replication/report/tab_boot}

The weights are imprecise. The weight on the 10-year yield has a 90 percent interval from \bl{wb:ten:lo} to \bl{wb:ten:hi} around \bl{w:ten}. The interval for equity valuation runs from \bl{wb:eq:lo} to \bl{wb:eq:hi}, and for the dollar from \bl{wb:usd:lo} to \bl{wb:usd:hi}. The FRB/US weights on all three lie inside these intervals, the equity weight near the edge: it is lower in absolute value than \bl{frw:eq:pct} percent of the bootstrap draws, about 14 percent in a two-sided test. The data do not reject that the model weights equal the market weights. This is a statement about power, not a finding that they are equal, and it treats the FRB/US weights and the VAR as fixed.

The index is better determined than its weights, and its correlations with the others are stable. The correlation with the Goldman Sachs index is \bl{rstar:mkt:gs}, with a 5th to 95th percentile range from \bl{bt:rstar:corr:gs:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:corr:gs:hi}, and with the FRB/US index \bl{rstar:mkt:frbus}, from \bl{bt:rstar:corr:frbus:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:corr:frbus:hi}. The level of the reading is not stable. The September 2026 reading relative to $\rs$ is \bl{rstar:latest:market}, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{bt:rstar:latest:lo} to \bl{bt:rstar:latest:hi}. The Goldman Sachs reading (\bl{rstar:latest:gs}) lies inside that interval. The FRB/US reading (\bl{rstar:latest:frbus}) lies just above it, and the 90 percent interval for the market index minus the FRB/US index runs from \bl{gap:frbus:lo} to \bl{gap:frbus:hi}. The sampling error of the weights is therefore of the same size as the differences between the readings, and the Goldman Sachs and FRB/US readings lie on opposite sides of the 1973--2026 average.
