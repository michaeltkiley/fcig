\begin{abstract}
Financial conditions indexes weight asset prices by their effects in a macroeconomic model or by their common statistical variation. I take the weights from monetary policy itself. Around FOMC announcements, policy news moves the 10-year Treasury yield, stock prices and the dollar in one direction. That policy direction, scaled to a policy move, defines a monetary conditions financial conditions index (MCFCI). Measured relative to the neutral real rate, its level says whether conditions are tight or loose. The policy direction is nearly the direction of the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model and of the Goldman Sachs index. Over five decades it moves with them, and its relation to subsequent output matches theirs and, at one year, FRB/US's own answer. Statistical indexes built around credit spreads behave differently. The two directions coincide because policy and spending both act heavily through equity prices. Most models used for monetary policy analysis have no equity channel.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{JEL Classification:} E44, E52, G12, E32

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} financial conditions, monetary policy transmission, high-frequency identification, equity prices, neutral interest rate

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Policymakers describe the stance of monetary policy through financial conditions. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}. A statement of that kind needs a unit. How much of a policy move is a rise in bond yields, a fall in stock prices or a stronger dollar worth, and how should the three be combined?

Existing financial conditions indexes answer in one of two ways. Some weight each asset price by its effect on spending in a macroeconomic model: the Federal Reserve Board's FCI-G uses multipliers from the FRB/US model and others \citep{ajello2023}, and the Goldman Sachs index calibrates its weights to the effect on GDP growth \citep{hatzius2010}. Others weight indicators by their common variation, as the principal-component indexes of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago do \citep{brave2011}. Monetary policy offers a third answer. When the Federal Open Market Committee surprises markets, the 10-year yield, stock prices and the dollar move together in one direction. That direction says what policy itself moves and by how much.

I build a financial conditions index along that policy direction and compare it with the indexes built from macro models. The answer is that the directions coincide. The policy direction, identified from asset prices in the minutes around \bl{n:events} FOMC announcements, is nearly the direction of the FRB/US model and of the published Goldman Sachs index (Figure~\ref{fig:weights}). Over 1973--2026 the policy-news index, which I call the monetary conditions financial conditions index (MCFCI), moves with them, and a one-point move in each is followed by a similar fall in output. The reason is economic. Of the three asset prices, stock prices carry the largest part of what policy news moves, and in FRB/US they carry more than half of the effect of a policy move on financial conditions. Equities carry \bl{nv:share:eq} of a one-point reading of the MCFCI in the announcement windows, and between \bl{nv:eq:frbus:q4} and \bl{nv:eq:market:q4} of each index's reading of a funds-rate increase in FRB/US.

Four conclusions emerge.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{The policy direction is the macro-model direction.} In units of each asset's standard deviation, the policy direction has a cosine of \bl{nv:cos:market:gs} with the direction of the published Goldman Sachs weights and \bl{nv:cos:market:frbus} with FRB/US's. FRB/US reads a persistent 1 percentage point rise in the funds rate as \bl{nv:hike:market:q1} points of the MCFCI.
\item \textbf{Equities are the reason.} Policy news moves stock prices strongly, and stock prices move spending in the models that include them. Equities account for \bl{nv:eq:market:level} of the variance of the MCFCI and \bl{nv:eq:gs:level} of the Goldman Sachs index.
\item \textbf{The level of the MCFCI, measured relative to the neutral rate, reads the stance.} Its history matches the macro indexes, and its level separates tight from tightening: in 2022--24 the Board's FCI-G recorded a drag on growth while the MCFCI level read conditions as loose in \bl{nv:tt:202224:loose} percent of months.
\item \textbf{A point of the MCFCI goes with a policy-sized change in output.} In a reduced-form VAR the output gap falls between \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4} and \bl{abs:nv:var:market:fci:h4} percent a year after a one-point move, close to the \bl{abs:nv:frbus:market:gap:h4} percent FRB/US gives for the same path of financial conditions. Statistical stress indexes move output by a fraction of that per unit.
\end{enumerate}

The approach has three features. First, I identify the policy direction from the co-movement of asset prices with policy news in 30-minute windows around FOMC announcements, scale it to a move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point, and measure yields and valuations relative to the neutral real rate $\rs$. Second, I compare the policy direction with the macro-model directions of FRB/US and the published Goldman Sachs weights on the same assets and scale, and I derive the condition under which a market-based and a spending-based index must coincide: the two directions must be the same. Finally, I read the index over five decades against the macro and statistical indexes, and I relate it to subsequent output in a VAR benchmarked against FRB/US.

The agreement matters beyond measurement. The models most used for monetary policy analysis, from \citet{smets2007} to the Board's EDO and SIGMA models and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's DSGE model, contain no stock market. FRB/US does, and it is the model whose weights the market agrees with. At the Board the staff's alternative scenarios relied on FRB/US alone in every Greenbook from 2006 to 2010 and on about \bl{nv:tb:models} models per Tealbook in 2016--2020, most of them without equities. The evidence here suggests that quantitative models for policy need the equity channel that policy news and FRB/US both emphasize.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Financial conditions indexes.} Weights from macro models have a long history, from Monetary Conditions Indexes that combined a short rate and the exchange rate \citep{eika1996,batini2002} to indexes in interest-rate-equivalent units \citep{hatzius2010,ajello2023,bletzinger2026}. Statistical indexes extract common factors from many indicators \citep{brave2011}; their tail behavior predicts downside risk to growth \citep{adrian2019}. Event studies measure how existing indexes respond to policy surprises \citep{sack2023,arnaut2024}. I reverse the direction: the responses to policy news define the weights. The name differs from the Bank of Canada's Monetary Conditions Index and from earlier uses of ``MFCI'' for a model-based index \citep{beaton2009}.

\textbf{Equities and policy.} Policy surprises move stock prices strongly, mostly through risk premiums \citep{bernanke2005,rigobon2004,gurkaynak2005,bauer2023}. The window response here matches the closest estimates. Stock wealth moves spending \citep{chodorowreich2021}, and in large policy models a sizable share of the effect of policy runs through wealth \citep{ludvigson2002}. Standard New Keynesian models treat stock prices as redundant \citep{castelnuovo2010}, and a recent review of forecasting at the Bank of England found that its main model lacked a detailed financial sector \citep{bernanke2024}. Research models add pieces of the channel: risk premiums that respond to policy \citep{kekre2022,pflueger2022} and stock wealth in heterogeneous-agent models \citep{auclert2020}.

\textbf{The neutral level.} Measuring yields and valuations relative to $\rs$ connects the index to FCI$^*$, the neutral level of financial conditions \citep{caballero2026}.

\section{A Financial Conditions Index from Monetary Policy News}\label{sec:method}
The index is built in three steps: measure what policy news moves, turn the responses into weights, and choose the level against which each asset price is measured. Each step answers an economic question.

\subsection{What Policy News Moves}\label{sec:windows}
An FOMC statement is released at a known time. In the 30 minutes around it, news about policy dominates other news, so the co-movement of asset prices in the window reveals what policy moves. I use the U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}: \bl{n:events} announcements from \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}. Policy news $s$ is the first principal component of the changes in five interest-rate futures \citep{nakamura2018}, scaled so that one unit raises the two-year Treasury yield by 1 percentage point.

In each window, policy news of size $s$ moves the three asset prices by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:window}
x=\psi s+u ,
\end{equation}
where $x$ collects the changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar, $\psi$ is their usual response to one unit of news and $u$ is everything else, with covariance $S$.

Policy news that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the 10-year yield \bl{psi:ten} percentage point, lowers stock prices \bl{abs:psi:eq} percent and raises the dollar \bl{psi:usd} percent (Figure~\ref{fig:window}). Policy news explains \bl{nv:r2:two} of the window variance of the two-year yield and about a third of each of the other three: \bl{nv:r2:ten}, \bl{nv:r2:eq} and \bl{nv:r2:usd}. The stock response is in line with the literature. \citet{bauer2023} find a response of about 7.4 percent per point of the two-year yield in the same windows over 1988--2019, with a similar share of variance explained.

\subsection{The Policy Direction and the Weights}\label{sec:weights}
An index should read the usual response to policy news as one point and be disturbed as little as possible by other news. Among linear combinations $w'x$ with $w'\psi=1$, the one that recovers $s$ with the smallest error is the generalized least squares estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
w=\frac{S^{-1}\psi}{\psi'S^{-1}\psi} .
\end{equation}
I call $S^{-1}\psi$ the policy direction: the direction in the space of the three asset prices along which policy news moves them, measured against their other movements. An asset points along it to the extent that it responds strongly to policy news relative to its other movements. The weights are the policy direction scaled so that the usual response reads one point. The equity weight is \bl{w:eq} per percent, the 10-year weight \bl{w:ten} per percentage point and the dollar weight \bl{w:usd} per percent (Table~\ref{tab:nvweights}). The units make the coefficients hard to compare. The share of a policy move each asset carries, $w_a\psi_a$, is a better guide: equities carry \bl{nv:share:eq} of a one-point reading, with a 90 percent interval from \bl{nv:share:eq:lo} to \bl{nv:share:eq:hi}, the 10-year yield \bl{nv:share:ten} and the dollar \bl{nv:share:usd}.

\input{../replication/report/tab_nv_weights}

A worked case shows the reading. On 28 January 2004 the Committee dropped its statement that policy accommodation could be maintained for a ``considerable period.'' In the window the two-year yield rose \bl{jan2004:d2y} basis points, the 10-year yield \bl{jan2004:d10y} basis points and the dollar \bl{jan2004:dusd} percent, and stock prices fell \bl{abs:jan2004:deq} percent. The MCFCI reads these moves as a tightening of \bl{jan2004:F} point: \bl{jan2004:F:c:ten} from the 10-year yield, \bl{jan2004:F:c:eq} from equities and \bl{jan2004:F:c:usd} from the dollar.

\subsection{Measured Relative to What? Nominal, Real and Relative to $\rs$}\label{sec:steps}
The weights convert movements into points of policy. To read the level of conditions, each asset price needs a reference. Three steps take it there, each answering a problem with the step before.

\textbf{Nominal.} The first index uses rates as published, equity valuation as the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE) and the real broad dollar. A nominal index treats a 10-year yield of 8 percent alike in 1981 and in 2000. That is reasonable only if expected inflation is stable, and it was not: expected inflation over the next year averaged \bl{einf:1981} percent in 1981 and is \bl{einf:latest} percent today. The 10-year yield averaged \bl{ex1:yr:1980:y10} percent in 1980 and \bl{ex1:yr:2023:y10} percent in 2023, and the nominal index reads 1980 as \bl{abs:ex1:lv:nominal:diff} points tighter than 2023.

\textbf{Real.} The second step subtracts expected inflation at a matched horizon, ten years for the 10-year yield:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real}
x^{\text{real}}_{10,t}=i_{10,t}-\pi^e_{10,t} .
\end{equation}
Less expected inflation, the two yields were nearly equal, at \bl{ex1:lv:real:1980:y10} and \bl{ex1:lv:real:2023:y10} percent, and the real index reads 1980 as \bl{abs:ex1:lv:real:diff} points tighter. The trend in nominal rates is gone, but the neutral real rate also moved: the Laubach--Williams estimate of $\rs$ averaged \bl{rstar:lw:pre} percent over 1990--2007 and \bl{rstar:lw:post} percent over 2009--2016 \citep{laubach2003,holston2023}. A real yield of 2 percent restrains spending more when the neutral rate is 1 percent than when it is 3.

\textbf{Relative to $\rs$.} The third step measures the real 10-year yield and the earnings yield of equities against $\rs$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rstar}
x^{*}_{10,t}=i_{10,t}-\pi^e_{10,t}-\rs_t ,\qquad x^{*}_{eq,t}=-\kappa\left(\frac{E_t}{P_t}-\rs_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $E/P$ is the inverse of CAPE and $\kappa$ converts the earnings-yield gap to CAPE log points at the sample-average earnings yield, so that the equity weight applies unchanged. The dollar is a real exchange rate and is not adjusted. Relative to $\rs$, the 1980 yield was \bl{abs:ex1:lv:rstar:1980:y10} points below neutral and the 2023 yield \bl{ex1:lv:rstar:2023:y10} point above it: a yield nearly three times as high was the easier of the two. The index as a whole reads 1980 as \bl{abs:ex1:lv:rstar:diff} points tighter than 2023, because stock prices were low relative to earnings in 1980, with a CAPE of about \bl{ex1:yr:1980:cape} against \bl{ex1:yr:2023:cape}. The step matters most when the neutral rate moves fast. Over 2008--09 the MCFCI tightened by \bl{nv:ep0809:market} points a year relative to $\rs$; with $\rs$ held at its mean the tightening is \bl{nv:ep0809:market:fixed} point. The fall in the neutral rate added \bl{nv:rsadd:market} points.

Each step removes slow-moving variation. A quadratic time trend accounts for \bl{nv:trend:nominal} percent of the variance of the nominal index over \bl{nv:trend:first}--\bl{nv:trend:last}, \bl{nv:trend:real} percent of the real index and \bl{nv:trend:rstar} percent of the index relative to $\rs$. Most of the slow movement in the nominal index is the trend in inflation and the neutral rate, and the two steps take it out.

The last step ties the index to FCI$^*$, the level of financial conditions consistent with output at potential \citep{caballero2026}. Write the neutral level of the asset prices as $x^*_t=\iota\,\rs_t+c_t$, where $\iota$ is each asset's loading on $\rs$ and $c_t$ collects the neutral premiums. The gap of a reading to its neutral level is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
w'(x_t-x^*_t)=w'(x_t-\iota\,\rs_t)-w'c_t .
\end{equation}
The first term is the index relative to $\rs$. When the neutral premiums are constant, it is the gap to neutral up to a constant. Against the published FCI$^*$, \bl{fs:first:q} to \bl{fs:last:q}, the correlation of the level readings with the FCI-G gap to FCI$^*$ rises from between \bl{fs:gap:rstar:fixed:min} and \bl{fs:gap:rstar:fixed:max} with $\rs$ held fixed to between \bl{fs:gap:rstar:min} and \bl{fs:gap:rstar:max} relative to $\rs$. All results below use the index relative to $\rs$; Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the series.

\section{The MCFCI and Macro-Model Indexes}\label{sec:macro}
\subsection{The Macro-Model Direction}\label{sec:spending}
In a macro-model index an asset earns weight by moving spending. Let $g_h$ be the effect on GDP after $h$ quarters of a persistent unit shift in each asset price. I call $g_h$ the macro-model direction. FRB/US weights each asset by its effect after four quarters relative to the effect of a persistent 1 percentage point rise in the federal funds rate \citep{brayton2014}, so that a point is a funds-rate equivalent. The Goldman Sachs weights are calibrated to the effect on GDP growth; I use the published U.S.\ weights reported by \citet{caballero2026b} on the same three assets. To compare, I put each set of weights on the MCFCI's scale, so that each reads the usual announcement response as one point.

\subsection{When the Two Directions Coincide}\label{sec:agree}
A market-based and a spending-based index answer different questions. The first reads a movement as the policy news that would ordinarily produce it. The second reads it as the change in the policy rate with the same effect on spending. They give the same reading of every movement only if the macro-model direction is the policy direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:agree}
g_h\propto S^{-1}\psi ,
\end{equation}
that is, only if the assets that move spending most, per unit of their ordinary volatility, are the ones along which policy news moves markets (Appendix~\ref{app:derivation}). Nothing forces this. A model could give the dollar a large weight for its effect on net exports while policy news moves the dollar little.

In the data the two directions nearly coincide. On the common scale the Goldman Sachs weights are \bl{nv:w:gs:ten}, \bl{nv:w:gs:eq} and \bl{nv:w:gs:usd} on the 10-year yield, equities and the dollar, against \bl{nv:w:market:ten}, \bl{nv:w:market:eq} and \bl{nv:w:market:usd} for the MCFCI, and each lies inside the MCFCI's 90 percent interval. FRB/US's are \bl{nv:w:frbus:ten}, \bl{nv:w:frbus:eq} and \bl{nv:w:frbus:usd}. In units of each asset's standard deviation, the policy direction has a cosine of \bl{nv:cos:market:gs} with the Goldman Sachs direction and \bl{nv:cos:market:frbus} with the FRB/US direction (Figure~\ref{fig:weights}a). The units agree too. In FRB/US, a persistent 1 percentage point rise in the funds rate moves the three asset prices by amounts the MCFCI reads as \bl{nv:hike:market:q1} points on impact and \bl{nv:hike:market:q4} after four quarters.

\subsection{Why the Directions Coincide: Equities}\label{sec:equities}
The directions coincide because both put equities at the center (Table~\ref{tab:nvequity}). On the market side, policy news moves stock prices strongly relative to their other movements, so equities carry the largest share of a policy move, \bl{nv:share:eq}. On the spending side, FRB/US contains household stock wealth in consumption and the return on equity in the cost of capital. In the model a funds-rate increase works almost entirely through asset prices, and equities carry between \bl{nv:eq:frbus:q4} and \bl{nv:eq:frbus:q1} of FRB/US's own reading of the increase in the first two years. Empirical evidence supports a large channel: the marginal propensity to consume out of stock wealth is about 3 cents per dollar per year \citep{chodorowreich2021}, and in large policy models of the 1990s between a quarter and two-thirds of the effect of policy on GDP after two years ran through wealth \citep{ludvigson2002}.

\input{../replication/report/tab_nv_equity}

Equities also dominate the history of the indexes. They account for \bl{nv:eq:market:level} of the variance of the MCFCI, \bl{nv:eq:gs:level} of the Goldman Sachs index and \bl{nv:eq:frbus:level} of the FRB/US index. FRB/US gives the dollar more weight than the market does, and the difference matters when the dollar and stock prices move in opposite directions (Section~\ref{sec:part}).

\section{Financial Conditions over Five Decades}\label{sec:history}
\subsection{The Indexes Together}\label{sec:together}
Figure~\ref{fig:history} shows the three level indexes relative to $\rs$ from 1973 to 2026. The MCFCI and the Goldman Sachs index have a correlation of \bl{corr:level:1973-2026:market:gs} in levels and the MCFCI and the FRB/US index \bl{corr:level:1973-2026:market:frbus} (90 percent interval \bl{corr:level:1973-2026:market:frbus:lo} to \bl{corr:level:1973-2026:market:frbus:hi}, from a block bootstrap of months). In 12-month changes the correlations are \bl{corr:change12:1973-2026:market:gs} and \bl{corr:change12:1973-2026:market:frbus}. Conditions were tightest in the early 1980s, when real yields stood well above $\rs$ and stock prices were low relative to earnings, loosest around 2000, and they tightened sharply in 2008--09.

\subsection{Tight or Tightening}\label{sec:tight}
A level and a change answer different questions. A level relative to neutral says whether conditions restrain or support spending. A change measure, such as the Board's FCI-G, says how much the movements of the past year will subtract from growth in the next. FCI-G is close to the change in a level index: its correlation with the 12-month change of the MCFCI is \bl{corr:change12:1973-2026:market:fcig}, and \bl{corr:change12:1973-2026:market:fcig:rstar} with FCI-G built relative to $\rs$. The two readings part when conditions tighten from a loose level (Figure~\ref{fig:tight}). In 2022--24, FCI-G recorded a drag on growth while the MCFCI level read conditions as loose in \bl{nv:tt:202224:loose} percent of months; the level averaged \bl{nv:tt:202224:level} point. In 1994 the same was true in \bl{nv:tt:1994:loose} percent of months. In 1980--82 both read conditions as tight, with the level at \bl{nv:tt:198082:level} points, and in 1998--2001 both read them as loose.

\subsection{Where the Indexes Part}\label{sec:part}
The indexes differ most when the dollar and stock prices move in opposite directions, because FRB/US weights the dollar more than the market does. January 1998 is the clearest case. Over the preceding year the real dollar rose \bl{nv:jan98:x:usd} percent, equity valuations rose \bl{nv:jan98:x:eq} percent and the 10-year yield fell \bl{abs:nv:jan98:x:y10} point. On the three common assets, the MCFCI reads the year as an easing of \bl{abs:nv:jan98:market:total} point and the Goldman Sachs weights as an easing of \bl{abs:nv:jan98:gs:total} point; FRB/US reads it as a tightening of \bl{nv:jan98:frbus:total} point, because its dollar term, \bl{nv:jan98:frbus:usd}, outweighs the equity term. Such cases are rare. Over \bl{nv:cnt:n} months of 12-month changes since 1996, the three readings have the same sign in \bl{nv:cnt:same} percent and materially opposite signs, at least a quarter point each way, in \bl{nv:cnt:opp25} months.

\subsection{Statistical Indexes}\label{sec:stat}
The Chicago Fed's NFCI and ANFCI extract the common factor of more than a hundred indicators of risk, credit and leverage \citep{brave2011}. They behave differently from the MCFCI. In levels the MCFCI has a correlation of \bl{corr:level:1973-2026:market:nfci} with the NFCI and in 12-month changes \bl{corr:change12:1973-2026:market:nfci}. The three asset prices of the MCFCI explain \bl{nfcireg:change12:nfci:3:vars:R2} of the variance of 12-month changes in the NFCI; adding the Baa spread raises the share to \bl{nfcireg:change12:nfci:3:vars:plus:spread:R2}.

The NFCI is also asymmetric. Since 1990 its skewness is \bl{nv:skew:19902026:nfci}, against \bl{nv:skew:19902026:market} for the MCFCI: it spikes in periods of stress and is compressed when conditions are easy. It measures stress, not whether conditions support or restrain spending, and it complements an index in units of policy: stress indicators carry information about downside risk to growth \citep{adrian2019}.

The difference comes from the indicators, not the statistical method. The first principal component of the MCFCI's three asset prices has a correlation of \bl{nv:pc:level:A:mkt} with the MCFCI. Adding the BBB and mortgage spreads moves the component toward the spreads, lowers its correlation with the MCFCI to \bl{nv:pc:level:B:mkt} and makes it skewed (\bl{nv:pc:B:skew}).

\section{Financial Conditions and Activity}\label{sec:activity}
\subsection{A Reduced-Form VAR}\label{sec:var}
Does a point of each index go with a policy-sized change in output? I estimate a two-variable quarterly VAR in the CBO output gap and one index, with four lags, from 1973 to 2026 and dummies for 2020Q1--2021Q1, and trace the response to an innovation that raises the index by one point. Two orderings bound the answer. With the output gap first, the innovation does not move output within the quarter, so GDP responds with a lag. With the index first, the response is the generalized impulse response, which credits the index with any news that moves both in the same quarter. Both orderings mix the structural shocks that move financial conditions, policy among them, so the responses are reduced-form: they show how output moves after a typical movement in each index, not the effect of a financial shock.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:varresults}
The three policy-unit indexes give nearly the same responses (Table~\ref{tab:nvvar}). A year after a one-point move in the MCFCI, the output gap is \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4} percent lower with GDP lagged (90 percent interval \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4:hi} to \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4:lo}) and \bl{abs:nv:var:market:fci:h4} percent lower in the generalized response. The responses for the Goldman Sachs and FRB/US indexes are \bl{abs:nv:var:gs:gap:h4} and \bl{abs:nv:var:frbus:gap:h4} percent with GDP lagged. The effect fades in the second year, to about \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h8} percent after two years. FCI-G, in points of GDP growth, gives \bl{abs:nv:var:fcig:gap:h4} percent at a year. A one-point move in the NFCI, converted to policy units by its response to policy news, goes with a fall of only \bl{abs:nv:var:nfci:gap:h4} percent.

\input{../replication/report/tab_nv_var}

\subsection{Against FRB/US}\label{sec:benchmark}
FRB/US gives a structural benchmark. For each index I find the funds-rate path that, in FRB/US, makes the index follow its own VAR path, and read GDP off the model. At one year the VAR and the model agree: for the MCFCI the VAR gives \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4} and FRB/US \bl{abs:nv:frbus:market:gap:h4} percent, and for the Goldman Sachs index \bl{abs:nv:var:gs:gap:h4} and \bl{abs:nv:frbus:gs:gap:h4} percent (Figure~\ref{fig:var}). After that they part: FRB/US's effect keeps building, to \bl{abs:nv:frbus:market:gap:h12} percent after three years for the MCFCI, while the VAR's fades to \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h12}. Most empirical estimates of the effects of policy shocks peak later than this VAR, at 18 to 24 months \citep{ramey2016}.

The similarity of the VAR responses follows from the similarity of the directions. If output depends on the asset prices through a macro direction $g$, the reduced-form response to index $k$ with weights $w_k$ is proportional to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
\beta_k=\frac{g'\Omega w_k}{w_k'\Omega w_k},
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ is the covariance of the asset prices. Indexes that point in nearly the same direction in the metric of $\Omega$ give nearly the same responses, whatever $g$ is. The correlations of the three indexes' readings, $w_j'\Omega w_k/\sqrt{(w_j'\Omega w_j)(w_k'\Omega w_k)}$, are \bl{nv:corromega:market:gs} for the MCFCI and the Goldman Sachs index and \bl{nv:corromega:market:frbus} for the MCFCI and FRB/US.

\subsection{Which Asset Prices Carry the Response}\label{sec:components}
In the VAR the MCFCI is one variable, so its three asset prices act on output in proportion to their weights. I test that restriction by replacing the index with its three contributions, each weight times its asset price in index points, so that each can move output on its own. The shock is the policy move of the windows: each contribution moves by its response to policy news, in index points (\bl{nv:cq:share:rates} for the 10-year yield, \bl{nv:cq:share:equities} for equities and \bl{nv:cq:share:dollar} for the dollar), so the three add to the one-point move of the index. Output is ordered first, as before.

Freeing the components keeps most of the one-year response. The output gap is \bl{abs:nv:cq:rstar:free:h4} percent lower after a year, against \bl{abs:nv:cq:rstar:index:h4} percent with the index as one variable, and the interval excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:nvcomp}). The response fades sooner because the shock itself fades sooner: eight quarters on, the index stands at \bl{nv:cq:rstar:free:fci:h8} points with the components free and \bl{nv:cq:rstar:index:fci:h8} with the index as one variable. The output-gap equation does not reject equal coefficients on the three contributions at every lag (p-value \bl{nv:cq:rstar:p}): the data are consistent with the weights of the index.

Equities carry the response. Of the one-year fall of \bl{abs:nv:cq:rstar:free:h4} percent, equities account for \bl{abs:nv:cq:rstar:equities:h4} and the 10-year yield for \bl{abs:nv:cq:rstar:rates:h4}. The dollar works the other way: its part of the shock raises the output gap by \bl{nv:cq:rstar:dollar:h4} percent after a year and \bl{nv:cq:rstar:dollar:h8} after two. A stronger dollar should lower net exports, so this is the opposite of the channel FRB/US builds in. The dollar moves with the yield and with equities, and a conditional response of this kind does not identify the effect of the dollar alone; it shows that the activity response does not run through the dollar. The same pattern holds with the index measured in real terms (equities \bl{abs:nv:cq:real:equities:h4}, dollar \bl{nv:cq:real:dollar:h4}) and in nominal terms (\bl{abs:nv:cq:nominal:equities:h4} and \bl{nv:cq:nominal:dollar:h4}).

\subsection{Levels and Changes}\label{sec:levelchange}
A level index in a VAR with lags carries the information of a change measure. In the output-gap equation, four lags of the MCFCI level fit as well as four lags of its four-quarter change or of FCI-G ($R^2$ of \bl{nv:lf:market:level:gap:rtwo}, \bl{nv:lf:market:change4:gap:rtwo} and \bl{nv:lf:market:fcig:gap:rtwo}), and the level coefficients sum to nearly zero (p-value \bl{nv:lf:market:gap:p}), so the lags act as changes. That is why the level indexes and FCI-G give similar responses. For forecasting activity, the level and the change say the same thing; the level adds the reading of where conditions stand relative to neutral (Section~\ref{sec:tight}).

\section{Equities and Macro Models}\label{sec:models}
The policy direction and the FRB/US direction coincide because both put heavy weight on equity prices. Equities also carry the response of output in the VAR (Section~\ref{sec:components}). Most models used for monetary policy analysis have no equity channel (Table~\ref{tab:nvmodels}). In the medium-scale New Keynesian model of \citet{christiano2005} and \citet{smets2007}, the only asset price is the shadow value of installed capital, and household stock wealth does not enter consumption. The Board's EDO and SIGMA models and the New York Fed's DSGE model follow that structure, the last adding entrepreneurial net worth and a credit spread. As \citet{castelnuovo2010} put it, the standard New Keynesian model ``typically considers stock prices as redundant for the computation of the equilibrium values of inflation, output, and the policy rate.'' FRB/US is the exception: equity prices are the present value of earnings, and stock wealth enters consumption and the cost of capital.

\input{../replication/report/tab_nv_models}

The Board staff's use of models has moved toward the models without equities. In all \bl{nv:gb:n} Greenbooks from January 2006 to April 2010, the alternative scenarios were simulations of FRB/US. In the \bl{nv:tb:n} Tealbooks of 2016--2020, the scenarios used \bl{nv:tb:models} models on average, FRB/US alone in none of them, SIGMA in \bl{nv:tb:sigma} percent and another DSGE model in \bl{nv:tb:dsge} percent (Appendix~\ref{app:tealbook}). The concern is not confined to the Federal Reserve: a review of forecasting at the Bank of England found that its main model ``does not include detailed representations of the financial sector'' and could not capture key channels of transmission \citep{bernanke2024}.

Research models capture parts of the channel. Models in which policy moves risk premiums reproduce the stock response to policy news \citep{pflueger2022,kekre2022}, but their effects on output are short-lived. Heterogeneous-agent models with stock wealth produce persistent effects on output, but stock responses several times smaller than in the data \citep{auclert2020}. Estimated models with a stock-wealth channel find it significant for activity \citep{castelnuovo2010}. No model yet combines a stock response of the size policy news produces, an effect of stock prices on spending of the size the evidence supports, and effects on output that last. The MCFCI gives a target for such a model: in units of policy, equities carry about \bl{nv:share:eq} of what policy moves.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:robust}
\textbf{Information effects.} An announcement can reveal the Committee's view of the economy as well as its policy \citep{nakamura2018}. With the policy surprise of \citet{bauer2023aer}, orthogonalized to public news, the equity weight is \bl{inf:BS:orth:w:eq} (announcements from \bl{inf:BS:orth:first} to \bl{inf:BS:orth:last}), larger in magnitude than the baseline \bl{w:eq}. The equity role does not come from information effects.

\textbf{Event samples.} Through 2019 the equity weight is \bl{wv:to:2019:w:eq}; without the years at the lower bound it is \bl{wv:no:lower:bound:w:eq}. Equities remain the largest share of a policy move.

\textbf{The VAR.} Without the 2020 dummies the one-year response to the MCFCI is \bl{abs:nv:var:market:gap:h4:nd} percent with GDP lagged. With the index measured in real terms it is \bl{abs:real:market:d:gap:h4} percent and in nominal terms \bl{abs:nominal:market:d:gap:h4} percent.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Monetary policy news identifies a direction in asset prices, and scaled to a policy move that direction defines a financial conditions index in units of policy. The policy direction is nearly the direction of the macro-model indexes, its history moves with theirs, and a point of it goes with a policy-sized change in output a year later. Measured relative to the neutral rate, its level says whether conditions are tight or loose. The two directions coincide through equities, which carry the largest share of what policy moves. Most models used for policy analysis leave that channel out.

\section{The Weights and the Agreement Condition}\label{app:derivation}
Let the window movements be $x=\psi s+u$, with $u$ of mean zero, covariance $S$ and uncorrelated with $s$. Among linear estimators $\hat s=w'x$ with $w'\psi=1$, the variance of the error $w'u$ is $w'Sw$; minimizing it subject to $w'\psi=1$ gives $w=S^{-1}\psi/\psi'S^{-1}\psi$. A spending-based index with weights proportional to $g_h$, normalized to read $\psi$ as one, is $w_B=g_h/(g_h'\psi)$. For any movement, the difference between the two readings is $(w_B-w)'x=(w_B-w)'u$, because the policy part cancels. The readings coincide for every movement if and only if $w_B=w$, that is, if $g_h\propto S^{-1}\psi$.

\section{Data and Construction}\label{app:data}
All series are monthly averages, January 1973 to September 2026. Expected inflation is the Cleveland Fed's estimate at one and ten years from 1982 and the Livingston Survey before, shifted to the Cleveland level over 1982--84. $\rs$ is the one-sided Laubach--Williams estimate. CAPE is Shiller's, spliced in 1990--92 to a daily series. The real broad dollar is the Board's, spliced in 2006. Policy news, the window responses and the weights use \bl{n:events} USMPD monetary events, \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}; unscheduled events with no change in MP1 and March--April 2020 are excluded. The factor is scaled by its OLS slope, with an intercept, on the two-year yield change, so that one unit raises the two-year yield and the index each by exactly 1. The window S\&P 500 and DXY weights apply to 100 times the log of CAPE and of the real broad dollar. The FRB/US weights are cumulative GDP responses at four quarters to a persistent unit shift in each variable, with VAR expectations, divided by the response to a persistent 1 percentage point rise in the funds rate. The output gap is $100(\text{GDP}/\text{potential}-1)$ with CBO potential output. The replication package rebuilds every number (\texttt{fci\_paper/replication}).

\section{The Greenbooks and Tealbooks}\label{app:tealbook}
I read the alternative-scenarios section of every Greenbook Part 1 for the scheduled FOMC meetings from January 2006 to April 2010 and every Tealbook A from 2016 to 2020, from the Board's historical materials. Each Greenbook states in the first sentence of the section that the scenarios are simulations of the FRB/US model. From June 2010 the Tealbook describes them as simulations of staff models, and from 2016 each scenario heading names the model used. FRB/US remains in use throughout for the confidence intervals around the staff projection; the count concerns the alternative scenarios only.
