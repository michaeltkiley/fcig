\begin{abstract}
How many points of monetary policy is a movement in financial conditions worth? The answer depends on what the policymaker wants to know. An event study tells how large a conventional policy surprise an observed market movement resembles. A spending model tells how large a policy-rate change would have a comparable effect on GDP. I compare the two for the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar, weighting the assets by their responses to monetary policy news in announcement windows and by their effects on GDP in the FRB/US model. On the combination of asset prices that follows a policy surprise the two agree once they are put on a common scale, and that scale depends on how long the movements are assumed to last. They differ when equities and the dollar move in other proportions: in point estimates the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does. Realized unemployment and aggregate GDP growth do not separate the readings, and the tests have little power. In U.S. data, changing the measure of policy news alters financial-condition readings about as much as replacing market weights with spending weights. These choices leave the broad tightening of 2022--24 visible under every calibration, but they change the estimated magnitude, and occasionally the direction, of smaller movements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Suppose the Federal Reserve leaves the policy rate unchanged, but the dollar appreciates and stock prices fall. Both movements tend to restrain spending. How much tightening has occurred? One way to answer is to ask how large a monetary policy surprise would ordinarily produce a comparable movement in financial markets. Another is to ask how large a change in the policy rate would have a comparable effect on spending. A policymaker might reasonably expect the two answers to be close.

Whether they are close depends on which asset prices have moved. Around a typical monetary policy announcement, long yields, stock prices and the dollar move together in fairly predictable proportions. The two methods give similar readings for that combination. But suppose the tightening comes mainly through the dollar. In the Federal Reserve's model, an appreciation of the dollar has a larger effect on spending than its usual response to policy news would suggest. An equity-led tightening is the converse case. The distinction matters whenever policymakers use the familiar effects of their own announcements to interpret financial developments between meetings.

Figure~\ref{fig:two} gives an example. Spending-based indexes make such comparisons routine: \citet{caballero2026b}, using the Goldman Sachs weights, note that a 20 percent fall in equity prices and a 17 percent appreciation of the currency each tighten the U.S.\ index by about one point, because equities receive a small weight but are volatile and the exchange rate a larger weight through expenditure switching. Here, in one case equity valuations fall by 5 log points. In the other the dollar appreciates by 3.9 log points. I chose the two sizes so that the first method, with policy news measured by a factor from interest-rate futures, rates them alike: each is worth about a quarter of a point of the two-year yield (\bl{ag:ex:eq:mkt} and \bl{ag:ex:usd:mkt}). The second method, which weights asset prices by their effects on GDP in the FRB/US model, rates the dollar movement \bl{ag:h4:fr} times the equity movement. The figure also shows the first method when policy news is instead the change in the two-year Treasury yield in the same announcement windows. It then rates the dollar movement \bl{sg:two:ratio:mkt} times the equity movement. How policy news is measured matters as much for this comparison as how spending effects are priced.

Policymakers read the stance of policy from financial conditions. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}, and in March 2024 that ``there are many different financial conditions indicators, and you can kind of, you know, see different answers to that question'' \citep{powell2024}. A reading of financial conditions is a reading of policy only to the extent that a point of the index means a point of policy. This paper asks how many points of policy a movement in financial conditions is worth under two calibrations, how far the answer depends on measurement choices, whether the choice would have changed the reading of history, and whether later activity favors either calibration.

I find that the answer depends on the question. On the usual response of long yields, equities and the dollar to a policy surprise, the two calibrations agree up to a scale that depends on how long asset movements are assumed to last: the model prices the response at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} funds-rate points with persistent paths (interval \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi}) and at \bl{ag:bp:hl4} with a half-life of four quarters, against one by construction for the event study. They differ in composition. For the two movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two} the model rates the dollar movement \bl{sg:base:cf} points tighter than the equity movement. The market-implied reading rates them \bl{sg:base:cm} apart with the futures factor and \bl{sg:two:cm} apart with the two-year yield. In point estimates the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does under every definition of policy news that I try, and the size depends on the definition. The measure of policy news matters about as much as the choice of weights: in 12-month changes from 1996 to 2026 the average absolute difference between the model and the baseline market reading is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} of the average market move, and the average difference between the baseline market reading and the market reading with the two-year yield is \bl{nb:two}.

How often would this have changed an assessment? Across \bl{mo:all:n} months from 1996 to 2026, the baseline market-implied and spending-equivalent readings of the annual change agree on its direction in \bl{mo:all:25:agree} percent of months. They differ by at least a quarter point in \bl{mo:all:25:big:gap} percent of months (interval \bl{mo:all:25:big:gap:lo} to \bl{mo:all:25:big:gap:hi}), yet give opposite readings that are both at least that large in only \bl{mo:all:25:opposing:n} months. The choice changes the size of a tightening much more often than its direction. In 1998--2001 the average readings have opposite signs in the point estimates, both small, and the dollar was the main source of the difference. In 2022--24 every calibration shows a large tightening, about one point or more. Aggregate unemployment and GDP growth do not separate the two readings in the main sample. Composition does predict the net-export contribution to growth there, as the model's channels imply, but not significantly after 1996.

The paper has five parts. First, I define what a policy equivalent is under each method and show why their weights differ (Section~\ref{sec:indexes}). Second, I estimate the weights under four definitions of policy news, show how the model's weights arise from expenditure channels, and show how persistence changes the comparison (Section~\ref{sec:agree}). Third, I measure the disagreement on announcements and at horizons from a day to a year (Section~\ref{sec:size}). Fourth, I examine three historical episodes (Section~\ref{sec:history}). Finally, I ask whether subsequent activity favors either reading (Section~\ref{sec:outlook}). The appendices give the derivations, the construction of the series and the sensitivity of the results.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Weights and their meaning.} Indexes of financial conditions differ mainly in the source of their weights \citep{hatzius2010}: effects on output from a model \citep{dudley2000,hatzius2018,ajello2023}, principal components \citep{brave2011}, or event-study regressions \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005,bernanke2005,nakamura2018,bauer2023}. The older literature on monetary conditions indexes recognized that the weights on asset prices depend on the shock and the dynamics, so that their policy meaning is not invariant \citep{eika1996}. I quantify that difficulty with two observed sets of U.S.\ weights and ask where it matters.

\textbf{The closest comparisons.} \citet{arnaut2024} estimate how an existing index responds to policy surprises, \citet{sack2023} relate FCI-G to policy-induced changes in the two-year yield, and \citet{ajello2023} translate movements into a growth impulse with model multipliers. \citet{bletzinger2026} build a U.S.\ macro-finance index in units comparable to a policy rate and compare it with the Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G, finding divergences driven by equities and the strongest tightening in 2022--24. I ask how a policy-news weighting and a macro weighting read the same movements. \citet{boehm2025} show that announcements also move equities and exchange rates along a dimension orthogonal to yield changes, so a movement that departs from the conventional pattern can come from the announcement itself.

\textbf{Information about activity.} \citet{hatzius2010} build an index conditional on past activity and inflation, \citet{aramonte2013} show that comparisons among indexes depend on the crisis in the sample, and \citet{lombardi2025} separate safe yields from priced risk and find that risk predicts activity more strongly. \citet{adrian2019} show that conditions shift the lower quantiles of growth, and \citet{brave2011} build an index of risk, credit and leverage. \citet{caballero2026} define FCI$^*$, the level of financial conditions consistent with output at potential. The neutral rate matters for the level of every index \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}; I measure the inputs relative to $\rs$ and do not estimate a neutral level for the index (Appendix~\ref{app:steps}).
\textbf{Why the meaning of a point matters.} \citet{caballero2024} study targeting an index of financial conditions in a closed economy, and \citet{caballero2026b} extend the analysis to a multi-asset open economy, in which domestic asset prices and the exchange rate both enter aggregate demand and policy responses link the markets. That gives a reason to care which mix of asset prices delivers a given index reading. My question comes one step earlier: when does a portfolio inferred from conventional policy news give those markets the same relative importance as a spending calibration? The answer does not require endorsing targeting. The same ambiguity arises for anyone who communicates about a desired path of financial conditions, since the path has to say what a point on it means, and for a neutral benchmark such as FCI$^*$ \citep{caballero2026}, which takes the aggregation of asset movements as given.

\section{Two Ways to Read a Financial Movement}\label{sec:indexes}
\subsection{Two Meanings of a Policy Equivalent}\label{sec:meanings}
Take a movement in three asset prices: the 10-year Treasury yield, equity valuation and the real broad dollar. Each is measured relative to $r^*$ and to its 1973--2026 average (Appendix~\ref{app:steps}). I call a reading of the movement a policy equivalent if it says how much tightening of the policy rate the movement amounts to. There are two natural ways to compute one.

The market index starts from policy. At each Federal Open Market Committee announcement I measure the policy surprise with the factor of \citet{nakamura2018}, scaled so that one unit raises the two-year yield by one percentage point, and I measure how the three asset prices respond in a narrow window around the announcement. The weights are chosen to recover the size of the surprise from the three asset prices together. A movement is then worth the size of the surprise that would, on average, produce it, in points of the two-year yield. A policy surprise of one unit reads one. I call this the market-implied reading.

The FRB/US index starts from spending. Each asset price is weighted by its effect on GDP four quarters later in the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model \citep{brayton2014}, in the experiment in which the asset price shifts persistently, divided by the effect of a persistent one percentage point rise in the federal funds rate. A movement is then worth the funds-rate change with the same effect on GDP, in funds-rate points. I call this the spending-equivalent reading.

The first calculation reads a movement through what policy does to markets. The second reads it through what markets do to spending. Both are legitimate. They need not agree.

\subsection{Why the Weights Differ}\label{sec:why}
Consider the dollar. It can be a reliable indicator of a policy surprise because it responds consistently when the expected path of U.S.\ interest rates changes. How useful it is as an indicator depends on how much it also moves for other reasons, and on what yields and equities already reveal. Its weight in a spending index depends on different things: how an appreciation affects net exports, and how long the appreciation lasts. An asset can be an excellent indicator of a policy surprise and have a modest effect on spending. It can also be a noisy indicator and have a large effect on spending. The same is true of equities, whose effect on spending runs through wealth and the cost of capital. A fall in equity prices that reflects a higher discount rate is policy-like, and a fall that reflects weaker earnings prospects is not policy at all. The event study averages over announcements, where the first kind dominates. The model prices an equity movement by its estimated effect on spending, whatever its source. Neither calculation is wrong about its own question.

Two equations make the argument precise. Let $x$ be the movement in the three asset prices around an announcement, $s$ the policy surprise, and $\psi$ the vector of responses of the asset prices to a unit of surprise:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
x=\psi s+u .
\end{equation}
The vector $u$ collects the ways in which a particular movement departs from the usual response to conventional rate news. It has covariance $\Sigma$. Even an announcement can produce such a departure. The market weights $w_M$ recover $s$ from $x$ by generalized least squares, $w_M\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$, normalized so that $w_M'\psi=1$. The FRB/US weights $b_h$ are the effects of the three asset prices on GDP at horizon $h$ in funds-rate points. The model prices the usual response $\psi$ at $b_h'\psi$, which I report in its own units, and dividing by it gives the normalized weights $w_{B,h}=b_h/(b_h'\psi)$, so that both indexes read one on a conventional policy surprise. The difference between the two readings of a movement $x$ is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'u ,
\end{equation}
because the policy part $\psi s$ cancels. The same difference has a second reading. The fitted policy component of any movement $x$ is $\psi M$, where $M=w_M'x$ is the market-implied reading; the remainder $x-\psi M$ is the combination of asset prices that a conventional policy surprise cannot reproduce. Once the scales are matched, the spending-equivalent valuation of that remainder is precisely the difference between the two readings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rem}
D_h(x)=w_{B,h}'(x-\psi M),
\end{equation}
because $w_{B,h}'\psi=1$. A reader can say what the difference measures without assigning it a structural cause. In words, the two methods disagree only to the extent that asset prices move in unusual proportions and the methods value those departures differently. An unusual movement is not enough by itself: if the weights are the same, there is nothing to value differently. A difference in weights is not enough either: if the asset prices keep moving together as they do around policy news, the difference has nothing to act on. That is why two indexes can track each other closely for decades and still give different readings during an equity boom or a dollar surge. Throughout, $D$ is the difference computed on the movements of the three asset prices over the horizon in question, and in Section~\ref{sec:outlook} it is computed on 12-month changes. Appendix~\ref{app:ma} derives the weights.

\subsection{Putting Numbers on It}\label{sec:requires}
Figure~\ref{fig:two} applies the weights to two movements. With policy news measured by the futures factor, the market-implied reading rates a 5 log point fall in equity valuation and a 3.9 log point appreciation of the dollar alike. The spending-equivalent reading gives the dollar movement \bl{ag:h4:fr} times the effect of the equity movement, and the ratio of ratios, the model's dollar-to-equity ratio over the market's, is \bl{ag:h4:rr} (interval \bl{ag:h4:rr:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:rr:hi}). With policy news measured by the change in the two-year yield, the market-implied reading already gives the dollar \bl{sg:two:ratio:mkt} times the equity movement and the ratio of ratios is \bl{sg:two:ror}. The market-implied reading is therefore not a single object: its weights depend on what the researcher calls policy news, as the model's depend on the horizon and persistence of the experiment. Neither movement is a policy surprise, so nothing in the construction requires the readings to agree. Whether the difference is large in practice depends on how often asset prices move in such proportions, which is the question of Section~\ref{sec:size}.

\section{Why the Two Answers Agree on a Policy Move}\label{sec:agree}
\subsection{The Estimated Weights}\label{sec:market}
Table~\ref{tab:weights} gives the market weights. A policy move that raises the two-year yield by one point raises the 10-year yield by \bl{psi:ten} points, lowers equity valuation by \bl{abs:psi:eq} percent and raises the real dollar by \bl{psi:usd} percent. The data are the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}: \bl{n:events} announcements from \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}. Each variable carries a substantial part of the policy move, with shares of \bl{share:ten} for the 10-year yield, \bl{share:eq} for equity valuation and \bl{share:usd} for the dollar. The weights are imprecise, the weight on the 10-year yield most of all (Appendix~\ref{sec:precision}), and they depend on how policy news is measured (Section~\ref{sec:signal}).

\input{../replication/report/tab_weights}

The weights say what the variables do when policy moves, not what causes their movements at other times. A rise in stock prices that comes from news about earnings has little to do with policy, and the index treats it as if it did. An announcement can also reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and then the responses mix policy and information \citep{nakamura2018}. I measure the responses as markets receive them and set the information effect aside. The index measures policy-equivalent tightening. It is not a forecast of activity.

Table~\ref{tab:frbus} compares the market weights with the FRB/US weights. The two sets agree on the 10-year yield, where the FRB/US weight is \bl{ratio:ten} times the market weight. The model puts less weight on equity valuation (\bl{ratio:eq} times) and more on the dollar (\bl{ratio:usd} times). On a policy move the difference does not matter, because the policy part cancels in equation~(\ref{eq:D}). On a departure from the usual response, the model gives a fall in equity prices its estimated effect on output, and the market index treats it as policy-equivalent.

\subsection{What Counts as Policy News?}\label{sec:signal}
The market weights are designed to recover a specified signal, so the definition of policy news is a measurement choice in its own right. The baseline signal is the futures-based factor of \citet{nakamura2018}. A natural alternative is the change in the two-year Treasury yield in the same announcement windows, since the index is quoted in two-year-yield points. Table~\ref{tab:signal} re-estimates the market weights with each definition, using the same 253 announcements for the first two, and also with the factor through 2019 and outside the lower-bound years. The model weights are held fixed and normalized again for each definition.

\input{../replication/report/tab_signal}

\textbf{Two signals on the same announcements, two checks on the sample.} The futures factor and the two-year yield are two measures of policy news on the same 253 announcements, and they answer the question of this section. Estimating through 2019 and outside the lower-bound years asks a different question, whether the weights depend on the events used, so I treat these two as checks.

\textbf{The policymaker's question: the contrast in points.} Take the two movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two}. The model rates the dollar movement \bl{sg:base:cf} points tighter than the equity movement (the normalization changes slightly with the signal). The market-implied reading rates them \bl{sg:base:cm} apart with the futures factor and \bl{sg:two:cm} apart with the two-year yield. The model less market contrast is \bl{sg:base:contrast:diff} (90 percent interval \bl{sg:base:contrast:diff:lo} to \bl{sg:base:contrast:diff:hi}) for the baseline, \bl{sg:two:contrast:diff} (\bl{sg:two:contrast:diff:lo} to \bl{sg:two:contrast:diff:hi}) for the two-year yield, \bl{sg:t19:contrast:diff} (\bl{sg:t19:contrast:diff:lo} to \bl{sg:t19:contrast:diff:hi}) through 2019 and \bl{sg:nlb:contrast:diff} (\bl{sg:nlb:contrast:diff:lo} to \bl{sg:nlb:contrast:diff:hi}) without the lower-bound years. The intervals come from 5,000 bootstrap draws of the announcements; with 500 draws the lower endpoint of the baseline interval was below zero. Switching from the futures factor to the two-year yield lowers the contrast by \bl{pr:two:contrast:diff:d} on common draws (\bl{pr:two:contrast:diff:dlo} to \bl{pr:two:contrast:diff:dhi}). That is about 14 basis points of the two-year yield for these two movements, and the interval includes zero.

\textbf{Does the model's dollar-to-equity weight ratio equal the market's?} This is a question about proportionality and not about size. The cross-product $c=w_{B,\$}w_{M,E}-w_{B,E}w_{M,\$}$ is zero if and only if the two weight vectors give the dollar and equities the same relative weight. Its 90 percent interval excludes zero for the futures factor (\bl{pr:base:cross}, \bl{pr:base:cross:lo} to \bl{pr:base:cross:hi}) and includes it for the two-year yield (\bl{pr:two:cross}, \bl{pr:two:cross:lo} to \bl{pr:two:cross:hi}). The ratio of the model's dollar-to-equity ratio to the market's, which tests the same hypothesis on a scale-free ratio, is \bl{sg:base:ror} for the baseline, \bl{sg:two:ror} for the two-year yield (interval \bl{sg:two:ror:lo} to \bl{sg:two:ror:hi}), \bl{sg:t19:ror} through 2019 and \bl{sg:nlb:ror} without the lower-bound years. Its denominator, the market's equity effect, is small and imprecise, so I treat the ratio as secondary.

\textbf{Does switching the signal change the market's own relative weights?} The direct test uses the two market weight vectors on the same draws. With $F$ the futures factor and $Y$ the two-year yield, $T=w^{F}_{M,\$}w^{Y}_{M,E}-w^{Y}_{M,\$}w^{F}_{M,E}$ is zero if the two calibrations give the dollar and equities proportional weights. It is \bl{tp:two} (\bl{tp:two:lo} to \bl{tp:two:hi}) and positive in \bl{tp:two:pos} percent of the draws. The market's dollar-to-equity weight ratio under the two-year yield is \bl{tp:two:rr} times its ratio under the factor (\bl{tp:two:rr:lo} to \bl{tp:two:rr:hi}). The change is large in the point estimates, and its interval includes zero at the 10 percent level. Through 2019 and without the lower-bound years the factor's own weights are close to the baseline (\bl{tp:t19:rr} and \bl{tp:nlb:rr} times). I report the change in relative weights as a point-estimate finding. The intervals are not adjusted for the number of definitions.

\textbf{What each signal captures.} The two signals are close in the announcement windows, with a correlation of \bl{sig:corr}, but they are not the same. Write the two-year change as the factor plus a remainder. The remainder is \bl{sp:resvar} percent of the variance of the two-year change (interval \bl{sp:resvar:lo} to \bl{sp:resvar:hi} percent), or about \bl{sp:ressd} percent of its standard deviation. Figure~\ref{fig:signal} shows what each part moves. A point of the factor raises the 10-year yield by \bl{sp:ten:F} points, lowers the S\&P 500 by \bl{abs:sp:eq:F} percent and raises the dollar by \bl{sp:usd:F} percent. A point of the remainder is associated with a rise in the 10-year yield of \bl{sp:ten:R} points and in the dollar of \bl{sp:usd:R} percent, and with an equity response of \bl{sp:eq:R} percent (\bl{sp:eq:R:lo} to \bl{sp:eq:R:hi}) that the data cannot distinguish from zero. Giving the remainder a larger role in the definition of policy news therefore moves the weights toward the 10-year yield and the dollar and away from equities. The equity weight falls from \bl{sg:base:weq} to \bl{sg:two:weq}. Switching the response vector and the residual covariance in turn, each accounts for about half of the change (\bl{sig:cf:psi} and \bl{sig:cf:sig}), and the order does not matter. The regressions show what variation each signal captures. They do not show whether the remainder is further policy news, a term-premium movement or measurement error.

\textbf{What carries through.} The disagreement is similar under every definition: the ratio of the mean absolute difference to the mean absolute market move in 12-month changes is \bl{sg:base:d12}, \bl{sg:two:d12}, \bl{sg:t19:d12} and \bl{sg:nlb:d12}. The 1998--2001 readings have opposite signs under all four definitions in the point estimates, with the dollar contributing more to the difference than equities (Table~\ref{tab:signal}). In 2022--24 every definition shows a large tightening: both readings are above one point under all four. They are equal to within 0.05 under the baseline and through 2019. Under the two-year yield the market-implied reading is \bl{sg:two:22m} and the spending-equivalent reading \bl{sg:two:22f}, and without the lower-bound years \bl{sg:nlb:22m} and \bl{sg:nlb:22f}. That difference comes from the 10-year yield, which the market weights price at 0.93 to 0.99 against about 0.6 in the normalized model weights, and not from equities and the dollar. The near-exact offset of the equity and dollar contributions in 2022--24 belongs to the baseline definition. The judgment that 2022--24 was a large tightening does not.

\textbf{A check on the spending side.} The multipliers of the Board's FCI-G offer a check, with the caveat that they share a lineage with FRB/US, so agreement measures robustness within a family. Per 1 percent change in each variable, the FCI-G dollar multiplier is \bl{fg:fcig} times its equity multiplier, against \bl{fg:frbus} in FRB/US (these ratios use the multipliers per 1 percent, not the sizes of the movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two}, for which the FRB/US ratio is \bl{ag:h4:fr}). The ratio of ratios against the market is \bl{fg:base} under the baseline signal and \bl{fg:two} under the two-year yield. FCI-G corroborates the direction under the futures factors and not under the two-year yield. A genuinely independent check would use another model with documented asset-specific multipliers.

\subsection{What Lies behind the Model's Weights}\label{sec:channels}
The FRB/US weights are effects on GDP, and a reader should see where they come from. Figure~\ref{fig:channels} follows the two movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two} through the model under one persistence assumption, a persistent shift. Only the stated variable moves. The policy rate, the Treasury curve, the mortgage and car-loan rates, the BBB spread, equity valuation, household stock wealth and the dollar are held at baseline in every experiment except their own, so the policy rule is inactive and expectations are those of the model's VAR. The GDP object is the percent deviation of real GDP from baseline. The contributions are the model's own expenditure identities, weighted by shares in GDP, and they sum to GDP.

The equity movement reduces GDP by \bl{ch:gdp:eq} percent after four quarters, and domestic demand accounts for it. Consumption supplies \bl{ch:eq:cons} percent of the effect, through wealth and the cost of capital, and business fixed investment \bl{ch:eq:bfi} percent. Domestic demand in all supplies \bl{ch:eq:dom} percent, and net exports offset \bl{abs:ch:eq:nx} percent of it, because weaker demand reduces imports; inventories supply \bl{ch:eq:inv} percent and government \bl{ch:eq:gov} percent, so that the shares sum to 100. The dollar movement reduces GDP by \bl{ch:gdp:usd} percent, and net exports account for \bl{ch:usd:nx} percent of it: exports \bl{ch:usd:exp} percent and imports \bl{ch:usd:imp} percent. Domestic demand contributes \bl{ch:usd:dom} percent, inventories \bl{ch:usd:inv} percent and government \bl{ch:usd:gov} percent of the GDP effect. With a half-life of four quarters the shares are similar (consumption \bl{ch:eq:cons4} percent of the equity effect and net exports \bl{ch:usd:nx4} percent of the dollar effect). The two weights therefore reflect two different channels, and the dollar's larger weight is a statement about trade. It carries no implication about how persistent the dollar is, which is the assumption of the experiment.

The experiments need two mappings from the market series to the model. The equity experiment shocks the required return on equity, and household stock wealth falls with it in the same simulation, so valuation and wealth move jointly; the 5 log point fall in valuation is entered as a 5 percent fall in stock wealth on impact. The market side measures equity by the S\&P 500 return in the announcement window, which gives the weight on equity valuation, and applies that weight to monthly changes in the earnings-yield gap converted to CAPE log points (Appendix~\ref{app:steps}); a one percent window return is treated as one log point of valuation, with earnings unchanged. The dollar experiment raises the model's real exchange rate by one percent per unit, and the market side measures the dollar by the DXY in the window and applies the weight to the log of the broad real dollar. DXY is a nominal index of six currencies and the model's dollar is a trade-weighted real index. I treat them as the same unit, and over a window or a month the nominal moves dominate.

The model's own policy shock gives a funds-rate equivalent of \bl{ps:ffr} for the usual announcement mix against \bl{ps:replay} for the announcement response. FRB/US can therefore assign a value near one to the observed announcement mix even though its own policy shock generates a different mix: heavier in equities and lighter in the dollar. The comparison is not a decomposition of how much of transmission the three assets omit, because the policy shock changes the path, the equity response, the channels that operate and the unit of normalization together. Appendix~\ref{app:policyrate} gives the experiment.

\subsection{The FRB/US Reading of a Policy Move}\label{sec:onannounce}
The model prices the usual response of the three variables to a unit of policy news at \bl{ag:h2:bpsi}, \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} and \bl{ag:h8:bpsi} funds-rate points at two, four and eight quarters (Table~\ref{tab:agree}). At four quarters the 90 percent interval is \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi}. The market index prices the same response at exactly one. The two do not differ significantly at any horizon with persistent paths; with decaying paths the model's reading is lower (Section~\ref{sec:persist}). Nothing in the construction of the FRB/US weights refers to policy announcements, so a value near one is an empirical coincidence in these units. It is not a general property of policy: the model's own policy shock below has a funds-rate equivalent of \bl{ps:ffr} (Section~\ref{sec:channels}).

The units differ. A unit of the market signal raises the two-year yield by one percentage point on impact. A unit of the model experiment is a persistent one-point rise in the funds rate. The number \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} is a cross-experiment conversion. It does not establish that the two policy paths have the same effect on GDP. Dividing the model's weights by it puts both indexes on one scale and makes them agree exactly along the policy direction. That is an identity and not a finding. The finding is the number before it was divided out.

\subsection{Composition or Persistence?}\label{sec:persist}
The comparison confounds two things. A persistent dollar appreciation in FRB/US and the dollar's response to policy news are different experiments, so a difference in weights could reflect the initial composition of the movement or the path that follows it. Figure~\ref{fig:persist} separates them with the same set of calculations under common decaying paths for the three variables, with half-lives of 8, 4 and 2 quarters.

Persistence changes how much tightening a policy move represents. The model's reading of the usual response falls from \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} with persistent paths to \bl{ag:bp:hl4} (interval \bl{ag:bp:hl4:lo} to \bl{ag:bp:hl4:hi}) with a half-life of four quarters and \bl{ag:bp:hl2} with a half-life of two. Persistence does not change which movement counts more. With the same decay for the dollar and for equities, the model's dollar-to-equity ratio is \bl{ag:mp:hl8}, \bl{ag:mp:hl4} and \bl{ag:mp:hl2} at half-lives of 8, 4 and 2 quarters, against \bl{ag:mkt:ratio} for the market weights (interval \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:lo} to \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:hi}). Different half-lives for the two assets give a wider range, \bl{ag:mp:min} to \bl{ag:mp:max}, and none near one. Across horizons of two to eight quarters and these paths the model's reading of the usual response runs from \bl{bp:min} to \bl{bp:max}. The persistent case is the closest to the data, since the half-lives of the levels of the three variables since 1996 are 20 to 26 quarters, so the range for decaying paths shows the sensitivity of the conversion and not a likely value. The contrast between the two weightings is a contrast in the effect on GDP per unit of each asset price. The definition of policy news (Section~\ref{sec:signal}) moves the contrast by an amount at least as large.

\section{How Much Do the Two Readings Disagree in Practice?}\label{sec:size}
The disagreement depends on how often asset prices move in proportions other than the usual response. I measure it as the mean absolute value of $D$ divided by the mean absolute value of the market move, over the same windows. A ratio of 0.3 means that the typical difference between the two readings is a third of the typical move.

On the 253 announcements, with the weights re-estimated without the event being evaluated, the ratio in intraday windows is \bl{dz:ev:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:ev:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:ev:ratio:hi}), and the 90th percentile of the difference is \bl{dz:ev:p90} against a mean absolute market move of \bl{dz:ev:move}. These are the windows in which the weights are estimated, with each event held out.

The ratio is larger in daily data than in the intraday windows and does not shrink at longer horizons. On daily changes of the same three assets, 1996--2026, it is \bl{dl:k1:ann} on announcement days and \bl{dl:k1:oth} on other days; at one week it is \bl{dl:k5:ann} and \bl{dl:k5:oth}, and at 22 trading days \bl{dl:k22:ann} and \bl{dl:k22:oth} (Table~\ref{tab:daily}). No difference between announcement and other windows is distinguishable from zero. Announcement days contain a great deal of financial news besides the announcement. The fitted response to policy news is a particular combination of asset prices, and the difference over a whole announcement day looks like the difference on other days.

\input{../replication/report/tab_daily}

For windows that end on any day, the ratio is \bl{dl:lad1}, \bl{dl:lad5}, \bl{dl:lad22}, \bl{dl:lad66} and \bl{dl:lad252} at 1, 5, 22, 66 and 252 trading days. A window of 22 or more days contains announcements in any case; removing the moves on announcement days gives \bl{dl:ex1}, \bl{dl:ex5}, \bl{dl:ex22}, \bl{dl:ex66} and \bl{dl:ex252}, within 0.02 of those. In 12-month changes of the monthly series, the ratio is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:12m:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:12m:ratio:hi}), and the difference exceeds 0.25 in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months (Figure~\ref{fig:D}). The months overlap, so runs in this series are not independent evidence of persistence. The statistic is a sample summary with wide intervals. Roughly 0.25 to 0.33 describes the baseline across horizons from a day to a year, and the ratio is 0.36 to 0.42 under the other definitions of policy news (Table~\ref{tab:signal}).

How large is that relative to measurement noise in the market weights themselves? The same statistic computed on bootstrap draws of the baseline market weights against their point estimates has a median of \bl{nb:med} (5th to 95th percentile \bl{nb:lo} to \bl{nb:hi}), and the baseline market weights against those of the two-year definition give \bl{nb:two} (\bl{nb:t19} through 2019 and \bl{nb:nlb} without the lower-bound years), against \bl{nb:fr} for the spending-equivalent weights against the baseline market weights. The disagreement between the model and the market is therefore about as large as the disagreement between two market calibrations that differ only in the measure of policy news (\bl{nb:two} against \bl{dz:12m:ratio}), and it is of the size of the sampling noise in the market weights. The sampling noise limits how finely the two calibrations can be told apart. It is a statement about precision and is separate from the differences across calibrations.

The intraday and daily figures do not isolate a source for the gap between 0.19 and 0.3. The intraday windows are narrow and the weights exclude the event evaluated. The daily comparison uses nominal assets and weights fitted on the same announcements (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The comparison across horizons is the cleaner result: the disagreement does not shrink as the window lengthens.

\section{Would a Policymaker Have Read History Differently?}\label{sec:history}
Three episodes show when the choice of weights matters. All readings are retrospective estimates, made with weights estimated on the full sample. For each episode I report two comparisons that are easily confused. The first is the difference between the full FRB/US index and the market index in average level, which includes variables the market index does not have and the effect of centering each variable on its average. The second applies the two weightings to the same three assets in 12-month changes, which isolates the weights. A gap in levels can coexist with agreement in changes. Table~\ref{tab:epmain} gives both. Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows the baseline readings month by month and the contributions of each asset to their difference, and Figure~\ref{fig:band} shows the two readings under every combination of the measurement choices of Section~\ref{sec:agree}.

Because the model gives equities less weight than the event study, the fall in equities makes the spending-equivalent reading less tight than the market-implied one, and contributes \bl{ce:2022:ceq} to $D$. Because the model gives the dollar more weight, the appreciation makes the spending-equivalent reading tighter, and contributes \bl{ce:2022:cusd}. The two contributions to the disagreement cancel, so that $D$ averages \bl{ce:2022:D} (\bl{ce:2022:D:lo} to \bl{ce:2022:D:hi}), although each asset tightened conditions. A policymaker would have described a sizable tightening under every one of them. The full indexes differ in average level by \bl{br:ep2022:gap} points (interval \bl{br:ep2022:lo} to \bl{br:ep2022:hi}), with the equity weights (\bl{br:ep2022:eq}) and the dollar (\bl{br:ep2022:usd}) both contributing, and that difference does not affect the readings of the year's tightening. Unemployment rose by \bl{ce:2022:real} points over the following year, against forecasts of \bl{ce:2022:fcm} from the market-implied reading and \bl{ce:2022:fcf} from the spending-equivalent reading. Both weightings overpredicted the rise, so the weights do not explain the miss.

\textbf{Across measurement choices.} In 1998--2001 the market-implied readings run from \bl{bd:1998:mlo} to \bl{bd:1998:mhi} across the four definitions of policy news and the spending-equivalent readings from \bl{bd:1998:flo} to \bl{bd:1998:fhi} across definitions and persistence assumptions, so that the ranges of the point estimates do not overlap and the signs differ throughout; the intervals of the baseline readings do overlap (Table~\ref{tab:epmain} and the text above). In 2022--24 the market-implied readings run from \bl{bd:2022:mlo} to \bl{bd:2022:mhi} and the spending-equivalent readings from \bl{bd:2022:flo} to \bl{bd:2022:fhi}; both are about one point or more of tightening, and the ranges overlap. Persistence barely moves the normalized spending-equivalent reading, because the normalization absorbs the scale (the spread across persistence assumptions is \bl{bd:2022:fper:spread} in 2022--24), although it moves the conversion of the typical policy response into funds-rate points. The definition of policy news moves the market-implied reading, and in 2022--24 the spread of the market-implied readings is larger than the spread of the spending-equivalent ones. On the market side the definition of policy news sets the range of the readings, and on the model side persistence adds little.

\textbf{2004--06.} The matched readings are close (the difference is \bl{ce:2004:D}), and the gap in levels is \bl{br:ep2004:gap} (interval \bl{br:ep2004:lo} to \bl{br:ep2004:hi}). The equity weights add \bl{br:ep2004:eq} and house prices subtract \bl{abs:br:ep2004:house}, so the full FRB/US index contains a variable that the market index does not have, and equities do not explain the gap.


\textbf{How representative are the episodes?} I chose the episodes for their economic interest. They illustrate a mechanism and are not a random draw. By the monthly counts of Table~\ref{tab:months}, 1998--2001 is a period of unusually frequent large gaps (\bl{mo:19982001:25:big:gap:n} of \bl{mo:19982001:n} months against \bl{mo:all:25:big:gap} percent of all months) and 2022--24 is quieter than typical (\bl{mo:202224:25:big:gap:n} of \bl{mo:202224:n} months).

These episodes give a concrete answer to when the choice of weights matters, in point estimates. The model gives less weight than the event study to equities and more to the dollar under the baseline definition, so the two readings part when valuations and the dollar rise together, as in 1998--99, and they offset when valuations fall as the dollar rises, as in 2022--24. The offset does not survive every definition of policy news, as the paragraph on measurement choices above shows.

\section{Can Subsequent Activity Tell the Weightings Apart?}\label{sec:outlook}
\subsection{The Test}\label{sec:onetest}
Consider two years in which the market index tightened by the same amount. If the dollar accounted for more of the tightening in one year, did activity subsequently weaken more? If the market-implied reading is a sufficient summary of the asset prices, the answer is no. If the spending-equivalent reading is, the answer is yes, by the amount that the model's weights imply. I regress the 12-month-ahead change in the unemployment gap on own lags and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test}
y_{t+12}-y_t=\cdots+\beta M_t+\gamma D_t ,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the 12-month change in the market index (relative to $r^*$) and $D$ the 12-month change of the normalized FRB/US-minus-market difference on the same changes. If the market-implied reading is sufficient, $\gamma=0$. If the spending-equivalent reading $M+D$ is sufficient, $\gamma=\beta$. I read the two restrictions as tests of incremental predictive sufficiency in the span of the two portfolios, conditional on the controls. Three assets span a larger space, and the unrestricted three-asset regression of Section~\ref{sec:eqalone} is a different test. Failure to separate the portfolios for aggregate unemployment or GDP is consistent with low power and with forward-looking asset prices, and it cannot validate either structural weight. That wording matters for unemployment, since the FRB/US weights are constructed from effects on GDP; four-quarter GDP growth is the model-facing check. This is the only primary test, and Table~\ref{tab:comp} reports both restrictions with the joint covariance of the estimates.

\subsection{The Answer}\label{sec:answer}
On 1976--2026 the coefficient on the index change is \bl{rs:du:main:beta} and on composition \bl{rs:du:main:gamma} (\bl{rs:du:main:gamma:lo} to \bl{rs:du:main:gamma:hi}); the difference $\gamma-\beta$ is \bl{rs:du:main:gmb} (\bl{rs:du:main:gmb:lo} to \bl{rs:du:main:gmb:hi}). Neither restriction is rejected. Moving $D$ from its 5th to its 95th percentile at the same index change shifts the year-ahead outlook by \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point, with an interval from \bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}, so the sample cannot establish an outlook difference of either sign. On 1996--2026 the coefficient on composition is \bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma} (\bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma:lo} to \bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma:hi}) and the model's restriction is rejected ($\gamma-\beta=$ \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb}, \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb:lo} to \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb:hi}); excluding 2008--15 the coefficient on composition is \bl{rs:du:ex200815:gamma} and $\gamma-\beta$ is \bl{rs:du:ex200815:gmb}, close to the model's restriction. The sign changes across samples. For GDP growth neither restriction is rejected, and adding $D$ does not significantly improve out-of-sample forecasts (Clark--West \bl{cm:addD:cw}).

\subsection{Equities Alone}\label{sec:eqalone}
Equity valuation alone raises the adjusted $R^2$ of the change in unemployment by \bl{cm:eq:gain}, the market index by \bl{cm:mkt:gain}, the FRB/US weights by \bl{cm:frbus:gain}, the 10-year yield by \bl{cm:y10:gain}, the dollar by \bl{cm:usd:gain} and the three variables entered freely by \bl{cm:free:gain}. Both indexes draw heavily on equity valuation, which helps explain why unemployment data have difficulty distinguishing between them: the movements that distinguish the weightings are the ones in which the dollar and equity valuation move in different proportions, and there are few of them. Entered freely, the coefficients are not significantly different from proportional to the market weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wm}) or to the FRB/US weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wb}); the power is low.

\subsection{Trade-Sensitive and Domestic Expenditure}\label{sec:expend}
Section~\ref{sec:channels} traced the model's weights to channels: consumption and investment for equities, net exports for the dollar. If the weights describe those channels, composition might predict the components separately even where it does not predict the aggregates. I wrote two predictions from the channel logic before estimating the components: the coefficient on $D$ is negative for the contribution of net exports to growth over the next four quarters (P1), and it is larger for consumption and private investment than for net exports (P2). The aggregate nulls of the preceding subsections were already known, so the exercise is a check on a prediction and not a clean pre-registered test. Table~\ref{tab:expend} reports both.

Excluding 2008--15 the forecast coefficient is \bl{sd:u:ex200815:forecast} and the realization \bl{sd:u:ex200815:realized}, and the error is \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error}, with a robust interval of \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:hi}, which excludes zero, and a block-bootstrap interval of \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:blo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:bhi}, which includes it. Without both 2001 and 2008--09 the error is \bl{sd:u:ex2001and200809:error} (\bl{sd:u:ex2001and200809:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex2001and200809:error:hi}). The sign of the error coefficient is stable and its size is not: most of the full-sample effect on the realization comes from 2008--09. The outcomes overlap and the coefficients are not adjusted for their number.

What can a policymaker take from this? The two readings can be used interchangeably when markets move as they do around policy news, and the data cannot say which to prefer when they do not. A reading from either index should be accompanied by the question of what moved: the dollar, valuations, or yields. The answer indicates whether the two readings are likely to part.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}
The paper asks how many points of monetary policy a movement in financial conditions is worth. The answer depends on the question. An event study says how large a conventional policy surprise the movement resembles. A spending model says how large a policy-rate change would have a comparable effect on GDP. For the usual response to a policy surprise the two are close once they are put on one scale, and that scale is a measurement choice: the model prices the response at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} funds-rate points with persistent paths and \bl{ag:bp:hl4} with a half-life of four quarters. For other combinations of asset prices the readings differ. In point estimates the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does under every definition of policy news that I try. The model's weights come from two channels, consumption and investment for equities and net exports for the dollar.

The second result is that the measure of policy news matters about as much as the choice between market and spending weights. In 12-month changes the average difference between the model and the baseline market reading is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} of the average market move, and the average difference between the baseline market reading and the market reading with the two-year yield is \bl{nb:two}. The sampling noise in the market weights is of similar size, so the data cannot tell the calibrations apart much more finely than that.

The third result is how often this matters. Across \bl{mo:all:n} months from 1996 to 2026 the baseline readings agree on the direction of the annual change in \bl{mo:all:25:agree} percent of months, differ by at least a quarter point in \bl{mo:all:25:big:gap} percent, and oppose each other materially in only \bl{mo:all:25:opposing:n}. In 1998--2001 the readings differed mostly in size, with a gap of about 0.7 points in early 1998. In 2022--24 every calibration shows a large tightening of about one point or more. The baseline offset of the equity and dollar contributions is specific to that definition of policy news.

Aggregate activity does not separate the two readings, and the tests have little power. After 1996 the model's restriction is rejected for unemployment in the baseline sample, and the forecast-error coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:spf} are significant for the full sample and depend on 2008--09. Composition predicts the net-export contribution to growth under the futures-factor definitions, mostly through equities, which is not clean evidence for the dollar channel. A movement that departs from the policy-news mix can come from the announcement itself \citep{boehm2025}.

The limits are those of the design. The FRB/US weights come from one model and one experiment and are held fixed in the intervals. The market weights are imprecise, the horizons are compared on overlapping data, the daily comparison uses nominal assets, and there are five recessions in the sample. The paper does not say which definition of policy news is right. A reader who wants to use either index can report both readings and can treat their difference as a measure of how much the answer depends on the calibration.

\section{The Weights: a Derivation}\label{app:ma}
Let the movements in the three asset prices be $x=\psi s+u$, where $s$ is policy news, $\psi$ the responses and $u$ everything else, with covariance $\Sigma$. Let $g_h$ be the effects of a persistent unit shift in each asset price on spending at horizon $h$. The FRB/US weights are proportional to $g_h$. The market weights solve the problem of recovering $s$ from $x$: the generalized least squares estimator is $\hat s=w_M'x$ with $w_M=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$, which satisfies $w_M'\psi=1$ and, by the Gauss--Markov theorem, minimizes the variance of the estimate among linear unbiased estimators of $s$ when $u$ has mean zero and covariance $\Sigma$ and is uncorrelated with $s$. For any $x$ the difference between the normalized readings is $D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'u$, because the policy part cancels. The readings coincide for every movement if and only if $w_{B,h}=w_M$, that is, if $g_h\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$: the criterion that recovers policy news precisely and the criterion that measures spending effects select the same weights only in that case. The variance of the difference is $(w_{B,h}-w_M)'\Sigma(w_{B,h}-w_M)$.

Observed movements need not be of the form $\psi s+u$ with a single policy factor, and the model's effects need not be those of a movement of the same shape. If all observed movements lay along the single direction $\psi$, every weight vector normalized to price $\psi$ at one would give the same reading on them. The data would then identify the reading along that direction and leave the weights themselves undetermined. In the announcement windows $u$ is not zero, and it is the variation in $u$ that identifies $\Sigma$ and hence the weights. What distinguishes the two weightings in practice is the exposure of the difference in weights to the movements that occur, which Table~\ref{tab:dsize} measures as a ratio to the market move.

\section{Construction of the Series}\label{app:steps}
The three asset prices enter in three forms, each answering a problem of the one before. The headline results use the third.

\textbf{Nominal.} Rates as published. A nominal index treats a 10-year yield of 8 percent alike in 1981 and in 2000. That is reasonable only if inflation expectations and the neutral real rate are stable, and neither was. Expected inflation over the next year averaged \bl{einf:1981} percent in 1981 and is \bl{einf:latest} percent today, and the Laubach--Williams estimate of $\rs$ averaged \bl{rstar:lw:pre} percent over 1990--2007 and \bl{rstar:lw:post} percent over 2009--2016 (Figure~\ref{fig:infl}).

\textbf{Real.} Each rate less expected inflation at a matched horizon: ten-year expected inflation for the 10-year yield. The dollar is the real broad index.

\textbf{Relative to $\rs$.} The real 10-year yield less $\rs$. Equity valuation enters through the earnings yield $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$, which is also an implicit real rate, as the gap $E/P-\rs$, converted to CAPE log points at the sample-average earnings yield so that its weight on CAPE is unchanged. The baseline uses the one-sided, full-sample Laubach--Williams estimate. Each variable is measured relative to its 1973--2026 average, so $\rs$ matters through its movements over time.

The transformation matters for levels and not for the difference between the two readings. The ratio of the mean absolute difference to the mean absolute market move in 12-month changes, 1996--2026, is \bl{dz:12m:nom} (interval \bl{dz:12m:nom:lo} to \bl{dz:12m:nom:hi}) with nominal assets and \bl{dz:12m:ratio} relative to $\rs$.

\textbf{Data.} All series are monthly averages from January 1973 to September 2026, from the public sources in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The BBB spread is Moody's Baa yield less the 10-year yield. Months in which the CPI and unemployment releases are missing (October 2025) are interpolated. The unemployment gap is the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate, placed in the middle month of each quarter and interpolated. Expected inflation is the Cleveland Fed's estimate at one and ten years from 1982 and the Livingston Survey's 12-month CPI expectation before, shifted to the Cleveland level by the mean difference over 1982--84 and blended over 1982. CAPE is Shiller's series spliced over 1990--92 in logs to the monthly average of a daily series. The real broad dollar is spliced in 2006 in logs, as are the nominal dollar and the S\&P 500.

\textbf{Market index.} Policy news is the first principal component of MP1, MP2, ED2, ED3 and ED4 in the monetary-event windows, scaled by its regression on the two-year yield change so that one unit moves the two-year yield by one percentage point. Unscheduled events with no change in MP1 and March--April 2020 are dropped. Each of the 10-year yield change, the S\&P 500 return and the dollar (DXY) return in the window is regressed on the factor. The residual covariance $\Sigma$ of the three regressions gives $w=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$. The S\&P 500 and DXY weights apply to 100 times the log of CAPE and of the real broad dollar, as percent changes in the window and in the index levels agree to first order. The weights bootstrap resamples announcements with replacement and recomputes the policy-news factor and the weights each time; draws use the seed in \texttt{lib/config.py}.

\textbf{FRB/US index.} The weights are the cumulative GDP responses at four quarters to a persistent unit shift in each variable, simulated in FRB/US with VAR-based expectations, divided by the response to a persistent one percentage point rise in the funds rate. The weight on the policy rate is its direct effect with expectations of future rates held fixed. House prices enter as 100 times the log of the price-to-rent ratio relative to its trailing 15-year average. The expenditure decomposition of Figure~\ref{fig:channels} uses the model's own identities for consumption, residential and business fixed investment, inventories, government, exports and imports, weighted by their shares in GDP, with a residual below $10^{-7}$ percentage points; it is run with the Board's FRB/US package and the data vintage of October 2026, downloaded by step 00 into a gitignored directory, with a Python 3.11 environment built on first use. The policy-rate experiment shocks the inertial-Taylor-rule equation in the first quarter and is scaled (to \bl{ps:size} units of the add factor) so that the average simulated funds rate over eight quarters rises by one point; GDP at four quarters is linear in the size of the shock to within \bl{ps:lin} percent. For the decay experiments of Section~\ref{sec:persist}, each variable's shock decays geometrically with the half-life stated, and the response is computed in the model by linearity from the temporary-shock responses.

\textbf{Policy-news definitions.} The two-year definition replaces the futures factor by the change in the two-year Treasury yield in the same window, with the same regressions and the same bootstrap of announcements; the draws for different definitions are paired by keeping, for each resample, the announcements that fall in each subsample, so the subsample size varies by draw. The announcement bootstrap uses 5,000 draws for every weights-based interval; the block bootstraps of the VAR and the outcome regressions use 500. The FRB/US weights are held fixed.

\textbf{Forecasting and survey data.} The regressions use local projections of the 12-month-ahead change in the unemployment gap (four-quarter-ahead GDP growth) with HAC standard errors of 18 lags. The Survey of Professional Forecasters data are the median forecasts, 1990Q3--2025Q3, matched to the financial data up to the month before each survey's deadline.

\section{Inference and the Decomposition of the Levels}\label{app:differ}
\subsection{How Precisely Are the Weights Estimated?}\label{sec:precision}
The weights come from regressions on \bl{n:events} announcements. The weight on the 10-year yield has a 90 percent interval from \bl{wb:ten:lo} to \bl{wb:ten:hi} around \bl{w:ten}. The interval for equity valuation runs from \bl{wb:eq:lo} to \bl{wb:eq:hi}, and for the dollar from \bl{wb:usd:lo} to \bl{wb:usd:hi}. The FRB/US weights on all three lie inside these intervals, the equity weight near the edge: it is lower in absolute value than \bl{frw:eq:pct} percent of the bootstrap draws, about 14 percent in a two-sided test. The data do not reject that the model weights equal the market weights. This is a statement about power, not a finding that they are equal, and it treats the FRB/US weights as fixed. The intervals in the text come from the bootstrap of the announcements; the contrast in composition in Section~\ref{sec:persist} is the ratio of ratios, whose interval includes one at two quarters.

\subsection{The Policy-Rate Experiment in FRB/US}\label{app:policyrate}
A policy shock in the model gives a comparison with the announcement response. I shock the equation of the inertial Taylor rule in the first quarter only, with the rule active afterwards and expectations from the model's VAR, and I scale the shock so that the average simulated funds rate over the next eight quarters rises by one point. FRB/US has no two-year yield, so that average is my proxy for the unit of policy news. Per unit of the proxy, the 10-year yield rises \bl{ps:q1:ten} times, equity wealth falls \bl{ps:q1:eq} times and the dollar rises \bl{ps:q1:usd} times as much as in the announcement response. GDP four quarters later falls by \bl{ps:gdp} percent, a funds-rate equivalent of \bl{ps:ffr}, against \bl{ps:replay} for the usual announcement response. Priced along their paths, the 10-year yield, equities and the dollar account for \bl{ps:pr:ten}, \bl{ps:pr:eq} and \bl{ps:pr:usd} percent of the GDP effect of the model's policy shock, against \bl{ch:replay:ten}, \bl{ch:replay:eq} and \bl{ch:replay:usd} percent for the announcement response, and \bl{ps:rem} percent is left in the policy rate, spreads and house prices. The model's own policy transmission is heavier in equities and lighter in the dollar than the announcement response, and the difference in the funds-rate equivalent, \bl{ps:ffr} against \bl{ps:replay}, is the residual between the two experiments. The comparison has limits. The funds-rate average is a proxy, and a different proxy changes the unit. The equity fall on impact is large because stock wealth in FRB/US follows expected funds rates through the required return. The exercise is the model's own policy shock, not a reproduction of an announcement. The reading of about one in Section~\ref{sec:onannounce} is therefore not the statement that announcements move asset prices as a policy shock does in the model; it is a statement about the combined effect on GDP of different mixes.

\subsection{Limits of the Design}\label{sec:limits}
The weights come from announcements since 1996 and are applied from 1973; they assume that the responses reflect policy rather than information effects \citep{nakamura2018}, and if policy came to target financial conditions the responses could change, the Lucas-critique concern that \citet{hatzius2010} raise for all indexes. The FRB/US weights come from one model, at one horizon and in one experiment, and are held fixed in the intervals. The daily comparison of Section~\ref{sec:size} uses nominal assets, with the nominal broad dollar for the real dollar, no adjustment for $\rs$ and weights fitted on the same announcements; the intraday comparison holds each event out. The sample has five recessions, two of them out of sample, and the 12-month outcomes overlap, so the counts of tests are descriptive and not adjusted for their number. The neutral rate is not neutral to outcomes, because the Laubach--Williams model uses output, inflation and unemployment.
