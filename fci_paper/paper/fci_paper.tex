\begin{abstract}
When can a movement in financial conditions be read as a movement in monetary policy? Two calibrations give an answer. One weights asset prices by how they respond to monetary policy news in announcement windows. The other weights them by their effects on GDP in the FRB/US model. For the combination of asset prices that follows a policy surprise, the two readings are close once they are on a common scale. For other combinations they differ. In point estimates the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does under every definition of policy news that I try. The size of the contrast depends on that definition; common assumptions about persistence change it little. The difference between the readings is roughly 0.2 to 0.4 of the average market move, at horizons from intraday to a year. In 1998--2001 the two readings of the same asset movements had opposite signs in the point estimates, both small, and the dollar was the main source of the difference. In 2022--24 both readings were about one point of tightening. Realized unemployment and aggregate GDP growth do not separate the two readings. Composition predicts the net-export component of growth in the main sample, as the model's channels imply, but not significantly after 1996.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Suppose the Federal Reserve leaves the policy rate unchanged, but the dollar appreciates and stock prices fall. Both movements tend to restrain spending. How much tightening has occurred? One way to answer is to ask how large a monetary policy surprise would ordinarily produce a comparable movement in financial markets. Another is to ask how large a change in the policy rate would have a comparable effect on spending. A policymaker might reasonably expect the two answers to be close.

Whether they are close depends on which asset prices have moved. Around a typical monetary policy announcement, long yields, stock prices and the dollar move together in fairly predictable proportions. The two methods give similar readings for that combination. But suppose the tightening comes mainly through the dollar. In the Federal Reserve's model, an appreciation of the dollar has a larger effect on spending than its usual response to policy news would suggest. An equity-led tightening is the converse case. The distinction matters whenever policymakers use the familiar effects of their own announcements to interpret financial developments between meetings.

Figure~\ref{fig:two} gives an example. In one case equity valuations fall by 5 log points. In the other the dollar appreciates by 3.9 log points. I chose the two sizes so that the first method rates them alike. By the first method, which weights asset prices by how they respond to policy news, each is worth about a quarter of a point of policy tightening (\bl{ag:ex:eq:mkt} and \bl{ag:ex:usd:mkt} points of the two-year yield). By the second, which weights them by their effects on GDP in the FRB/US model, the dollar movement is worth \bl{ag:h4:fr} times the equity movement.

Policymakers read the stance of policy from financial conditions. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}, and in March 2024 that ``there are many different financial conditions indicators, and you can kind of, you know, see different answers to that question'' \citep{powell2024}. A reading of financial conditions is a reading of policy only to the extent that a point of the index means a point of policy. This paper asks when two calibrations of that meaning agree, how large the disagreement is, whether it would have changed the reading of history, and whether later activity favors either one.

I find that the two calibrations agree on the movement that policy itself produces and part ways on the combinations that it does not. On the typical response of long yields, equities and the dollar to a policy surprise, the model prices the response at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} funds-rate points with persistent paths (interval \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi}), against one by construction for the event study. Rescaling the model's weights by that number puts the two readings on one scale. The disagreement lies in composition. In point estimates the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does under every definition of policy news that I try. The size of the contrast depends on the definition: the ratio of the model's dollar-to-equity ratio to the market's is \bl{sg:base:ror} for the baseline futures factor and \bl{sg:two:ror} when policy news is the change in the two-year yield in the same windows, and only the baseline interval excludes one. Assumptions about decay that are common to both assets change it little. In the data the difference between the two readings is roughly 0.2 to 0.4 of the average market move, at horizons from intraday to a year. History shows what the difference can look like. In 1998--2001 the point estimates of the two readings of the same asset movements had opposite signs, both small, and the dollar was the main source of the difference. In 2022--24 both readings were about one point of tightening, and both forecasts of unemployment exceeded the rise. Aggregate unemployment and GDP growth do not separate the two readings in the main sample. Composition does predict the net-export contribution to growth there, as the model's channels imply, but not significantly after 1996.

The paper has five parts. First, I define what a policy equivalent is under each method and show why their weights differ (Section~\ref{sec:indexes}). Second, I estimate the weights, show how the model's weights arise from expenditure channels, show where the two readings agree, and show how the assumed persistence of asset prices and the definition of policy news change the comparison (Section~\ref{sec:agree}). Third, I measure the disagreement on announcements and at horizons from a day to a year (Section~\ref{sec:size}). Fourth, I examine three historical episodes (Section~\ref{sec:history}). Finally, I ask whether subsequent activity favors either reading (Section~\ref{sec:outlook}). The appendices give the derivations, the construction of the series and the sensitivity of the results.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Weights and their meaning.} Indexes of financial conditions differ mainly in the source of their weights \citep{hatzius2010}: effects on output from a model \citep{dudley2000,hatzius2018,ajello2023}, principal components \citep{brave2011}, or event-study regressions \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005,bernanke2005,nakamura2018,bauer2023}. The older literature on monetary conditions indexes recognized that the weights on asset prices depend on the shock and the dynamics, so that their policy meaning is not invariant \citep{eika1996}. I quantify that difficulty with two observed sets of U.S.\ weights and ask where it matters.

\textbf{The closest comparisons.} \citet{arnaut2024} estimate how an existing index responds to policy surprises, \citet{sack2023} relate FCI-G to policy-induced changes in the two-year yield, and \citet{ajello2023} translate movements into a growth impulse with model multipliers. \citet{bletzinger2026} build a U.S.\ macro-finance index in units comparable to a policy rate and compare it with the Goldman Sachs index and FCI-G, finding divergences driven by equities and the strongest tightening in 2022--24. I ask how a policy-news weighting and a macro weighting read the same movements. \citet{boehm2025} show that announcements also move equities and exchange rates along a dimension orthogonal to yield changes, so a movement that departs from the conventional pattern can come from the announcement itself.

\textbf{Information about activity.} \citet{hatzius2010} build an index conditional on past activity and inflation, \citet{aramonte2013} show that comparisons among indexes depend on the crisis in the sample, and \citet{lombardi2025} separate safe yields from priced risk and find that risk predicts activity more strongly. \citet{adrian2019} show that conditions shift the lower quantiles of growth, and \citet{brave2011} build an index of risk, credit and leverage. \citet{caballero2026} define FCI$^*$, the level of financial conditions consistent with output at potential. The neutral rate matters for the level of every index \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}; I measure the inputs relative to $\rs$ and do not estimate a neutral level for the index (Appendix~\ref{app:steps}).
\textbf{Why the meaning of a point matters.} \citet{caballero2024} study targeting an index of financial conditions in a closed economy, and \citet{caballero2026b} extend the analysis to a multi-asset open economy, in which domestic asset prices and the exchange rate both enter aggregate demand and policy responses link the markets. That gives a reason to care which mix of asset prices delivers a given index reading. My question comes one step earlier: when does a portfolio inferred from conventional policy news give those markets the same relative importance as a spending calibration? The answer does not require endorsing targeting. The same ambiguity arises for anyone who communicates about a desired path of financial conditions, since the path has to say what a point on it means, and for a neutral benchmark such as FCI$^*$ \citep{caballero2026}, which takes the aggregation of asset movements as given.

\section{Two Ways to Read a Financial Movement}\label{sec:indexes}
\subsection{Two Meanings of a Policy Equivalent}\label{sec:meanings}
Take a movement in three asset prices: the 10-year Treasury yield, equity valuation and the real broad dollar. Each is measured relative to $r^*$ and to its 1973--2026 average (Appendix~\ref{app:steps}). I call a reading of the movement a policy equivalent if it says how much tightening of the policy rate the movement amounts to. There are two natural ways to compute one.

The market index starts from policy. At each Federal Open Market Committee announcement I measure the policy surprise with the factor of \citet{nakamura2018}, scaled so that one unit raises the two-year yield by one percentage point, and I measure how the three asset prices respond in a narrow window around the announcement. The weights are chosen to recover the size of the surprise from the three asset prices together. A movement is then worth the size of the surprise that would, on average, produce it, in points of the two-year yield. A policy surprise of one unit reads one. I call this the market-implied reading.

The FRB/US index starts from spending. Each asset price is weighted by its effect on GDP four quarters later in the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model \citep{brayton2014}, in the experiment in which the asset price shifts persistently, divided by the effect of a persistent one percentage point rise in the federal funds rate. A movement is then worth the funds-rate change with the same effect on GDP, in funds-rate points. I call this the spending-equivalent reading.

The first calculation reads a movement through what policy does to markets. The second reads it through what markets do to spending. Both are legitimate. They need not agree.

\subsection{Why the Weights Differ}\label{sec:why}
Consider the dollar. It can be a reliable indicator of a policy surprise because it responds consistently when the expected path of U.S.\ interest rates changes. How useful it is as an indicator depends on how much it also moves for other reasons, and on what yields and equities already reveal. Its weight in a spending index depends on different things: how an appreciation affects net exports, and how long the appreciation lasts. An asset can be an excellent indicator of a policy surprise and have a modest effect on spending. It can also be a noisy indicator and have a large effect on spending. The same is true of equities, whose effect on spending runs through wealth and the cost of capital. A fall in equity prices that reflects a higher discount rate is policy-like, and a fall that reflects weaker earnings prospects is not policy at all. The event study averages over announcements, where the first kind dominates. The model prices an equity movement by its estimated effect on spending, whatever its source. Neither calculation is wrong about its own question.

Two equations make the argument precise. Let $x$ be the movement in the three asset prices around an announcement, $s$ the policy surprise, and $\psi$ the vector of responses of the asset prices to a unit of surprise:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
x=\psi s+u .
\end{equation}
The vector $u$ collects the ways in which a particular movement departs from the usual response to conventional rate news. It has covariance $\Sigma$. Even an announcement can produce such a departure. The market weights $w_M$ recover $s$ from $x$ by generalized least squares, $w_M\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$, normalized so that $w_M'\psi=1$. The FRB/US weights $b_h$ are the effects of the three asset prices on GDP at horizon $h$ in funds-rate points. The model prices the usual response $\psi$ at $b_h'\psi$, which I report in its own units, and dividing by it gives the normalized weights $w_{B,h}=b_h/(b_h'\psi)$, so that both indexes read one on a conventional policy surprise. The difference between the two readings of a movement $x$ is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'u ,
\end{equation}
because the policy part $\psi s$ cancels. The same difference has a second reading. The fitted policy component of any movement $x$ is $\psi M$, where $M=w_M'x$ is the market-implied reading; the remainder $x-\psi M$ is the combination of asset prices that a conventional policy surprise cannot reproduce. Once the scales are matched, the spending-equivalent valuation of that remainder is precisely the difference between the two readings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rem}
D_h(x)=w_{B,h}'(x-\psi M),
\end{equation}
because $w_{B,h}'\psi=1$. A reader can say what the difference measures without assigning it a structural cause. In words, the two methods disagree only to the extent that asset prices move in unusual proportions and the methods value those departures differently. An unusual movement is not enough by itself: if the weights are the same, there is nothing to value differently. A difference in weights is not enough either: if the asset prices keep moving together as they do around policy news, the difference has nothing to act on. That is why two indexes can track each other closely for decades and still give different readings during an equity boom or a dollar surge. Throughout, $D$ is the difference computed on the movements of the three asset prices over the horizon in question, and in Section~\ref{sec:outlook} it is computed on 12-month changes. Appendix~\ref{app:ma} derives the weights.

\subsection{Putting Numbers on It}\label{sec:requires}
Figure~\ref{fig:two} and Table~\ref{tab:example} (Appendix~\ref{app:differ}) apply both sets of weights to two movements. The market index reads the two alike, as the weights imply that a 5 log point fall in equity valuation and a 3.9 log point appreciation of the dollar are each about a quarter of a policy move. The FRB/US index gives the dollar movement \bl{ag:h4:fr} times the effect of the equity movement. The ratio of ratios, the model's dollar-to-equity ratio over the market's, is \bl{ag:h4:rr} (interval \bl{ag:h4:rr:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:rr:hi}). Neither movement is a policy surprise, so nothing in the construction requires the readings to agree. Whether the difference is large in practice depends on how often asset prices move in such proportions, which is the question of Section~\ref{sec:size}.

\section{Why the Two Answers Agree on a Policy Move}\label{sec:agree}
\subsection{The Estimated Weights}\label{sec:market}
Table~\ref{tab:weights} gives the market weights. A policy move that raises the two-year yield by one point raises the 10-year yield by \bl{psi:ten} points, lowers equity valuation by \bl{abs:psi:eq} percent and raises the real dollar by \bl{psi:usd} percent. The data are the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}: \bl{n:events} announcements from \bl{event:first} to \bl{event:last}. Each variable carries a substantial part of the policy move, with shares of \bl{share:ten} for the 10-year yield, \bl{share:eq} for equity valuation and \bl{share:usd} for the dollar. The weights are imprecise, the weight on the 10-year yield most of all (Appendix~\ref{sec:precision}).

\input{../replication/report/tab_weights}

The weights say what the variables do when policy moves, not what causes their movements at other times. A rise in stock prices that comes from news about earnings has little to do with policy, and the index treats it as if it did. An announcement can also reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and then the responses mix policy and information \citep{nakamura2018}. I measure the responses as markets receive them and set the information effect aside. The index measures policy-equivalent tightening. It is not a forecast of activity.

Table~\ref{tab:frbus} compares the market weights with the FRB/US weights. The two sets agree on the 10-year yield, where the FRB/US weight is \bl{ratio:ten} times the market weight. The model puts less weight on equity valuation (\bl{ratio:eq} times) and more on the dollar (\bl{ratio:usd} times). On a policy move the difference does not matter, because the policy part cancels in equation~(\ref{eq:D}). On a departure from the usual response, the model gives a fall in equity prices its estimated effect on output, and the market index treats it as policy-equivalent.

\subsection{What Lies behind the Model's Weights}\label{sec:channels}
The FRB/US weights are effects on GDP, and a reader should see where they come from. Figure~\ref{fig:channels} follows the two movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two} through the model under one persistence assumption, a persistent shift. Only the stated variable moves. The policy rate, the Treasury curve, the mortgage and car-loan rates, the BBB spread, equity valuation, household stock wealth and the dollar are held at baseline in every experiment except their own, so the policy rule is inactive and expectations are those of the model's VAR. The GDP object is the percent deviation of real GDP from baseline. The contributions are the model's own expenditure identities, weighted by shares in GDP, and they sum to GDP.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../replication/report/fig_channels.pdf}
\caption{How the Two Movements Reach GDP in FRB/US}
\label{fig:channels}
\begin{flushleft}\footnotesize\textit{Notes:} Percent deviation of real GDP from baseline and contributions by expenditure component, persistent shift in the variable, other financial variables held at baseline, policy rule inactive. Investment is residential and business fixed investment; net exports are exports less imports. The figure uses the current Board data package (LONGBASE of October 2026), whose four-quarter responses are within 2 percent of those behind the weights in Table~\ref{tab:frbus}.\end{flushleft}
\end{figure}

The equity movement reduces GDP by \bl{ch:gdp:eq} percent after four quarters, and domestic demand accounts for it. Consumption supplies \bl{ch:eq:cons} percent of the effect, through wealth and the cost of capital, and business fixed investment \bl{ch:eq:bfi} percent. Domestic demand in all supplies \bl{ch:eq:dom} percent, and net exports offset \bl{abs:ch:eq:nx} percent of it, because weaker demand reduces imports; inventories supply 13 percent and government $-1$ percent, so that the shares sum to 100. The dollar movement reduces GDP by \bl{ch:gdp:usd} percent, and net exports account for \bl{ch:usd:nx} percent of it: exports \bl{ch:usd:exp} percent and imports \bl{ch:usd:imp} percent. Domestic demand contributes \bl{ch:usd:dom} percent, inventories 13 percent and government $-7$ percent of the GDP effect. With a half-life of four quarters the shares are similar (consumption \bl{ch:eq:cons4} percent of the equity effect and net exports \bl{ch:usd:nx4} percent of the dollar effect). The two weights therefore reflect two different channels, and the dollar's larger weight is a statement about trade. It carries no implication about how persistent the dollar is, which is the assumption of the experiment.

The channels also show why the usual response to policy news is not a good guide to the model's own policy transmission. A persistent one-point rise in the funds rate in the model moves the 10-year yield, equity wealth and the dollar in proportions that differ from the announcement responses. Pricing each at its effect on GDP at four quarters, the 10-year yield, equities and the dollar account for \bl{ch:replay:ten}, \bl{ch:replay:eq} and \bl{ch:replay:usd} percent of the GDP effect of the usual announcement response, and for \bl{ch:hike:ten}, \bl{ch:hike:eq} and \bl{ch:hike:usd} percent of the effect of the model's own hike, with the remainder in the policy rate and other channels. The model's own policy transmission is heavier in equities and lighter in the dollar than the announcement response. The reading of about one in Section~\ref{sec:onannounce} is therefore not the statement that announcements move asset prices as a rate hike does in the model; it is a statement about the combined effect on GDP of different mixes. I do not build a policy experiment that reproduces the announcement path in the model, because the model's expectations do not identify one; the comparison is a description of two sets of asset movements, not a decomposition of a residual.

\subsection{The FRB/US Reading of a Policy Move}\label{sec:onannounce}
The model prices the usual response of the three variables to a unit of policy news at \bl{ag:h2:bpsi}, \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} and \bl{ag:h8:bpsi} funds-rate points at two, four and eight quarters (Table~\ref{tab:agree}). At four quarters the 90 percent interval is \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:lo} to \bl{ag:h4:bpsi:hi}. The market index prices the same response at exactly one. A value near one is a notable coincidence because nothing in the construction of the FRB/US weights refers to policy announcements. They are the effects on GDP of persistent shifts in each variable. Had the usual response to a policy surprise been mostly a dollar movement, or mostly an equity movement, the model could have priced it far above or below one. That it prices it near one means that the event study and the model describe the same policy move with about the same strength. This is the empirical content of agreement: two calibrations built for different purposes, one from announcement windows and one from a macroeconomic model, assign nearly the same number of funds-rate points to the same movement in asset prices. The two do not differ significantly at any horizon with persistent paths; with decaying paths the model's reading is lower (Section~\ref{sec:persist}).

The units differ. A unit of the market signal raises the two-year yield by one percentage point on impact. A unit of the model experiment is a persistent one-point rise in the funds rate. The number \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} is a cross-experiment conversion, and its closeness to one is an empirical coincidence in the chosen units. It does not establish that the two policy paths have the same effect on GDP. Dividing the model's weights by it puts both indexes on one scale and makes them agree exactly along the policy direction. That is an identity and not a finding. The finding is the number before it was divided out.

\subsection{Composition or Persistence?}\label{sec:persist}
The comparison confounds two things. A persistent dollar appreciation in FRB/US and the dollar's response to policy news are different experiments, so a difference in weights could reflect the initial composition of the movement or the path that follows it. Figure~\ref{fig:persist} separates them with the same set of calculations under common decaying paths for the three variables, with half-lives of 8, 4 and 2 quarters.

Persistence changes how much tightening a policy move represents. The model's reading of the usual response falls from \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} with persistent paths to \bl{ag:bp:hl4} (interval \bl{ag:bp:hl4:lo} to \bl{ag:bp:hl4:hi}) with a half-life of four quarters and \bl{ag:bp:hl2} with a half-life of two. Persistence does not change which movement counts more. With the same decay for the dollar and for equities, the model's dollar-to-equity ratio is \bl{ag:mp:hl8}, \bl{ag:mp:hl4} and \bl{ag:mp:hl2} at half-lives of 8, 4 and 2 quarters, against \bl{ag:mkt:ratio} for the market weights (interval \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:lo} to \bl{ag:mkt:ratio:hi}). Different half-lives for the two assets give a wider range, \bl{ag:mp:min} to \bl{ag:mp:max}, and none near one. The contrast between the two weightings is a contrast in the effect on GDP per unit of each asset price. The definition of policy news, taken up next, moves the contrast by an amount at least as large.

\subsection{What Counts as Policy News?}\label{sec:signal}
The market weights are designed to recover a specified signal, so the definition of policy news is a second measurement choice, and persistence does not capture it. The baseline signal is the futures-based factor of \citet{nakamura2018}. A natural alternative is the change in the two-year Treasury yield in the same announcement windows, since the index is quoted in two-year-yield points. Table~\ref{tab:signal} re-estimates the market weights with each definition, using the same 253 announcements for the first two, and also with the factor through 2019 and outside the lower-bound years. The model weights are held fixed and normalized again for each definition.

\input{../replication/report/tab_signal}

\textbf{Direction survives and size does not.} The model's dollar-to-equity ratio exceeds the market's under all four definitions. The ratio of ratios is \bl{sg:base:ror} for the baseline, \bl{sg:two:ror} for the two-year yield on the same announcements (interval \bl{sg:two:ror:lo} to \bl{sg:two:ror:hi}), \bl{sg:t19:ror} through 2019 and \bl{sg:nlb:ror} without the lower-bound years. On the same announcements and the same bootstrap draws, the ratio is smaller under the two-year signal than under the factor in 94 percent of the draws, with a difference of \bl{sg:pair:two:ror} (\bl{sg:pair:two:ror:lo} to \bl{sg:pair:two:ror:hi}). The statement that the model gives the dollar more than twice the relative weight is therefore specific to the futures factor. Because the ratio's denominator, the market's equity effect, is small and imprecise, I also report the contrast in points. The model's dollar-minus-equity effect for the two movements of Figure~\ref{fig:two} is about a quarter of a point under every definition (\bl{sg:base:cf} for the baseline; the normalization changes slightly with the signal), and the market's is \bl{sg:base:cm} for the baseline and \bl{sg:two:cm} for the two-year yield. The model less market contrast is \bl{sg:base:contrast:diff} (\bl{sg:base:contrast:diff:lo} to \bl{sg:base:contrast:diff:hi}) for the baseline and between \bl{sg:two:contrast:diff} and \bl{sg:t19:contrast:diff} across definitions.

\textbf{Why the equity weight falls.} The two signals are close in the announcement windows, with a correlation of \bl{sig:corr} between them, but \bl{sig:res} percent of the standard deviation of the two-year change lies outside the factor. That part moves the 10-year yield and the dollar and not equities. Regressing each asset on both signals, equity valuation loads on the factor ($t=$ \bl{sig:t:eq:ns}) and not on the two-year yield ($t=$ \bl{sig:t:eq:2y}), while the 10-year yield and the dollar load on the two-year yield ($t=$ \bl{sig:t:ten:2y} and \bl{sig:t:usd:2y}). The equity weight rises from \bl{sg:base:weq} to \bl{sg:two:weq} when the signal changes. Switching the response vector and the residual covariance in turn, each accounts for about half of the change (\bl{sig:cf:psi} and \bl{sig:cf:sig}), and the order does not matter. I read this as saying that equities respond to the longer-horizon content of the futures factor, and that the two-year yield carries near-term and maturity-specific variation that moves yields and the dollar. This is an interpretation. The regressions establish what variation each signal captures; they do not establish whether the two-year part is noise or news, and a yield has measurement error and maturity-specific news that the factor, a combination of five futures, averages away.

\textbf{What carries through.} The disagreement is similar under every definition: the ratio of the mean absolute difference to the mean absolute market move in 12-month changes is \bl{sg:base:d12}, \bl{sg:two:d12}, \bl{sg:t19:d12} and \bl{sg:nlb:d12}. The 1998--2001 readings have opposite signs under all four definitions, with the dollar contributing more to the difference than equities (Table~\ref{tab:signal}). In 2022--24 both readings are above one point of tightening under all four, and they are equal to within 0.05 under the baseline and through 2019. Under the two-year yield the market-implied reading is \bl{sg:two:22m} and the spending-equivalent reading \bl{sg:two:22f}, and without the lower-bound years \bl{sg:nlb:22m} and \bl{sg:nlb:22f}. That difference comes from the 10-year yield, which the market weights price at 0.93 to 0.99 against about 0.6 in the normalized model weights, and not from equities and the dollar. The equity and dollar offset of Section~\ref{sec:history} is therefore specific to the futures-factor definitions.

\textbf{A check on the spending side.} The multipliers of the Board's FCI-G offer a check, with the caveat that they share a lineage with FRB/US, so agreement measures robustness within a family. Per 1 percent, the FCI-G dollar multiplier is \bl{fg:fcig} times its equity multiplier, against \bl{fg:frbus} in FRB/US. The ratio of ratios against the market is \bl{fg:base} under the baseline signal and \bl{fg:two} under the two-year yield. FCI-G corroborates the direction under the futures factors and not under the two-year yield. A genuinely independent check would use another model with documented asset-specific multipliers.

\section{How Much Do the Two Readings Disagree in Practice?}\label{sec:size}
The disagreement depends on how often asset prices move in proportions other than the usual response. I measure it as the mean absolute value of $D$ divided by the mean absolute value of the market move, over the same windows. A ratio of 0.3 means that the typical difference between the two readings is a third of the typical move.

On the 253 announcements, with the weights re-estimated without the event being evaluated, the ratio in intraday windows is \bl{dz:ev:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:ev:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:ev:ratio:hi}), and the 90th percentile of the difference is \bl{dz:ev:p90} against a mean absolute market move of \bl{dz:ev:move}. These are the windows in which the weights are estimated, with each event held out.

The ratio is larger in daily data than in the intraday windows and does not shrink at longer horizons. On daily changes of the same three assets, 1996--2026, it is \bl{dl:k1:ann} on announcement days and \bl{dl:k1:oth} on other days; at one week it is \bl{dl:k5:ann} and \bl{dl:k5:oth}, and at 22 trading days \bl{dl:k22:ann} and \bl{dl:k22:oth} (Table~\ref{tab:daily}). No difference between announcement and other windows is distinguishable from zero. Announcement days contain a great deal of financial news besides the announcement. The fitted response to policy news is a particular combination of asset prices, and the difference over a whole announcement day looks like the difference on other days.

\input{../replication/report/tab_daily}

For windows that end on any day, the ratio is \bl{dl:lad1}, \bl{dl:lad5}, \bl{dl:lad22}, \bl{dl:lad66} and \bl{dl:lad252} at 1, 5, 22, 66 and 252 trading days. A window of 22 or more days contains announcements in any case; removing the moves on announcement days gives \bl{dl:ex1}, \bl{dl:ex5}, \bl{dl:ex22}, \bl{dl:ex66} and \bl{dl:ex252}, within 0.02 of those. In 12-month changes of the monthly series, the ratio is \bl{dz:12m:ratio} (interval \bl{dz:12m:ratio:lo} to \bl{dz:12m:ratio:hi}), and the difference exceeds 0.25 in \bl{dz:12m:sh25} percent of months (Figure~\ref{fig:D}). The months overlap, so runs in this series are not independent evidence of persistence. The statistic is a sample summary with wide intervals. Roughly 0.25 to 0.33 describes the baseline across horizons from a day to a year, and the ratio is 0.36 to 0.42 under the other definitions of policy news (Table~\ref{tab:signal}).

The intraday and daily figures do not isolate a source for the gap between 0.19 and 0.3. The intraday windows are narrow and the weights exclude the event evaluated. The daily comparison uses nominal assets and weights fitted on the same announcements (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The comparison across horizons is the cleaner result: the disagreement does not shrink as the window lengthens.

\section{Would a Policymaker Have Read History Differently?}\label{sec:history}
Three episodes show when the choice of weights matters. All readings are retrospective estimates, made with weights estimated on the full sample. For each episode I report two comparisons that are easily confused. The first is the difference between the full FRB/US index and the market index in average level, which includes variables the market index does not have and the effect of centering each variable on its average. The second applies the two weightings to the same three assets in 12-month changes, which isolates the weights. A gap in levels can coexist with agreement in changes. Table~\ref{tab:epmain} gives both. Figure~\ref{fig:episodes} shows the matched episode averages with 90 percent intervals from the bootstrap of the announcements, and Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows the readings month by month and the contributions of each asset to their difference.

The difference of \bl{ce:1998:D} (\bl{ce:1998:D:lo} to \bl{ce:1998:D:hi}) is a point estimate; its interval includes zero. The signs differ in the averages and in \bl{ce:1998:opp} of the \bl{ce:1998:nmo} months. The monthly paths show what the average conceals (Figure~\ref{fig:paths}). Through 1998 and 1999 the difference was positive and reached about 0.7 points, because the dollar was appreciating and valuations were rising, and the model gives the dollar more weight and equities less. In 2001, as valuations fell, the difference turned negative. The small average comes from a boom and a reversal. An observer following the two readings in 1998--99 would have disagreed about the direction of the change by a few tenths of a point for a sustained period, and in the opposite direction in 2001. The dollar appreciated by \bl{ce:1998:xusd} log points a year on average, which adds \bl{ce:1998:cusd} to $D$; equity valuations rose by \bl{ce:1998:xeq} log points a year, which adds \bl{ce:1998:ceq}. Unemployment rose by \bl{ce:1998:real} points over the following year, against forecasts of \bl{ce:1998:fcm} from the market-implied reading and \bl{ce:1998:fcf} from the spending-equivalent reading.

The full indexes differ much more in levels. The FRB/US index read \bl{br:ep1998:gap} points tighter than the market index on average (interval \bl{br:ep1998:lo} to \bl{br:ep1998:hi}), and \bl{br:ep1998:eq} of that difference comes from the equity weights. Valuations were far above their average, and the market index, which gives each point of valuation more weight, read conditions as easier. The level and the change are different objects. The level difference is the weights applied to a persistent departure of valuations from their average; the difference in annual changes is the weights applied to what moved that year. Equities explain the first and the dollar explains most of the second.

\textbf{2022--24.} The two readings of the 12-month change averaged \bl{ce:2022:dm} points (interval \bl{ce:2022:dm:lo} to \bl{ce:2022:dm:hi}) and \bl{ce:2022:fr} (\bl{ce:2022:fr:lo} to \bl{ce:2022:fr:hi}). Because the model gives the dollar more weight, the appreciation makes the spending-equivalent reading tighter, and contributes \bl{ce:2022:cusd}. Both weightings overpredicted the rise, so the weights do not explain the miss.

\section{Can Subsequent Activity Tell the Weightings Apart?}\label{sec:outlook}
\subsection{The Test}\label{sec:onetest}
Consider two years in which the market index tightened by the same amount. If the dollar accounted for more of the tightening in one year, did activity subsequently weaken more? If the market-implied reading is a sufficient summary of the asset prices, the answer is no. If the spending-equivalent reading is, the answer is yes, by the amount that the model's weights imply. I regress the 12-month-ahead change in the unemployment gap on own lags and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test}
y_{t+12}-y_t=\cdots+\beta M_t+\gamma D_t ,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the 12-month change in the market index (relative to $r^*$) and $D$ the 12-month change of the normalized FRB/US-minus-market difference on the same changes. If the market-implied reading is sufficient, $\gamma=0$. If the spending-equivalent reading $M+D$ is sufficient, $\gamma=\beta$. I read the two restrictions as tests of incremental predictive sufficiency in the span of the two portfolios, conditional on the controls. Three assets span a larger space, and the unrestricted three-asset regression of Section~\ref{sec:eqalone} is a different test. Failure to separate the portfolios for aggregate unemployment or GDP is consistent with low power and with forward-looking asset prices, and it cannot validate either structural weight. That wording matters for unemployment, since the FRB/US weights are constructed from effects on GDP; four-quarter GDP growth is the model-facing check. This is the only primary test, and Table~\ref{tab:comp} reports both restrictions with the joint covariance of the estimates.

\subsection{The Answer}\label{sec:answer}
On 1976--2026 the coefficient on the index change is \bl{rs:du:main:beta} and on composition \bl{rs:du:main:gamma} (\bl{rs:du:main:gamma:lo} to \bl{rs:du:main:gamma:hi}); the difference $\gamma-\beta$ is \bl{rs:du:main:gmb} (\bl{rs:du:main:gmb:lo} to \bl{rs:du:main:gmb:hi}). Neither restriction is rejected. Moving $D$ from its 5th to its 95th percentile at the same index change shifts the year-ahead outlook by \bl{cm:du:main:eff} percentage point, with an interval from \bl{cm:du:main:efflo} to \bl{cm:du:main:effhi}, so the sample cannot establish an outlook difference of either sign. On 1996--2026 the coefficient on composition is \bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma} (\bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma:lo} to \bl{rs:du:1996p:gamma:hi}) and the model's restriction is rejected ($\gamma-\beta=$ \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb}, \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb:lo} to \bl{rs:du:1996p:gmb:hi}); excluding 2008--15 the coefficient on composition is \bl{rs:du:ex200815:gamma} and $\gamma-\beta$ is \bl{rs:du:ex200815:gmb}, close to the model's restriction. The sign changes across samples. The market restriction is not rejected in any of \bl{rs:ntests} blocks, and the model's restriction is rejected in \bl{rs:nrej:gmb} of them, without correction for the number of tests. For GDP growth neither restriction is rejected, and adding $D$ does not significantly improve out-of-sample forecasts (Clark--West \bl{cm:addD:cw}).

\subsection{Equities Alone}\label{sec:eqalone}
Equity valuation alone raises the adjusted $R^2$ of the change in unemployment by \bl{cm:eq:gain}, the market index by \bl{cm:mkt:gain}, the FRB/US weights by \bl{cm:frbus:gain}, the 10-year yield by \bl{cm:y10:gain}, the dollar by \bl{cm:usd:gain} and the three variables entered freely by \bl{cm:free:gain}. Both indexes draw heavily on equity valuation, which helps explain why unemployment data have difficulty distinguishing between them: the movements that distinguish the weightings are the ones in which the dollar and equity valuation move in different proportions, and there are few of them. Entered freely, the coefficients are not significantly different from proportional to the market weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wm}) or to the FRB/US weights ($p=$ \bl{cm:prop:wb}); the power is low.

\subsection{Trade-Sensitive and Domestic Expenditure}\label{sec:expend}
Section~\ref{sec:channels} traced the model's weights to channels: consumption and investment for equities, net exports for the dollar. If the weights describe those channels, composition should predict the components separately even if it does not predict the aggregates. I specified two predictions before estimating. The coefficient on $D$ is negative for the contribution of net exports to growth over the next four quarters, because a reading in which the dollar weighs more is a reading in which trade is weaker (P1). The coefficient is larger for consumption and private investment than for net exports (P2). Table~\ref{tab:expend} reports both.

In 1976--2026 the coefficient on $D$ for net exports is \bl{ex:net:main:D} (\bl{ex:net:main:D:lo} to \bl{ex:net:main:D:hi}; bootstrap interval \bl{ex:net:main:D:blo} to \bl{ex:net:main:D:bhi}), an effect of \bl{ex:net:main:D:eff} percentage points of growth between the 5th and 95th percentiles of $D$. On 1996--2026 the signs are the same and the evidence is weaker: the net-export coefficient is \bl{ex:net:1996p:D} (\bl{ex:net:1996p:D:lo} to \bl{ex:net:1996p:D:hi}) and the difference has a bootstrap interval of \bl{ex:dmn:1996p:blo} to \bl{ex:dmn:1996p:bhi}. Net exports respond to growth abroad and to policy feedback, and the exercise cannot separate a dollar effect from a response to the same shocks. Against it, the aggregate regressions point the other way for GDP, and for unemployment after 1996 (Section~\ref{sec:answer}).

\subsection{What Forecasters Expected and What Happened}\label{sec:spf}
Forecasters' errors combine the outcome with what they had already anticipated. Table~\ref{tab:spf} regresses the Survey of Professional Forecasters' unemployment forecast, the realized change and the error (realized less forecast), over the same \bl{cm:spf:n} surveys (1990Q3--2025Q3), on the index change and $D$. The coefficients on the forecast and the error add to the coefficient on the realization. When FRB/US reads the same asset movements as tighter than the market index does, the forecast does not respond (\bl{sd:u:all:forecast}, interval \bl{sd:u:all:forecast:lo} to \bl{sd:u:all:forecast:hi}), unemployment tends to come in lower (\bl{sd:u:all:realized}, \bl{sd:u:all:realized:lo} to \bl{sd:u:all:realized:hi}), and so the error is negative (\bl{sd:u:all:error}, \bl{sd:u:all:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:all:error:hi}). Composition predicts what subsequently happened, not what forecasters expected, and with a sign opposite to the one the spending-equivalent reading would imply if forecasters had not priced composition. The result depends on the sample, and I test which episodes carry it. Excluding 2001 changes little (the error coefficient is \bl{sd:u:ex2001:error}, interval \bl{sd:u:ex2001:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex2001:error:hi}). Excluding 2008--09 reduces it to \bl{sd:u:ex200809:error} (\bl{sd:u:ex200809:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200809:error:hi}) and the coefficient on the realization to \bl{sd:u:ex200809:realized} (\bl{sd:u:ex200809:realized:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200809:realized:hi}). Excluding 2008--15 the forecast coefficient is \bl{sd:u:ex200815:forecast} and the realization \bl{sd:u:ex200815:realized}, and the error is \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error}, with a robust interval of \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:lo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:hi}, which excludes zero, and a block-bootstrap interval of \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:blo} to \bl{sd:u:ex200815:error:bhi}, which includes it. The sign of the error coefficient is stable and its size is not: most of the full-sample effect on the realization comes from 2008--09. The outcomes overlap and the coefficients are not adjusted for their number.

What can a policymaker take from this? The two readings can be used interchangeably when markets move as they do around policy news, and the data cannot say which to prefer when they do not. A reading from either index should be accompanied by the question of what moved: the dollar, valuations, or yields. The answer indicates whether the two readings are likely to part.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}
The paper asks when a movement in financial conditions can be read as a movement in monetary policy under two calibrations. For the usual response to a policy surprise, the two readings are close: the model prices it at \bl{ag:h4:bpsi} funds-rate points with persistent paths and \bl{ag:bp:hl4} with a half-life of four quarters, and after rescaling they agree exactly along that direction. For other combinations of asset prices the readings differ, because the model gives the dollar more weight, relative to equities, than the event study does. The size of that contrast depends on how policy news is defined: the ratio of ratios is \bl{sg:base:ror} for the baseline factor and \bl{sg:two:ror} for the two-year yield on the same announcements, and common assumptions about decay change it little. The model's weights come from two channels, consumption and investment for equities and net exports for the dollar. The difference between the readings is roughly 0.2 to 0.4 of the average market move at horizons from a day to a year.

Aggregate activity does not separate the two. Conditional on the market index, composition does not predict unemployment or GDP with a sign or size that the sample can pin down, and equity valuation alone carries most of the content. Composition does predict the net-export contribution to growth in the main sample, as the model's channels imply, and not significantly on the post-1996 sample. These are statements about measurement. The causal effect of a movement on spending and its source remain separate questions; a movement that departs from the policy-news mix can come from the announcement itself \citep{boehm2025}.

The limits are those of the design. The FRB/US weights come from one model and one experiment and are held fixed in the intervals; the market weights are imprecise; the horizons are compared on overlapping data; the daily comparison uses nominal assets; and there are five recessions in the sample. The most durable finding is the contrast in composition and the fact that its size depends on how policy news is measured. The episodes illustrate it. In 1998--2001 the point estimates of the two readings had opposite signs, both small, and the dollar was the main source; the interval for the difference includes zero. In 2022--24 both readings were about one point of tightening, and both forecasts of unemployment exceeded the rise, mostly through the own-lag terms. A reader who wants to use either index can now say when to expect them to differ.

\section{The Weights: a Derivation}\label{app:ma}
Let the movements in the three asset prices be $x=\psi s+u$, where $s$ is policy news, $\psi$ the responses and $u$ everything else, with covariance $\Sigma$. Let $g_h$ be the effects of a persistent unit shift in each asset price on spending at horizon $h$. The FRB/US weights are proportional to $g_h$. The market weights solve the problem of recovering $s$ from $x$: the generalized least squares estimator is $\hat s=w_M'x$ with $w_M=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$, which satisfies $w_M'\psi=1$ and, by the Gauss--Markov theorem, minimizes the variance of the estimate among linear unbiased estimators of $s$ when $u$ has mean zero and covariance $\Sigma$ and is uncorrelated with $s$. For any $x$ the difference between the normalized readings is $D_h(x)=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'x=(w_{B,h}-w_M)'u$, because the policy part cancels. The readings coincide for every movement if and only if $w_{B,h}=w_M$, that is, if $g_h\propto\Sigma^{-1}\psi$: the criterion that recovers policy news precisely and the criterion that measures spending effects select the same weights only in that case. The variance of the difference is $(w_{B,h}-w_M)'\Sigma(w_{B,h}-w_M)$.

Observed movements need not be of the form $\psi s+u$ with a single policy factor, and the model's effects need not be those of a movement of the same shape. If all observed movements lay along the single direction $\psi$, every weight vector normalized to price $\psi$ at one would give the same reading on them. The data would then identify the reading along that direction and leave the weights themselves undetermined. In the announcement windows $u$ is not zero, and it is the variation in $u$ that identifies $\Sigma$ and hence the weights. What distinguishes the two weightings in practice is the exposure of the difference in weights to the movements that occur, which Table~\ref{tab:dsize} measures as a ratio to the market move.

\section{Construction of the Series}\label{app:steps}
The three asset prices enter in three forms, each answering a problem of the one before. The headline results use the third.

\textbf{Nominal.} Rates as published. A nominal index treats a 10-year yield of 8 percent alike in 1981 and in 2000. That is reasonable only if inflation expectations and the neutral real rate are stable, and neither was. Expected inflation over the next year averaged \bl{einf:1981} percent in 1981 and is \bl{einf:latest} percent today, and the Laubach--Williams estimate of $\rs$ averaged \bl{rstar:lw:pre} percent over 1990--2007 and \bl{rstar:lw:post} percent over 2009--2016 (Figure~\ref{fig:infl}).

\textbf{Real.} Each rate less expected inflation at a matched horizon: ten-year expected inflation for the 10-year yield. The dollar is the real broad index.

\textbf{Relative to $\rs$.} The real 10-year yield less $\rs$. Equity valuation enters through the earnings yield $E/P=100/\text{CAPE}$, which is also an implicit real rate, as the gap $E/P-\rs$, converted to CAPE log points at the sample-average earnings yield so that its weight on CAPE is unchanged. The baseline uses the one-sided, full-sample Laubach--Williams estimate. Each variable is measured relative to its 1973--2026 average, so $\rs$ matters through its movements over time.

\begin{figure}[tbp]

\textbf{Data.} All series are monthly averages from January 1973 to September 2026, from the public sources in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The BBB spread is Moody's Baa yield less the 10-year yield. Months in which the CPI and unemployment releases are missing (October 2025) are interpolated. The unemployment gap is the unemployment rate less the Congressional Budget Office's natural rate, placed in the middle month of each quarter and interpolated. Expected inflation is the Cleveland Fed's estimate at one and ten years from 1982 and the Livingston Survey's 12-month CPI expectation before, shifted to the Cleveland level by the mean difference over 1982--84 and blended over 1982. CAPE is Shiller's series spliced over 1990--92 in logs to the monthly average of a daily series. The real broad dollar is spliced in 2006 in logs, as are the nominal dollar and the S\&P 500.

\textbf{Market index.} Policy news is the first principal component of MP1, MP2, ED2, ED3 and ED4 in the monetary-event windows, scaled by its regression on the two-year yield change so that one unit moves the two-year yield by one percentage point. Unscheduled events with no change in MP1 and March--April 2020 are dropped. Each of the 10-year yield change, the S\&P 500 return and the dollar (DXY) return in the window is regressed on the factor. The residual covariance $\Sigma$ of the three regressions gives $w=\Sigma^{-1}\psi/(\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi)$. The S\&P 500 and DXY weights apply to 100 times the log of CAPE and of the real broad dollar, as percent changes in the window and in the index levels agree to first order. The weights bootstrap resamples announcements with replacement and recomputes the policy-news factor and the weights each time; draws use the seed in \texttt{lib/config.py}.

\textbf{FRB/US index.} The weights are the cumulative GDP responses at four quarters to a persistent unit shift in each variable, simulated in FRB/US with VAR-based expectations, divided by the response to a persistent one percentage point rise in the funds rate. The weight on the policy rate is its direct effect with expectations of future rates held fixed. House prices enter as 100 times the log of the price-to-rent ratio relative to its trailing 15-year average. The expenditure decomposition of Figure~\ref{fig:channels} uses the model's own identities for consumption, residential and business fixed investment, inventories, government, exports and imports, weighted by their shares in GDP, with a residual below $10^{-7}$ percentage points; it is run with the current Board package (LONGBASE of October 2026), whose four-quarter responses are within 2 percent of those behind the stored weights, and the stored weights are used throughout the text. The step needs the Board's package and is not part of the replication run; its results are stored. For the decay experiments of Section~\ref{sec:persist}, each variable's shock decays geometrically with the half-life stated, and the response is computed in the model by linearity from the temporary-shock responses.

\textbf{Policy-news definitions.} The two-year definition replaces the futures factor by the change in the two-year Treasury yield in the same window, with the same regressions and the same bootstrap of announcements; the draws for different definitions are paired by keeping, for each resample, the announcements that fall in each subsample, so the subsample size varies by draw. The FRB/US weights are held fixed.

\textbf{Forecasting and survey data.} The regressions use local projections of the 12-month-ahead change in the unemployment gap (four-quarter-ahead GDP growth) with HAC standard errors of 18 lags. The Survey of Professional Forecasters data are the median forecasts, 1990Q3--2025Q3, matched to the financial data up to the month before each survey's deadline.

\section{Inference and the Decomposition of the Levels}\label{app:differ}
\subsection{How Precisely Are the Weights Estimated?}\label{sec:precision}
The weights come from regressions on \bl{n:events} announcements. The weight on the 10-year yield has a 90 percent interval from \bl{wb:ten:lo} to \bl{wb:ten:hi} around \bl{w:ten}. The interval for equity valuation runs from \bl{wb:eq:lo} to \bl{wb:eq:hi}, and for the dollar from \bl{wb:usd:lo} to \bl{wb:usd:hi}. The FRB/US weights on all three lie inside these intervals, the equity weight near the edge: it is lower in absolute value than \bl{frw:eq:pct} percent of the bootstrap draws, about 14 percent in a two-sided test. The data do not reject that the model weights equal the market weights. This is a statement about power, not a finding that they are equal, and it treats the FRB/US weights as fixed. The intervals in the text come from the bootstrap of the announcements; the contrast in composition in Section~\ref{sec:persist} is the ratio of ratios, whose interval includes one at two quarters.

\section{Sensitivity}\label{app:robust}
\subsection{Weights That Move}\label{sec:drift}
The weights define the unit, so I hold them fixed and show how they would drift. Re-estimating on an expanding window of announcements from 2006 keeps the 10-year and dollar weights inside the 90 percent band of the full-sample estimate at every date after 2010; the equity weight is outside at \bl{exp:post2010:n:out:eq} dates in 2011--14, by at most \bl{exp:post2010:min:eq} against a band edge of \bl{base:lo:eq} (Figure~\ref{fig:weightpath}). A 20-year rolling window fails after 2021, because it drops the announcements of 1996--2005. The 10-year weight is imprecise throughout. Apart from the equity weight in 2011--14, the weights stay within their sampling error.

\subsection{Limits of the Design}\label{sec:limits}
The weights come from announcements since 1996 and are applied from 1973; they assume that the responses reflect policy rather than information effects \citep{nakamura2018}, and if policy came to target financial conditions the responses could change, the Lucas-critique concern that \citet{hatzius2010} raise for all indexes. The FRB/US weights come from one model, at one horizon and in one experiment, and are held fixed in the intervals. The daily comparison of Section~\ref{sec:size} uses nominal assets, with the nominal broad dollar for the real dollar, no adjustment for $\rs$ and weights fitted on the same announcements; the intraday comparison holds each event out. The sample has five recessions, two of them out of sample, and the 12-month outcomes overlap, so the counts of tests are descriptive and not adjusted for their number. The neutral rate is not neutral to outcomes, because the Laubach--Williams model uses output, inflation and unemployment.
