## 1. Intervals: bias-corrected percentile intervals (most consequential)
* Note B now uses Efron's bias-corrected (BC) percentile intervals everywhere (`baseline_real/lib/intervals.py`; Efron 1987; the small-sample bias of VAR bootstraps, Kilian 1998). Reason [verified in Note B's 912 compared responses]: the bootstrap draws are smaller in magnitude than the point estimates for most responses, increasingly so at long horizons, so plain percentile intervals sit too close to zero. BC leaves widths unchanged (median ratio 1.01) and shifts intervals away from zero (median 0.04, 0.07, 0.13 at 12, 24, 36 months in Note B); at 12 lags 60 of 456 responses became significant at 10 percent and 6 stopped being.
* Note A's VAR intervals (steps 33 and 37, block bootstrap, `REPS` = 500 or 1,000 draws, plain percentiles) very probably have the same shift [inference: the mechanism is the VAR's lagged dependent variable; Note A's quarterly VAR has 4 lags and fewer observations, so the bias may be larger]. **Recommendation:** compute BC intervals from the saved draws of Note A's VAR responses (no re-estimation needed if the draws are kept) and compare; adopt one convention and state it once in the bootstrap or robustness text. Note A's current results that rest on "the interval excludes zero" (for example the output-gap response at 8 quarters, R = -0.42 [-0.69, -0.00]; E and F at 8 quarters with intervals including zero, FP-A75) may change verdict. The comparison script is `baseline_real/compare_intervals.py`.
* Verify before citing: Efron (1987) JASA 82(397), 171-185 and Kilian (1998) REStat 80(2), 218-230 [verified against Crossref, 19 Nov]; they are in `experimental/fin_shocks/paper/references.bib` (`efron1987`, `kilian1998`).

## 2. Generated-weights uncertainty in the VAR
* Note A bootstraps the weights (5,000 announcement draws, FP-A7: the 10-year weight 90 percent interval 0.13 to 1.13) but its VAR intervals hold the weights fixed. Note B found the same and ran one sensitivity (`baseline_real/18_weights_boot.py`: each draw re-estimates the weights and rebuilds the index) [verified]: the 10-year weight varies 0.23 to 1.08 across draws (estimate 0.62); the draws of the unemployment response spread 3 to 28 percent more and shift toward zero (76 percent of draws below the estimate at 24 months); with BC intervals the 24-month responses stay significant (unemployment [0.23, 0.98], prices [-0.99, -0.21]).
* **Recommendation:** one paired sensitivity in Note A (draw the weights from `weights_boot.csv`, rebuild the MCFCI, re-estimate the VAR response), reported as a sentence in the VAR section with the interval, and "conditional on the weights" in table notes. The weights draws already exist in Note A's package.

## 3. r*: more estimates, same result (answers items 1 and 2 of FP-A77)
* Note B tried Holston-Laubach-Williams (one-sided; the New York Fed publishes it one-sided only) and Lubik-Matthes (Richmond Fed; one median series, sidedness not stated on the page) beside Laubach-Williams [verified, `explore_review/` and Section 7 of Note B]. The effect of adding the FCI on unemployment after two years (since 1996) is 0.60 (LW), 0.62 (HLW), 0.81 (LM), against 0.73 without r*; the price-level effect -0.43, -0.37, -0.55. **The FCI result does not depend on which model-based r* is used; the response levels do** (price level with the FCI -0.60 to -1.11).
* Earlier exploration (FS-A83, FS-A84): mechanical proxies for r* (moving averages, a forward rate, SPF, one-sided HP, EWMA) do not reproduce the LW result since 1996; only model-based estimates do. [verified in `explore_rstar/`]
* **Recommendation for Note A's r* step:** a short robustness paragraph with HLW and LM (the data files are in `fin_shocks/baseline_real/data/`; the index is built by `lib/noteA.market_index` with `index_inputs(D, "rstar", col)`; correlation with LW one-sided since 1996: HLW 0.97, LM 0.68). Say the LW series is the one-sided current estimate. The index's loading on r* (kappa about 1.5, FP-A77 item 2) means r* choices move the r* step's level; the paired comparison across steps is not affected.

## 4. Components and weights: what Note B found, and what it implies for Section 5.4
* Note B tested the index restriction in its monthly equations (real, both starts; `15_components.py`) [verified]: the unemployment response does not depend on it (paired differences include zero); the price level falls about as much in the first year, and less after two years with the components free (0.07 against 0.60 since 1996); equal coefficients are rejected at 5 percent in the inflation equation in both samples, not in the unemployment equation; BIC favours the restriction in every equation, AIC in three of four. The dollar's own contribution is small; the weakening comes from the rates' own contribution when three closely related prices are entered freely [verified, point estimates].
* Note A's Section 5.4 (step 37) finds the same pattern for output (equities carry the response; the dollar, freed, has the wrong sign; equal coefficients not rejected at r*). **Consistent.** If Note A ever adds an inflation variable (FP-A75 option), expect the price response to depend on the restriction.
* **Goldman Sachs and FRB/US weights in a macro model** (FP-A77 item 5: "the four calibrations in a macro model"): Note B ran two (`explore_review/run_items_23_24.py`, real version, 24 months, since 1996): with the GS weights the effect of adding the FCI is the same as with the market weights (unemployment 0.75 against 0.73; prices -0.45 against -0.43); with the FRB/US weights it halves (0.35) and the price effect disappears (+0.10, not significant) [verified]. The FRB/US weights put less on equities (-0.032 against -0.052) and more on the dollar (0.104 against 0.066) [verified from `output/four_weights_nb.csv`]. **Implication [inference]: responses scale with the equity weight.** That is a useful, checkable sentence for Note A's Section 6 (equities and macro models): its central claim (equities carry the activity response and the FRB/US-direction index agrees with the policy direction because both weight equities heavily) predicts exactly this. Note B put only the GS weights in the paper (one robustness row).
* **Recommendation:** run Note A's quarterly VAR with the four calibrations as a short table, expecting GS = market, Y close, B (FRB/US) about half. Do it only if Section 5.4 is kept.

## 5. The "one unit" normalization and the ordinary-day contrast (item 4 of FP-A77)
* The review's point [secondhand, but the algebra is verified in Note B]: the weights satisfy w'psi = 1, so "the index tightens by one unit, as much as the yield itself" is a choice of units, not a result. Note B removed that phrasing and the ordinary-day comparison (loading 0.29 against 1.00; FS-A81, FS-A86) and kept the misspecification argument: rates alone can work in principle, whether they do is an empirical question, and a simulation (the true model with the FCI; the rates-only model recovers the wrong sign in 85 percent of samples at 12 months) shows the standard result is to be expected.
* **Recommendation for Note A:** state the normalization once as a normalization; give the move of each asset at a typical surprise (the review's figures for 5 basis points: about 2.7 bp on the 10-year, -0.39 percent on stocks, +0.21 percent on the dollar [secondhand; recompute from Note A's own weights]); present the ordinary-day loading, if at all, as a descriptive fact with no counterfactual and no decomposition.

## 6. Window inputs and definitions (check Note A's text)
* Note A's text says "30-minute windows" (`paper/mcfci.tex` lines 79, 95, 110). The database's monetary-event window is 30 minutes (10 before to 20 after the statement) only when there is no press conference; with a press conference it ends 60 minutes after the conference starts [verified in Acosta et al. 2025, WP 2025-30, Section 2.2]. Say which window Note A uses (it uses the "monetary events" sheet, as Note B).
* MP1 and MP2 are the Gurkaynak-Sack-Swanson implied surprises (scaled for the days left in the contract month), not raw FF1; ED2-ED4 are Eurodollar futures until 2021 and the matching SOFR futures from January 2022 [verified, same source]. Note A's definition of policy news should say so.
* The real-version window impacts assume expected inflation does not move in the window; state it once.

## 7. Expected inflation before 1982 (Note A shares the construction)
* `lib/noteA.expected_inflation` (vendored from `fci_note`) uses the Livingston 12-month CPI expectation, shifted to the Cleveland 1-year and 10-year levels over 1982-84 (-0.02 and -0.05 pp) and blended over 1982, so the pre-1982 **10-year** expectation moves one for one with the one-year survey [verified]. It enters Note A only through the real 10-year yield (the real and r* steps). Note A's text says "Cleveland Fed from 1982 and the Livingston Survey before" (`mcfci.tex` line 276) without the assumption. **Recommendation:** one sentence that the 1970s long-run expectation is the shifted one-year survey and that it affects the real and r* indexes before 1982, not the nominal index. Note B declined to build an alternative long-run series (the 1985 start covers it); Note A's trend-share statistics (72/62/33 percent, FP-A76) depend on this period, so a note is appropriate there.

## 8. Writing and presentation (apply to Note A)
* **Appendix pointers (author's rule, `experimental/WRITING_GUIDE.md`):** never a bare reference to an appendix; a sentence with the intuition and the result, and the reference in a footnote.
* **Terms (author, 19 Nov 2026):** "policy news" is the announcement-window surprise; the broad daily measure is "surprises in the policy direction", never "policy news". Note A's "policy-news index" and MCFCI wording should be checked against this.
* **Point changes, paired differences, significance:** say which a number is. Note B's tables give the paired difference with its interval beside the two responses.
* **Abstracts:** about 100-120 words, no results numbers; the introduction states the matched result once.
* **"Robust" claims:** tie a categorical claim to an assertion in the build (Note B's `claims_real.py`, and assertions in the report scripts, fail when a statement stops being true). Note A's sync check does this; extend it to any new sentence about significance.
