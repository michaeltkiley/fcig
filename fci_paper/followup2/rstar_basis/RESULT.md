# Does the r* basis stabilize the index? (rstar_basis)

**Question.** Does the r* basis make the index's relation to unemployment stable and its level mean the same across regimes?

**Design (pre-specified in `common.py`, `02_`, `03_`).** Market, GS, FRB/US at nominal, real, r*-gap steps. (A) Projection of u(t+12)-u(t) on u, its 12-month change and the index (level or 12-month flow, scaled by sd), origins 1976-2025, Covid rule, HAC(18); subperiods 1976-89 (n 160), 1990-2007 (216), 2009-19 (132); 2022-25 (42) descriptive only; Wald equality of the index slope; sup-F for one slope break (15% trim), p-values from a 24-month moving-block residual bootstrap (499); rolling 20-year windows; package VAR (12 lags) Phi(12) by subperiod. (B) Free coefficient on r* (LW, Kiley) beside M_rstar. (C) Intercept of a level regression of the index on the gap, by period, 24-month block bootstrap. About 130 statistics on shared data: a pattern, not individual tests. Recessions in samples: two in 1976-89, two in 1990-2007, none in 2009-19.

**Unit restriction [verified against `lib/cases.py`].** M_rstar = M_real-form + c r*, c = -w10 + wE(100/EPbar) = -0.609 - 0.0512(100/5.636) = **-1.518** (both terms negative). Nominal to real adds -w10 e10. The step also swaps 100 ln CAPE for the linear earnings-yield gap.

**Results.**
- *Slope stability.* Market level slope is stable across the three subperiods only at r* (Wald p 0.93; real 0.32; nominal 0.08); GS and FRB/US levels are stable at every step (p 0.5-0.8). Flow slopes are unstable at every step (market p 0.12, 0.04, 0.21 nominal, real, r*; r* rejects for GS 0.046, FRB/US 0.005): the flow slope falls after 1989 in every basis (market r*: 0.43, 0.26, 0.23 per sd). Sup-F rejects at 5% for 2 of 18 cases, both nominal flows; no r* case. Rolling flow-slope sd: market 0.087 r*, 0.125 real, 0.140 nominal, same order in GS and FRB/US.
- *VAR.* Phi(12) falls 0.65, 0.39, -0.01 (market r*, by subperiod) and similarly in every basis; equality rejected at all steps (p 0.004-0.02).
- *Unit restriction.* Free r* coefficient as a multiple rho of the restriction (1 = restriction, 0 = no adjustment): market level, LW 1.84 [-0.27, 5.0], p(rho=1) 0.42, p(rho=0) 0.14; flow 1.40 [-0.8, 8.0]; Kiley level -0.34 [-11, 13], flow -0.14 [-3.5, 2.4]. Adding e10: joint p 0.21-0.95. The restriction is not rejected; neither is its absence. r* barely moves at a 12-month horizon (flow sd 0.33), so the test has no power to separate them.
- *Neutrality, 2009-19 minus 1990-2007 (90% bootstrap).* Market: nominal -2.13 [-2.57, -1.59] (-0.52 sd of index), real -1.44 [-1.93, -0.99], r* LW +0.88 [0.55, 1.40] (+0.31 sd), r* Kiley +0.03 [-0.42, 0.31]. GS and FRB/US: nominal -0.83, -1.75; real -1.00, -1.10; r* LW +1.01, +0.66, all excluding zero. 2009-15 gives the same signs. The 1973-89 to 1990-2007 shift is -3.6 [-4.9, -3.2] even at r*.

**Verdict: cannot say; it weakly buttresses the neutrality half and does not buttress the stable-response half.** The r* basis shrinks the post-2008 drift in the level at a zero gap (to zero with Kiley, reversed in sign with LW), lowers rolling slope dispersion, and removes the nominal sup-F rejections. It does not remove the post-1989 fall in the unemployment response, does not make the level stable from the 1970s-80s, and the unit restriction cannot be tested at this power. Note B should not claim that r* stabilizes the response.

**Recommendation.** Section 5 or appendix: one sentence on the neutrality shift (nominal -2.1, real -1.4, r* +0.9 LW, 0.0 Kiley) and a plain statement that the unit restriction is untestable here. Do not cite dispersion counts.

**Limits.** Short subsamples; lower-bound years in 2009-19; 2021-26 infeasible for the VAR and uninformative for projections. The gap is zero outside the 2009-19 support (mean 1.7) and slopes differ by period, so the intercept is an extrapolation; persistent levels make intervals optimistic [inference].
