# Referee report 2: "Financial Conditions Indexes on a Common Basis"

## Recommendation: major revision

The question is useful: the Chair's remark that different indexes give different answers is a real policy problem, and separating measurement from weighting is a clean idea. The writing is clear and candid. The paper is a measurement note, though. The headline claim, "on a common basis the indexes move together", is weaker than the abstract says. Several statistics are built to agree, the weights are too imprecise to adjudicate between indexes, and the "common basis" is not fully common. The paper's own numbers support a more modest and more interesting message: the indexes agree on direction, disagree on level by the same amount as the benchmark's sampling error, and the neutral-rate step matters.

## Numerical checks (against numbers.tex, CSVs, tables)

Verified, no mismatch: correlations 0.99 / 0.94 / 0.76 (compare.csv); range 1.08 and 0.88; latest readings -0.93, -1.23, +0.27 (indexes_rstar.csv); bootstrap latest interval [-2.01, 0.24]; Phi(12) 0.65 [0.39, 0.83] and Okun 1.3; weights 0.609 / -0.051 / 0.065; shares sum to 1.01; w'psi = 0.994; robustness minima 0.91 and 0.72; 12-month flow-activity coefficient 0.50 within the Phi band.

Not mismatches, but misleading statements:
1. Section 5.3 is headed "The scales agree within sampling error", yet the published FCI-G effect is 0.13 (se 0.14) against its own claim of 0.5. That rejects the claim at about 2.6 se, and the text only says "only".
2. "Differences between the three readings are smaller than the sampling error" (Sec. 5.4). GS reads -1.23 and FRB/US +0.27, which are opposite in sign: easy versus tight. The abstract's "range of about a percentage point" hides this.
3. "FRB/US equity weight lower than 7 percent of draws". That is a one-sided 7 percent, about 14 percent two-sided. It is no evidence of equality, which the text concedes, but "all lie inside" is stretched at the edge (-0.032 vs bound -0.029).

## Ranked concerns

1. **Levels correlations are driven by persistence and the 1970s-80s (Sec. 4.1, Tab. compare, abstract).** I recomputed from indexes_*.csv. The market-FRB/US correlation at the r* step is 0.94 over 1974-2026 but 0.79 after 1990, and the real-step figure is 0.78 after 1990. The market-GS figure is 0.97 after 1990. Correlation of nonstationary, highly persistent levels is not the right metric, and the headline is inflated by the Volcker-era variance. Fix: report post-1990 and detrended, or change-based, correlations and cointegration/Granger-style measures in the main table. Doable with existing data.

2. **The "range" metric, and "units are common", are partly constructed.** Each index is demeaned over the same sample, so mean offsets between indexes are zero by construction. The reported "level difference of one point" is therefore only a within-sample dispersion. The units differ too: the GS index is rescaled by its own response to policy news, and FRB/US is in funds-rate points. The range is then sensitive to the rescaling. The std ratio of market to FRB/US is 1.28, so a scale difference alone produces a range. Fix: report the range after regressing out scale (or standardizing), and state the sensitivity to the GS conversion (se of resp:gs). Doable.

3. **The weights are very imprecise, and this undermines the benchmark.** The 10-year weight is 0.61 [0.13, 1.13] and the latest reading [-2.01, 0.24] straddles zero. The paper builds a "benchmark" on this, and then compares other indexes to it as though it were the truth. With sampling error as large as the gaps, the test cannot discriminate between candidates; the paper partly says so but the abstract and conclusion still treat it as a benchmark with "agreement". Reframe: the benchmark is one candidate, and it has no claim to being the standard. Also state that the weights bootstrap ignores FRB/US estimation error, which is also unknown. Text change, plus a joint comparison with FRB/US weights as random. Doable.

4. **Identification: external validity of weights (Sec. 2.2).** The weights use 253 announcements from 1996 on, with 30-minute windows (S&P and DXY returns, 10-year yield change), applied to level deviations since 1973 and to CAPE and a real dollar that are not what moves in the window. Equity prices in the window reflect discount-rate and expectations-of-future-policy effects, not the level of valuation. The mapping of a window S&P return to 100 ln CAPE is asserted "to first order". Pre-1996 weights are an out-of-sample extrapolation, and the lower-bound era responses are about path news. The 1977-80 "widest gap" episode lies entirely in that extrapolated period. Fix: sub-sample weight stability with formal tests; drop-1973-95 comparison. Partly doable.

5. **Information effects and non-policy movement are set aside, but they drive the results (Secs. 2.2, 6).** The GLS weights recover policy news, and then the index is applied to all movements, so "policy-equivalent tightening" for earnings-driven equity moves is a definition, not a finding. Since the equity group explains most gaps, the main finding is that the weights on equity disagree because they answer different questions (policy transmission to the 2-year yield vs. effect on GDP). That is a different object, so the "disagreement" is partly a category difference, and the abstract should say so. Add a version with Jarocinski-Karadi or Bauer-Swanson-orthogonalized surprises (the paper mentions it already but in robustness it uses only other windows). Possibly doable with the USMPD data.

6. **FRB/US comparator risks being a straw man.** It is built by the author, at four-quarter GDP effect with VAR-based expectations, with the policy-rate weight fixed at its direct effect. The paper itself says it is "mine" and not the Fed's own. The finding that it differs in equity weight (0.62 times) depends on these modelling choices and the 4-quarter horizon (the choice is not varied). FCI-G, the Board's own, is only compared in flows. Fix: vary the horizon (4, 8, 12 quarters), expectation mode (model-consistent), and show the effect on the weights and the range. Needs new FRB/US runs, not possible with existing results alone.

7. **r* uncertainty is ignored, and r* is full-sample one-sided (Secs. 3.3, 6).** Laubach-Williams has standard errors of 1-2 points, larger than the one-point range the paper discusses. The r* step "changes the reading most" but its own uncertainty is larger than every other source. A swing of -0.93 to +0.41 across the weights variations is also 1.3 points. The step also uses r* as a real-rate subtraction while the equity step uses E/P - r*, an assumed equivalence. Use the NY Fed bands or Kiley's alternative, plus a Holston-Laubach-Williams real-time (one-sided) version, in the main text. Existing data: partly (LW bands are public).

8. **VAR and forward impetus (Sec. 4).** (a) The Okun conversion, "an Okun coefficient of two", is asserted, and no output response is estimated. (b) The unemployment-first ordering is an identification choice, but the "response to a one-unit tightening" is a mix of policy and non-policy index movements, so it is not a causal policy multiplier, and it contradicts Sec. 5.3's use as a yardstick of FCI-G (0.5 per unit of growth). (c) The VAR-based Phi(12) shrinks from 0.65 to 0.40 with four lags and its 1990 interval is [0.21, 0.92]; the flow's correlation with FCI-G falls to [-0.03, 0.88] from 1990. Yet the text calls the finding robust. (d) The FI formula ties the flow to Phi, so correlations of FI with FCI-G (0.84) partly reflect that both are differences of the same levels; a 12-month-change comparison (0.98) shows it is mechanical. (e) 2020 dummies remove a period that is a major test of the index. (f) Block bootstrap with 12-month blocks on a 12-lag VAR is acceptable but state that the weights error is not propagated into the VAR bands.

9. **Out-of-sample/forecast claims (Sec. 5.5).** Flows add 0.11-0.14 adjusted R2 but no significant DM gain (best t = -1.12), and 2021-25 reverses. The "flows carry information, levels do not" headline is therefore not supported for out-of-sample use, and it is partly mechanical (flows are changes and unemployment persistence is the benchmark). Present as in-sample descriptive only, and compare with a changes-in-levels regressor (12-month change of the level). Doable.

10. **The tightening between steps is not a like-for-like story.** The correlation of real and r* versions is 0.57 over 1990-2026, which is a large change, yet the abstract says indexes "move together". The paper cannot be both: either r* matters a lot (a claim about the neutral rate, which has no ground truth here) or the indexes agree. Say that the paper's central robust finding is about r*, and that the r* step is not validated against any outcome. An activity test comparing real vs r* versions (the regressions exist in the appendix) belongs in the main text. Doable.

11. **Zero point and base period.** The mean of 1973-2026 is not neutral; the 1990 base changes readings by 1.4 (market -0.93 vs +0.45). All "tight/easy" language about 2009-16 and "today" depends on that choice and on the r* step. State this in the abstract.

12. **Literature and positioning.** Missing: Caballero et al. 2026 is only cited, not compared; Bauer-Swanson 2023 for orthogonalized surprises is cited without use; Jarocinski-Karadi on information effects; Gertler-Karadi / Gilchrist-Zakrajsek (excess bond premium) as an event-study credit-risk index; Hatzius et al. 2010 on the weight problem (cited); Koop-Korobilis dynamic FCI; Brave-Butters on NFCI; Ajello et al. 2023 (cited); Hakkio-Keeton (KCFSI) and Adrian-Boyarchenko-Giannone on vulnerable growth (only mentioned in discussion). The contribution relative to Arnaut-Bauer 2024 and Sack-Swanson 2023 (also one-factor policy weights) is small and needs an explicit statement of what is new.

## Minor points

- The abstract uses "correlation of 0.99 with GS": GS is my replication with published weights (not the proprietary series) and has 4 variables with BBB weight 39.6 percent that the benchmark omits. Add that the correlation is of two level series dominated by the 10-year yield.
- Table "tab:boot" correlation intervals are conditional on the VAR fixed and on GS/FRB/US fixed; say so.
- ANFCI correlation of 0.22 is reported but not interpreted beyond "different object"; NFCI is converted by weekly policy news, which is a weak identification.
- Sec. 4.2: "A value of 1" in FI is stated per unit of index, while FCI-G is per percent of GDP growth; the scale equivalence needs the Okun step spelled out in the main text.
- Appendix data note: October 2025 CPI and unemployment are interpolated; the latest-month reading is partly interpolated, so say what share.
- The 2026 readings use r* through 2026Q2 only. Online data for later months are extrapolated.
- The caption of Fig. 1 should state which indexes are in which units.

## What a reader skipping the appendix lacks

The main text needs: the weight formula assumptions (one-factor), the definition of the GS conversion and its uncertainty, how the FRB/US weights are produced (horizon, expectations), and the main-text statement that the weights bootstrap treats other indexes as fixed. Move the 1990-onward comparison (point 1) and the activity comparison of real vs r* into the main text, and state limits in the abstract.
