# Independent read of the 36-page manuscript (12 Nov 2026), revision d1da9b8

Reader: a fresh agent given only the PDF and three questions (no repository, no other files, no code). The report below is the reader's own, unedited. It is model output, not the author's view.

REFEREE READ OF "Reading Financial Conditions in Units of Monetary Policy" (36 pp; read from the PDF only)

1. WHAT A POLICY EQUIVALENT MEANS UNDER EACH METHOD (Sec. 2.1, p.4; Sec. 2.2, p.5; App. A, p.31)
- Both methods express a movement in three asset prices (10-year yield, equity valuation/CAPE, real broad dollar, all measured relative to r*) as a number of points of "policy". The number is the policy equivalent.
- Market-implied (event-study) reading: policy comes first. Asset-price responses to a Nakamura-Steinsson-type surprise in FOMC announcement windows are estimated. GLS weights w_M ∝ Σ⁻¹ψ are chosen so as to recover the surprise from the three prices together. The unit is a surprise that raises the 2-year yield by 1 pp. A movement is worth the size of the surprise that would on average produce it. It answers "how big a conventional policy surprise does this movement resemble?"
- Spending-equivalent (FRB/US) reading: spending comes first. Each asset price is weighted by its effect on GDP four quarters later in FRB/US, under a persistent shift. That is divided by the GDP effect of a persistent 1 pp rise in the funds rate. A movement is worth the funds-rate change with the same GDP effect. It answers "how big a policy-rate change would have a comparable effect on GDP?"
- To compare them, the model weights are rescaled by b'ψ (0.954 at four quarters) so that a conventional surprise reads one in both. Eq. (2) then shows the readings differ only through D = (w_B − w_M)'u, the part of the movement that departs from the usual policy-news pattern. If assets move in the usual proportions, the two agree. If the weights are identical, they agree always.

2. WHAT THE SAME-ANNOUNCEMENT RESULTS ADD BEYOND THE OPENING EXAMPLE (Sec. 3.1-3.2, pp.6-9; Figs. 1-2; Tables 1-2, C.1-C.2)
- The opening example (Fig. 1, p.2) is a single illustrative pair of movements. It chosen so the futures-factor market reading rates them equal (0.256 and 0.255). It shows that the two methods can disagree (dollar/equity ratio 2.51 versus 1.00). It does not show how fragile the market side is.
- Re-estimating the market weights on the same 253 announcements, with the 2-year yield change instead of the futures factor as "policy news", teaches four things the example could not.
 (i) The market side is not a single object. The equity weight halves, from −0.051 to −0.027. The market dollar/equity ratio goes from 1.00 to 2.06, and the ratio of ratios from 2.53 to 1.22. The 2-year yield change contains a "remainder" (20 percent of its variance, correlated 0.90 with the factor) that moves yields and the dollar but not equities. The remainder counts as signal, so equities become a weaker indicator.
 (ii) The measure of policy news matters about as much as the choice of weights. Mean |D| ratios are 0.33 (model versus baseline market) and 0.37 (baseline versus 2-year-yield market), p.2 and p.14.
 (iii) Statistical precision. Intervals for the contrast include zero under the 2-year yield (−0.079 to 0.287) and for the ratio of ratios (0.48 to 3.05). They exclude zero under the baseline (0.003 to 0.448; ratio 1.08 to 7.41). The FRB/US weights lie inside the market bootstrap intervals, so the data cannot reject equality (p.8).
 (iv) Persistence versus composition (Sec. 3.5, p.13; Fig. 4). Persistence changes the scale (0.954 falls to 0.82 and 0.71) but not the relative dollar/equity weight (2.5-2.6 for all half-lives).
- What I took from this: the headline "model weights the dollar more than the event study" is robust in sign across definitions, but not in size. It is statistically weak for the 2-year yield definition.

3. WHEN WOULD CHOOSING A DIFFERENT INDEX HAVE CHANGED THE ASSESSMENT? (Intro p.2; Sec. 5, pp.15-21; Tables 7-9; Figs. 5-7)
- Across 369 months, 1996-2026, the baseline market and spending readings of the 12-month change agree in sign in 87.5% of months. They differ by at least 0.25 point in 36.6% (interval 10-70%). They give materially opposite readings in only 8 months (Table 8, p.19). The choice mostly changes size and rarely direction.
- It would have mattered in 1998-2001. Averages are −0.06 (market) versus +0.16 (spending), with opposite signs in the point estimates, both small, difference 0.22 (interval −0.12 to 0.53). Dollar appreciation and rising valuations coincided in 1997-98, and the model gives the dollar more weight and equities less. The gap peaked at 0.76 in January 1998, and the signs differ in 7 of 48 months. Under every definition the ranges do not overlap, but the baseline intervals do (p.20).
- It would not have mattered in 2022-24. Both readings are about 1.02 and 1.01. Equity falls (contribution −0.10) and the dollar rise (+0.11) cancel in D. All calibrations show about one point or more of tightening (Table 9, p.21). The cancellation depends on the definition. With the 2-year yield D = −0.17. With no lower-bound years D = −0.19.
- 2004-06: close in changes (D = −0.09). The level gap of −0.11 is attributed to house prices, not weights (Table 7, p.17; p.21).
- Levels versus changes. In levels, the full FRB/US index reads 1.79 points tighter than the market index in 1998-2001 (equity weights: valuations far above average), but this does not carry over to the 12-month change.
- Outcomes (Sec. 6, pp.22-25). Subsequent unemployment and GDP growth do not separate the two readings, and the paper says the tests have low power. The exception is the forecast-error and 1996-2026 results, which are fragile to 2008-09 and to the definition.

(a) MAIN FINDING IN ONE SENTENCE
Converting a movement in yields, equities and the dollar to points of policy gives similar answers under an event-study weighting and an FRB/US spending weighting when asset prices move as they do around policy news, but they differ in size (rarely in sign) when equities and the dollar move in other proportions, and the choice of how to measure policy news shifts the readings about as much as the choice of weighting; the 2022-24 tightening reads about one point under every calibration.

(b) WHERE THE ARGUMENT BROKE DOWN FOR ME OR I HAD TO GUESS
- The key conversion in Sec. 3.4 (p.12) and Sec. 3.5. D is defined after dividing the model's weights by b'ψ so that the two indexes agree "exactly along the policy direction". The paper calls this an identity, not a finding, and says "the finding is the number before it was divided out" (0.954). But the headline "agree up to a scale" (abstract, intro) leans on that number, and the paper also says the model's own policy shock has a funds-rate equivalent of 1.77 (p.12; App. C.2). So I had to guess whether 0.954 is a meaningful agreement or a coincidence ("an empirical coincidence", p.12). The paper says both. A reader cannot tell what to conclude.
- Also unclear: Fig. 1 is built with a hand-picked equity fall of 5 log points and dollar rise of 3.9 log points, chosen so the baseline market reading is equal (p.2). The example is therefore constructed to show disagreement. Its quantitative weight (2.51, 2.53) is partly an artifact of that choice. I could not tell how typical such movements are until Sec. 4-5.
- The "ratio of ratios" test, the "proportionality" test and the "model less market" test are three different statistics for similar claims. Their intervals disagree across definitions (e.g. includes one for the 2-year yield), and the paper does not say which carries the main claim. The abstract says "in point estimates", so I took it as descriptive rather than significant.
- Section 4 gives a ratio of 0.19 in intraday windows (with events held out), 0.3 in daily data, and 0.33 in 12-month changes. The text says the sources of the gap are not isolated (p.15). I could not tell whether disagreement grows with horizon or is just a different mix of data (nominal versus real, in-sample versus held-out).

(c) CAN THE MAIN TEXT BE FOLLOWED WITHOUT THE APPENDICES?
- Mostly yes for the argument and the headline numbers. Sections 1-5 define the objects, give the logic (eqs. 1-3), the tables and the episodes. Appendix A (derivation, GLS/Gauss-Markov) is not needed. Appendix B (nominal, real, r* series; data) is partly needed. "Relative to r*", the real dollar, CAPE converted to log points and "the earnings-yield gap" are used in Secs. 2-5 but defined only in App. B (pp.32-33), so a reader without it cannot tell what the three inputs are. The same applies to the FRB/US experiment details (App. B, C.2), the factor construction (first principal component of MP1, MP2, ED2-ED4, App. B p.33) and Tables C.1-C.2, which the text cites for the other definitions. Table A.1 (p.31) is cited in the main text only as a pointer. The paper is also dense with cross-references and numbers per sentence, so a reader loses track of the baseline definitions.

(d) WHAT A JME EDITOR WOULD MOST LIKELY OBJECT TO
- No clear contribution or decisive result. The paper's own conclusion is that the answer "depends on the question", that the tests have low power, that the data cannot say which index to prefer, and that "the paper does not say which definition of policy news is right" (p.27). The practical advice is to report both. Many key claims are "in point estimates" with intervals including zero (1998-2001 difference −0.12 to 0.53; 2-year-yield contrast; D in 2022-24), and the main finding is partly an identity (the scaling by b'ψ).
- Weak identification of the market weights and a strained comparison. The market index rests on 253 windows. The equity response is an imprecise −5.14 to 5.23 for the remainder, the weights depend on the definition of policy news, and the equity weight is outside the full-sample bootstrap band in 2011-15 (p.26). The FRB/US weights are held fixed in all intervals, from one model, one horizon and one experiment (p.26). The FRB/US counterpart uses DXY nominal against a real dollar and a window return as a CAPE log point (pp.11-12), and the "counterpart" of policy news in FRB/US is a proxy (average funds rate) that gives 1.77 against 0.95.
- Information effects, and the market weights treat all equity moves in windows as policy (admitted p.7). The model weights are not validated against data either (Sec. 6 fails to separate them).
- Multiple definitions, many specifications and the exclusion of 2008-09 or 2001 with sign changes (Tables 11-14), with no adjustment for multiple testing ("not adjusted for the number of definitions", p.8). The results in 6.4-6.5 were written as predictions after the nulls were known and are acknowledged as not clean. Forecast-error results are driven by 2008-09.
- Paper-structure and style: a very long list of statistics for a conclusion that is modest. The Fig. 1 example is hand-picked. Little new economics for JME's audience, since the novelty is a comparison of two existing weighting schemes on a stylised three-asset set (the paper's closest comparisons, Sec. 1.1 p.3, are Bletzinger et al. and Caballero-Simsek). Also the FRB/US (Board) vintage and the author's own r* estimate (Kiley check) are not reproducible from the PDF alone.
