Financial Conditions, r∗, and the Transmission of Monetary Policy
                             Surprises
                                           Draft for discussion

                                            9 November 2026


                                                  Abstract
      Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy, but recent estimates of its effects
      are troubling. Surprise tightenings often have small or wrong-signed effects on unemployment
      and inflation. Read through a Phillips curve, the estimates imply that lowering inflation is
      very costly. We keep standard interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements
      and change only how their effects are traced through the economy. We build in two well-
      established features of how policy works. It acts through financial conditions as a whole, not
      through one interest rate. And what matters is the level of rates relative to the neutral rate r∗ ,
      the rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. Traced through the two-year yield
      alone, the surprises give the familiar wrong signs. Traced through a broad financial conditions
      index measured relative to r∗ , they give standard and precisely estimated effects. A policy
      move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the unemployment rate about
      0.4 percentage point and lowers industrial production about 1 percent within a year. It lowers
      the core price level about 0.6 percent after two years. The implied tradeoff between inflation
      and unemployment is much steeper than recent estimates of a flat Phillips curve, and steeper
      than when conditions are measured in nominal terms. Financial conditions matter most for the
      unemployment result. Measuring them relative to r∗ matters most for the price result and the
      tradeoff, which rely on data since 2020.

1    Introduction
Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy. Its use rests on estimates of how
much a change in policy moves unemployment and inflation. Those estimates have become harder
to defend. In samples since the mid-1990s, a surprise tightening often has small effects on unem-
ployment, or effects of the wrong sign (Ramey, 2016). Its effects on inflation look smaller still.
Two concerns follow. One is that policy has become less powerful (Boivin and Giannoni, 2006;
Boivin et al., 2010). The other is that inflation responds little to slack: the Phillips curve is flat
(Hazell et al., 2022; Del Negro et al., 2020), so lowering inflation requires a large and lasting rise in
unemployment. If both were true, policy would be weaker and more costly than standard models
suggest. This paper asks whether these findings reflect how policy works or how its effects are
measured.
    The question matters in practice. From 2022 to 2023 the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate
by more than 5 percentage points. Policymakers expected costs: in August 2022 the Chair warned


                                                      1
of “some softening of labor market conditions” and called these “the unfortunate costs of reducing
inflation” (Powell, 2022). They also described policy as working through broad financial conditions.
In November 2023 the Chair noted that “Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent
months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors” (Powell, 2023). Yet the
standard tools for measuring the effects of policy leave those conditions out.
    Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements were meant to settle the mea-
surement problem. The FOMC makes its decision before the announcement, so market moves in
a narrow window around the announcement, at most about 100 minutes long, cannot have caused
the decision. The change in interest-rate futures and Treasury yields in that window is then news
about policy, or at least about the part of policy that moves interest rates (Kuttner, 2001; Gürkay-
nak et al., 2005). These surprises measure the effects of policy on asset prices precisely (Bernanke
and Kuttner, 2005; Hanson and Stein, 2015; Nakamura and Steinsson, 2018; Swanson, 2021). But
when they are used to measure effects on unemployment and inflation, the old puzzles often return.
Recent work offers several explanations, each with a fix, and the fixes complement one another.
  • Information. An announcement reveals the Fed’s view of the economy as well as its policy,
    so a surprise tightening can signal good news (Romer and Romer, 2000; Nakamura and
    Steinsson, 2018). Separating the two gives larger price effects (Jarociński and Karadi, 2020;
    Miranda-Agrippino and Ricco, 2021).
  • Predictable surprises. Part of each surprise can be predicted from earlier public data. Remov-
    ing it, and adding surprises from the Chair’s speeches, gives larger and more precise effects
    (Bauer and Swanson, 2023a,b).
  • Timing. Markets react within minutes, but unemployment and inflation are monthly averages.
    Measuring inflation daily removes most of the wrong-signed response (Jacobson et al., 2026).
  • Credit and risk appetite. Credit costs carry part of the effect (Gertler and Karadi, 2015;
    Caldara and Herbst, 2019). Announcements also move stock prices and the dollar much more
    than interest-rate surprises can explain. Treated as a separate shock, this news has large
    effects (Kroencke et al., 2021; Bauer et al., 2023; Boehm and Kroner, 2024; Bauer et al.,
    2026; Boehm et al., 2026).
Most of this work, however, traces the surprise forward through a single interest rate, measured in
nominal terms. That leaves out two basic parts of how monetary policy works.
    First, policy affects spending through financial conditions as a whole: the cost of long-term
credit, the value of wealth and the exchange rate (Boivin et al., 2010). A surprise tightening moves
long-term yields, stock prices and the dollar, not only the two-year yield. A model that omits these
variables traces the wrong experiment. The financial conditions index (FCI) used below combines
the 10-year yield, stock valuations and the dollar. It is scaled so that, in the announcement windows,
it rises about 1 unit per percentage point rise in the two-year yield. In ordinary months it moves
only about 0.35 to 0.4 unit per point. A model with the two-year yield alone therefore traces the
typical move in that yield. That move comes with a much smaller change in financial conditions
than a policy surprise brings.
    Second, what matters for spending and inflation is the level of real interest rates compared with
the neutral rate r∗ , the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. The neutral rate fell
a lot from the 1990s to the 2010s (Laubach and Williams, 2003; Holston et al., 2017, 2023; Hamilton
et al., 2016; Kiley, 2020b), and it shaped the long-run trend in yields (Bauer and Rudebusch, 2020).
Since 1994, interest rates have trended down along with r∗ , while activity has trended up. A model
that links the level of rates to activity then partly picks up these opposite trends, and finds that
higher rates go with stronger activity. Activity responds to changes in financial conditions, but

                                                  2
inflation depends on how long real rates stay above or below r∗ . So measuring rates relative to r∗
should matter most for prices.
    Our contribution is to hold the surprise fixed and change only the variables that carry it to
the economy. Broad financial conditions, measured relative to r∗ , replace a single nominal rate.
Doing so removes the post-1994 wrong-signed unemployment response. It also gives price and
output effects comparable to cleaned-shock benchmarks, and a tradeoff much steeper than recent
post-1990 estimates.
    Our design is simple. The shock is the policy-news surprise of Nakamura and Steinsson (2018),
measured in narrow windows around 252 FOMC announcements from 1996 to 2026. We measure its
effect on the two-year yield and on the FCI within these windows, and assume that unemployment
and inflation do not move within them. A daily model then traces how the two-year yield and
the FCI evolve after each announcement. Monthly unemployment and core inflation then respond
to the monthly averages of these daily series, as in a standard vector autoregression (VAR). Like
Jacobson et al. (2026), we take into account when each announcement falls within its month. The
FCI and the order of steps (nominal, real, then relative to r∗ ) come from a companion note on
financial conditions and were fixed before this analysis (FCI note, 2026).
    We build up the channel from policy to the economy in four steps. Every step includes a
smooth quadratic time trend in the monthly equations. Below, “significant” means that the 90
percent interval excludes zero.
  • With the nominal two-year yield as the only channel, the post-1994 puzzles appear. The
    unemployment response changes sign with the number of monthly lags and is significantly
    negative with 12. Prices barely move, and industrial production rises.
  • Adding the FCI makes the unemployment response standard, large and significant. Prices
    fall only a little.
  • Measuring rates in real terms changes little.
  • Measuring the two-year yield and the FCI relative to r∗ completes the picture. Unemployment
    rises 0.36 to 0.40 percentage point after 12 months. The core price level falls 0.53 to 0.72
    percent after 24 months. Industrial production falls about 1 percent after 12 months. All are
    significant for every number of lags.
These effects are not small. Per point on the two-year yield, the unemployment effect is about
twice that of the benchmark of Bauer and Swanson (2023a), and the price effect is similar for two
years and larger after that.
    The results also bear on the inflation-unemployment tradeoff. We measure it by the “Phillips
multiplier” of Barnichon and Mesters (2021): the fall in the price level per percentage-point-year
of higher unemployment. With financial conditions measured relative to r∗ , the multiplier is about
−0.8 to −1.1 after two years and −0.5 to −0.85 after three, and its 90 percent sets exclude zero.
With conditions measured in nominal terms, it is about half as large and not significantly different
from zero. With the two-year yield alone, it cannot be measured at all. Our estimates are much
steeper than the post-1990 estimate of Barnichon and Mesters (2021), about −0.15, which lies
outside our sets. They are flatter than the pre-1990 estimate in that paper, and flatter than the
tradeoff implied by the benchmark of Bauer and Swanson (2023a), whose price effects are similar
to ours but whose unemployment effects are smaller.
    The steps play different roles. Within our design, the FCI delivers the unemployment result,
with its response to the surprise measured in the window. Without the FCI, no adjustment of the
two-year yield gives a reliable unemployment response. With it, every adjustment does. Measuring
conditions relative to r∗ delivers the price result. The unemployment result holds however we treat

cleaned surprises of Bauer and Swanson (2023a) and the pure-policy surprises of Jarociński and

unemployment response without the FCI. Second, adding the FCI to a standard monthly proxy
VAR, which uses the surprise as an instrument, is not enough. The FCI’s response to the surprise
must be measured in the window.

1.1   Connection to the literature
Identifying the effects of policy. Our paper is closest in spirit to Jacobson et al. (2026). Like
them, we hold the surprise and the method fixed and change one measurement. They change how
often the outcome is measured; we change how the channel from policy to the economy is measured.
They study inflation only, while our main result is for activity. Both changes remove wrong-signed
responses, and both point to a mismatch between what the economist measures and what drives
decisions. Bauer and Swanson (2023a) and Jarociński and Karadi (2020) improve the surprise itself.
In our design a surprise enters only through its immediate effect on the two-year yield and the FCI,
and through its timing. Their cleaned surprises change our results little and make them slightly
stronger, so their fixes and ours can be combined. Our approach also changes which assumptions
the estimates rest on. It depends less on how strongly the surprise moves rates over a month, and
less on its link to earlier news. It relies instead on the assumption that the economy responds to a
given change in financial conditions in the same way, whatever caused it.
    The use of futures-based surprises in monthly models goes back to Faust et al. (2004). The
choice of surprise series can matter (Bu et al., 2021; Brennan et al., 2025), and so can the number of
lags (Coibion, 2012). Related work on timing and mixed frequencies includes Kilian (2024), Chudik
and Georgiadis (2022), Francis et al. (2011), Ghysels (2016) and Gazzani and Vicondoa (2020).
    Financial channels and risk appetite. Gertler and Karadi (2015) and Caldara and Herbst
(2019) are closest in emphasizing financial channels. We use a broad index and measure it relative
to r∗ , but leave credit spreads out of the baseline. Recent studies of risk appetite and the “non-
yield” shock, the announcement news that moves stocks and the dollar beyond what interest rates
explain, differ from ours in where they place risk premia (Bauer et al., 2026; Boehm and Kroner,
2024; Boehm et al., 2026). They treat policy-driven moves in risk premia as a second shock; we
treat them as part of financial conditions. The channels are the same in both views: stock prices,
the dollar and credit costs. Our estimates keep only the part of the stock and dollar moves that
comes with interest-rate news, so they measure the effects of that dimension of policy, not of all
policy news. The response of stock prices to policy-driven moves in long rates has also changed
over time (Kiley, 2014). Section 2.2.4 shows how large the rest is. Caballero et al. (2026) propose a
neutral level of the FCI itself, FCI∗ , because r∗ must otherwise absorb asset-price movements that
the index already captures. We keep r∗ for the rates inside the two-year yield and the index; using
gaps to FCI∗ is a natural extension.
    The power of policy and the Phillips curve. A long literature finds that the effects of
policy shocks became smaller after the early 1980s. It attributes the change to a more systematic
response of policy and to better-anchored expectations (Boivin and Giannoni, 2006; Boivin et al.,

                                                  4
2010; Primiceri, 2005). Others argue that standard identification fails after the late 1980s because
policy became more forward-looking (Barakchian and Crowe, 2013). Our results do not suggest
that policy has lost power. Our effects are larger than in samples that start in the 1970s, though
that comparison is not a formal test of change over time.
    On the Phillips curve, recent work finds a small slope (Hazell et al., 2022; Del Negro et al.,
2020; Ball and Mazumder, 2019; Fitzgerald et al., 2024). It also stresses that the slope is hard to
identify when policy offsets demand shocks (McLeay and Tenreyro, 2020), and that conventional
methods tend to understate it (Barnichon and Mesters, 2020). Policy shocks offer one way to
identify it. Barnichon and Mesters (2021) measure the tradeoff by the ratio of cumulative inflation
to cumulative unemployment after such shocks. In that metric they find a much flatter tradeoff after
1990 than before. Responses to policy shocks also map directly into the outcomes of alternative
policies (McKay and Wolf, 2023). We add evidence of this kind and show that the measured
tradeoff depends on how the stance of policy is measured. Our price result relies on 2020–22, when
inflation surged and then began to fall. Others read that episode as evidence of a steeper, nonlinear
Phillips curve (Benigno and Eggertsson, 2023), or as the unwinding of supply shocks (Bernanke
and Blanchard, 2025); we return to this in Section 4.1.

    This section sets out the reasoning behind the design. We first describe how monetary policy
is thought to reach spending and prices. We then show why a standard VAR can miss this route,
and why that could produce the puzzles. Finally, we connect the argument to work on financial
conditions and economic activity.

                                                       6
2.2.1   The standard transmission mechanism
Monetary policy sets the short-term interest rate today and, through its communications, shapes
what people expect that rate to be in the future. Few households and businesses borrow at the
policy rate. They face the rates on consumer credit, mortgages and business debt, which arbitrage
links to current and expected short-term rates. The same rise in rates raises the return investors
require on stocks. For businesses, a higher cost of capital raises the user cost of new equipment and
buildings, and investment falls (Jorgenson, 1963; Hall and Jorgenson, 1967). The value of existing
capital relative to its replacement cost falls as well (Tobin, 1969). For households, lower stock
prices lower wealth, and consumption depends on wealth as well as on current income (Ando and
Modigliani, 1963; Friedman, 1957). Higher rates also strengthen the dollar, which shifts spending
toward imports and reduces exports (Fleming, 1962; Mundell, 1963).
     Interest rates of many maturities, equity prices and the dollar therefore form the core of the
transmission mechanism (Boivin et al., 2010). These links run from the early work cited above to
modern models of the business cycle, in closed and open economies (Christiano et al., 2005; Smets
and Wouters, 2007; Galí and Monacelli, 2005). Models with heterogeneous households give more
weight to the indirect effects that work through income (Kaplan et al., 2018; Auclert, 2019). But
in all of these models the short-term rate matters mainly because of what it does to the prices and
rates that people actually face. And they act with a lag (Friedman, 1961).
     What matters in these models is not the nominal rate but the real rate relative to the neutral
rate r∗ . The same holds for the other parts of financial conditions: a given yield or stock valuation
is tighter when r∗ is low than when it is high. The distinction matters most for inflation. Spending
responds to financial conditions within months. Inflation responds to the slack that builds up over
time, so it depends on how long, and by how much, conditions stay tight relative to r∗ . A measure
of conditions that ignores a falling r∗ will overstate how tight policy has been, and by more the
longer the period. That error is largest for the cumulative effects that drive inflation.

2.2.2   What standard VARs leave out
The workhorse evidence on monetary policy comes from VARs with a few macroeconomic variables
and one interest rate (Bernanke and Blinder, 1992; Christiano et al., 1999). Some studies add a
credit spread (Gertler and Karadi, 2015; Caldara and Herbst, 2019), and factor models bring in
many more series (Bernanke et al., 2005). But long-term yields, equity valuations and the dollar
are usually absent from the small models used to measure the effects of policy, although some recent
work adds the dollar and stock-market volatility (Boehm et al., 2026). These are the variables that,
on the mechanism above, carry those effects. Concern about missing variables is not new: Sims
(1992) argued that the price puzzle arises because the Federal Reserve responds to information that
small VARs do not contain (see also Castelnuovo and Surico, 2010). Our concern is different. Here
the missing variables are the channel itself.

    High-frequency surprises help identify the shock, but they do not by themselves fix how it
is carried forward. Our design therefore does two things. It includes financial conditions in the
system, so that they carry the effects of the surprise forward, as in equation (9). And it measures
the immediate effect of the surprise on the two-year yield and on financial conditions directly in
the announcement window, where policy news is the main force at work. The measurement of ft
also matters. If activity responds to financial conditions relative to r∗ , a measure that ignores r∗
differs from the true ft by a slow-moving error, and that error enters equation (9).

2.2.3   Financial conditions and real activity
A separate literature asks how financial conditions are related to economic activity. Asset prices
help forecast output and inflation, but which ones help changes over time (Stock and Watson,
2003). Credit spreads, and especially their excess bond premium component, are strong predictors
of activity (Gilchrist and Zakrajšek, 2012). Broad indexes summarize many such series and are
used to assess the outlook (Hatzius et al., 2010; Brave and Butters, 2011). Financial conditions
are also linked to the risks around growth, not only to its average (Adrian et al., 2019), and their
relationship with unemployment may be nonlinear (Kiley, 2020a). Federal Reserve staff now publish
a measure of the effect of financial conditions on growth, with weights from large macroeconomic
models (Ajello et al., 2023).
    This work has a limit that the event-study literature does not. A correlation between financial
conditions and later activity need not reflect a causal effect, because financial conditions also
respond to the economy. Hatzius et al. (2010) address part of this problem by removing the effects
of past growth and inflation from their indicators. The companion note uses the timing in a VAR
for the same purpose (FCI note, 2026). The two literatures have complementary strengths. Work
on high-frequency surprises identifies policy shocks cleanly but carries them through a small set of
variables. Work on financial conditions studies a broad set of variables but does not isolate policy
shocks. We bring them together: the weights in our index come from how its components respond
to policy news, and the surprise is carried forward through the estimated relationship between that
index and the economy.

2.2.4   Policy news beyond interest rates, and risk appetite
Policy news beyond interest rates. Recent work finds that announcements move stock prices
and the dollar much more than interest-rate surprises can explain (Boehm and Kroner, 2024; Bauer
et al., 2026). Appendix Table A7 checks what this means for our design. In our 252 windows, the
policy-news surprise explains 80 percent of the variance of the move in the two-year yield, but
only about a third for the 10-year yield (32 percent), the S&P 500 (31 percent) and the dollar (34
percent), and 48 percent for the FCI. The part of the FCI’s move that the surprise does not explain
is as large as the part we use: its standard deviation is 0.06 index unit, against 0.05 for the move
that comes with a one-standard-deviation surprise.
    The window ratio bF is fairly stable. It ranges from 0.87 to 1.17 across 1996–2007, 2008–2019,
2020–2026 and 2020–2023, and its 90 percent intervals overlap when announcements are split by
the level of the VIX or the excess bond premium. Bauer et al. (2026) find that the link between
interest-rate surprises and risk appetite varies a lot with the state of markets; in our windows, the
link between the surprise and the FCI varies less.


                                                 9
    The unexplained part of the FCI’s move has effects of the standard sign. We trace a one-
standard-deviation move in it through our model, with no move in the two-year yield. Unemploy-
ment rises 0.03 percentage point after 12 months, and the core price level falls 0.02 to 0.03 percent
after 24 months at the r∗ step, both significant. These signs match those that Bauer et al. (2026)
and Boehm et al. (2026) find for policy-driven shifts in risk appetite, and the sizes are similar to
those of a one-standard-deviation policy surprise.
    There are two readings of this unexplained part. On one, it is mostly news unrelated to policy
that happens to arrive in the window, and our estimates capture the effects of policy. On the other,
much of it is policy news that does not move interest rates, as Boehm and Kroner (2024) and Bauer
et al. (2026) argue. Then our estimates are the effects of one dimension of policy news, and the
effects of all policy news through financial conditions could be up to about twice as large. Both
readings agree on the channel: policy news reaches the economy through broad financial conditions.
    Credit. Credit is the most important channel we leave out of the baseline (Gilchrist and
Zakrajšek, 2012; Caldara and Herbst, 2019). Appendix D adds it in two ways. Adding the daily
Baa-10-year spread, and letting it respond on the announcement day, barely changes the results:
at the r∗ step, unemployment rises 0.28 to 0.35 percentage point after 12 months and the price
level falls 0.48 to 0.65 percent after 24 months, significant for all lag lengths. Adding the monthly
excess bond premium as a third monthly variable leaves the results with the FCI almost unchanged.
But without the FCI, the premium removes the wrong-signed unemployment response. So credit
conditions offer a partial alternative to the FCI for the unemployment puzzle, though not for the
price result. One reading is that the premium, like the FCI, absorbs policy-driven moves in risk
premia (Bauer et al., 2026).
    The parts of the FCI. Replacing the FCI with its three weighted parts, entered separately,
leaves the signs unchanged but makes the estimates less precise. The price result is then significant
only with 12 lags. A test of the proportional-weights restriction does not reject it at the 5 percent
level for unemployment at any step, or for either variable at the r∗ step (Appendix D).
    Commodity prices and output. Commodity prices mark a limit of the approach. Appendix
Table A8 adds the IMF all-commodity price index. Commodity prices then rise after a tightening,
by 2.7 to 7.2 percent after 24 months at the r∗ step (not significant), and by more at the nominal
step. This differs from Boehm et al. (2026) and Bauer et al. (2026), who find that oil and commodity
prices fall after a contractionary shock. In this larger model, industrial production also rises rather
than falls, and the unemployment effect is smaller (0.16 to 0.25 percentage point after 12 months at
the r∗ step). The price-level effect is similar (0.30 to 0.62 percent after 24 months). Our assumptions
are least plausible for prices like these, which look ahead and respond strongly to demand.

moved. They respond from the next month on, through the monthly average of the daily financial
series. That average depends on when in the month the announcement falls: an announcement
late in a month affects few days of that month’s average. This is how the model takes the timing
of announcements into account, as Jacobson et al. (2026) recommend. The ordering is the famil-
iar recursive one, in which slow-moving macroeconomic variables come before financial variables
(Bernanke and Blinder, 1992; Christiano et al., 1999). It also means that the daily equations include
the current month’s unemployment and inflation, even though those figures are published later. We
treat this as a modeling convention: the monthly values summarize conditions that markets observe
as the month unfolds.


                                                     11
    The key assumptions. Two assumptions carry the results. The first is the usual one in
a VAR. Once the shock has been identified, the economy responds to a change in the financial
variables in the same way whatever caused it. A policy shock identified by a Cholesky ordering or
an external instrument is traced through the same estimated dynamics as every other shock, and
we do the same. This has a familiar cost. Stock prices partly anticipate future activity, so a move
that policy news pushes through stocks need not act on the economy like a move with another
cause. This is the same problem as the information effect in any VAR with a high-frequency shock;
as in the companion note, we acknowledge it and set it aside.
    The second assumption is specific to this paper and has two parts. A standard VAR estimates
the effect of the shock on every variable in the system. Here, the surprise reaches unemployment
and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and has no direct effect on them in the
window. Anything else it moves, such as credit spreads or expectations, is left out. So are the
moves in stock prices and the dollar in the window that are unrelated to sa ; the surprise explains
only about a third of those moves (Section 2.2.4). The non-yield shock of Boehm and Kroner
(2024) is uncorrelated with policy-news surprises, so such moves do not bias bF . But the weights
in equation (3) treat them as noise, and part of them may be policy news (Boehm and Kroner,
2024; Bauer et al., 2026). In addition, the monthly equations see the FCI as a single index. Its
weights show how the three components respond to policy news, not how the economy responds to
each. So we assume that the three affect unemployment and prices in proportion to those weights.
Section 2.2.4 and Appendix D relax both parts.

2026, unemployment, inflation and interest rates contain slow movements that a short monthly
model cannot capture. Rates drifted down along with r∗ . Inflation was low in the 2010s and rose
after 2020. The natural rate of unemployment may have shifted in ways the CBO estimate misses.
Without a trend, these slow movements show up in the estimated effect of the financial variables,
and trend gets mixed with transmission. A quadratic is the simplest smooth trend that allows
inflation to fall and then rise. Section 3.4 shows that measuring rates relative to r∗ does much of
the trend’s work.

3.1   Standard methods after the early 1980s
Standard identification schemes have not delivered conventional effects of monetary policy in sam-
ples that start after the early 1980s. Ramey (2016) makes the point with the specification of


                                                13
Christiano et al. (1999): estimated on 1965–1995 it gives the classic responses, but on 1983–2007
“the results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the un-
employment rate”; a version of the Romer–Romer hybrid VAR gives, on the same later sample,
contractionary shocks that “appear to be expansionary”, as Barakchian and Crowe (2013) found
for 1988–2007; and a proxy SVAR with the Romer–Romer shock as instrument, estimated on 1969–
2007, gives a “pronounced, statistically significant price puzzle” (her Figures 1 and 2). Her reading
is that, because policy has been conducted well, true monetary shocks are rare, and it is hard
to extract shocks that are not contaminated by the central bank’s foresight. Caldara and Herbst
(2019) find, for 1994–2007 with a proxy SVAR that uses high-frequency surprises, that without
a credit spread in the system the response of industrial production is weak and uncertain (about
−0.2 percent at the posterior mean, with a credible set that includes positive values), against about
−0.75 percent when the excess bond premium is added.

3.3    The implied inflation-unemployment tradeoff
Do the responses imply a good tradeoff for policy? Larger effects on both unemployment and prices
need not: what matters is the ratio. Following Barnichon and Mesters (2021), we define the Phillips
multiplier at horizon H as
                                                           H
                                         P̃H            1 X
                                 PH =        ,   ŨH =         Ũh ,                          (17)
                                         ŨH           12 h=1

where P̃H is the response of the core price level after H months, in percent, and ŨH is the cumulative
response of the unemployment gap, in percentage-point years. Because the price level cumulates
inflation, PH is the cumulative change in inflation per unit of cumulative unemployment. Its inverse,
−1/PH , is a sacrifice ratio: the percentage-point years of unemployment needed to lower the price
level 1 percent. A more negative PH means a steeper Phillips curve.
    ŨH can be close to zero, and then the ratio is not well defined. So we form 90 percent sets for
PH in the spirit of Anderson and Rubin (1949): the set contains every value P for which the 90
percent bootstrap interval of P̃H − P ŨH contains zero. The sets are computed exactly, not on a
grid, and can be unbounded, which shows when the data cannot pin down the tradeoff. Table 5
reports P24 and P36 for each step and for the main sample variations.

3.4     What accounts for the change
Table 4 can be read as a two-by-two comparison. Including the FCI is what gives a reliable
unemployment response. Measuring rates relative to r∗ is what gives a strong price response.
Without the FCI, the r∗ -gap two-year yield already gives a strong price effect, but its unemployment
effect is weak. With the nominal FCI, unemployment rises but prices fall less. Only with both
does the economy respond in the standard way on both counts. Section 4 reports how this pattern
changes with the sample, the adjustment of the yield and the time trend.
    Why does the FCI matter? The model assumes that policy reaches unemployment and prices
through the two-year yield and the FCI, the broad index of the 10-year yield, equity valuations and
the real dollar, weighted by their responses to policy news (Table 6). In the announcement window
the FCI moves with the two-year yield: a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage
point raises the FCI by 0.99 (in the same units). The monthly VAR of Section 3.1 has no FCI; if the
FCI matters for unemployment and responds to policy, the two-year yield alone omits a channel.
Figure 4 shows the responses of the two-year yield and the FCI to the policy move in the baseline.

5.1   Implications for policy
Policy has not lost power. Measured through broad financial conditions (longer-term yields,
equity prices and the dollar), a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point,
and tightens financial conditions by about as much for a time, raises unemployment about 0.4
percentage point within a year and lowers core prices about 0.6 percent within two (Section 3.2).
The unemployment effect is larger than in recent benchmarks and in a rough reading of FRB/US.
The price effect is similar to the cleaned-shock benchmark for two years and larger after that. The
weak or wrong-signed effects found with the nominal two-year yield alone reflect how the stance of
policy was measured, not a lack of power.
    Judge the stance by financial conditions relative to r∗ . Policy affects the economy
roughly in proportion to how much it moves broad financial conditions, and the right yardstick for
those conditions is the neutral rate r∗ , not zero or their past average. This supports the practice
of monitoring financial conditions when setting policy, as the FOMC did in 2023 (Powell, 2023).
It also explains why the same policy rate can be more or less restrictive at different times. Two
cautions apply. First, r∗ is uncertain, and our intervals hold it fixed. The Laubach–Williams
estimates we use are one-sided, but their parameters are estimated on the full sample, so they are
not what a policymaker could have known in real time. Second, with Kiley’s r∗ , the price effect is
somewhat weaker.
    The cost of disinflation. Measured relative to r∗ , lowering the core price level 1 percent
through tighter financial conditions costs about 1 percentage-point year of unemployment over two
years (for example, unemployment half a point higher for two years). Measured in nominal terms,
it would appear to cost two to three times as much, and with the two-year yield alone the cost
cannot be measured at all. So part of the apparent flattening of the Phillips curve, and of the
high cost of disinflation it implies, may reflect how the stance of policy has been measured. Two
cautions apply. The difference between the two measures is imprecise. And it rests on 2020–22:

before 2020, our estimates show no measurable tradeoff, in line with the flat post-1990 estimates.
That period may simply have lacked a tightening relative to r∗ large enough to reveal one.
   What this paper does not do. Our estimates are effects of surprises. They do not by
themselves say how much of the 2022–24 disinflation, or of the rise in unemployment, was caused
by the tightening; that requires a decomposition of the whole episode, which we leave to future
work. We also do not test whether tightenings and easings, or periods of high and low inflation,
have different effects.

5.2   Conclusion
Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements measure policy shocks well, at least
the part of policy news that moves interest rates. The hard part has been tracing their effects
on the economy. We keep the surprise as it is and change only how its effects are traced. Two
standard parts of the transmission mechanism explain much of the post-1994 puzzle: policy works
through financial conditions as a whole, and what matters for spending and inflation is the level
of rates and financial conditions relative to a slow-moving neutral rate. Traced this way, the
effects on unemployment, prices and industrial production have the standard signs and are precisely
estimated. The unemployment effect is larger than cleaned-shock benchmarks. The price effect
is similar for two years and larger after that. And the implied tradeoff between inflation and
unemployment is of conventional size: steeper than when conditions are measured in nominal
terms, and much steeper than recent estimates of a flat Phillips curve, though not steeper than
cleaned-shock benchmarks.
    The two parts play different roles. Within our design, the FCI, with its response measured
in the window, delivers the unemployment effect, in every version and sample we try. Measuring
conditions relative to r∗ delivers the price effect and the steeper tradeoff, which rest mainly on the
years since 2020, when the stance of policy relative to r∗ moved most.
    These findings complement work on information effects, predictable surprises, timing and policy-
driven risk appetite. That last line of work and ours agree that stock prices and the dollar carry
much of the effect of policy. They differ in whether to treat those moves as a separate shock or as
part of financial conditions. Several mechanisms can operate at once, and a natural next step is to
trace cleaned or separated surprises through our approach.
    Our results have limits. They rest on the assumption that the economy responds to financial
conditions the same way whatever moved them, and on the time trend. Commodity prices rise
after a tightening in our model. Credit conditions offer a partial alternative explanation of the
unemployment puzzle. And our intervals do not include uncertainty about r∗ or the FCI weights.
    The broader lesson is that the fall in r∗ over the past three decades is more than a fact about
the level of interest rates. Unless rates are measured relative to r∗ , that fall also makes the effects
of policy, and the cost of lowering inflation, harder to measure.

B      Construction and estimation details
Events. We use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the
window measures available in the U.S. Monetary Policy Event-Study Database (Acosta et al.,
2025). As in the companion note, we leave out unscheduled announcements with no change in the
policy rate, and March–April 2020. The one later announcement in the database, September 2026,
is left out because too few later days are available. The DXY index used in the window covers
fewer currencies than the broad dollar used in the daily and monthly index, which we treat as a
measurement difference.
    From monthly to daily. Expected inflation and r∗ are not available daily, and they move
slowly. We take the monthly gap between Rtv in equation (8) and yt2 , interpolate it linearly between
the middles of adjacent months, and add it to the daily yield. The daily index is

                         Fτv = wB yτ10 + wQ 100 ln CAPEτ + wD 100 ln DOLτ + ovτ ,                              (19)

where DOLτ is the daily nominal broad dollar. The offset ovτ is the monthly difference between
the index in equation (4) and the average of the first three terms, interpolated in the same way.

the daily series are very close to the monthly measures: the root-mean-square difference is at most

from day to day, so day-to-day moves in the index come from market prices. Table A15 shows the
measures used at each frequency. The daily calendar has every weekday; missing values, such as
on holidays, are filled from the previous observation for up to three days. Each daily equation has
13 coefficients with the FCI; each monthly equation has 3 + 4L.

D     Relaxing the key assumptions
Section 2.3 describes two restrictions specific to this paper. The surprise reaches unemployment
and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and the three parts of the FCI act in
proportion to their weights. This appendix relaxes each in turn, and then adds a monthly credit
measure to the monthly block. Table A17 repeats the main steps for the first two exercises. In
each exercise the daily model and the monthly equations are extended with the new variables.
Everything else is as in the baseline, including the quadratic trend and 200 draws.
    Adding a credit spread. The daily model and the monthly equations now include the Baa-
10-year spread: Moody’s Baa corporate yield minus the 10-year Treasury yield, available daily from
1986. It is a rough measure of credit conditions. Unlike the other variables in the model, it can
respond to the surprise on impact. It has no 30-minute series, so its impact is the change over the
announcement day regressed on the surprise, scaled to a 1 percentage point window move in the
two-year yield. The impact is −0.25 percentage point. A tightening narrows this spread on the
day, because corporate yields rise less than the 10-year Treasury yield. This is not the widening
that a credit channel would suggest. After the announcement the spread moves little: its monthly
average response stays between −0.16 and +0.01 percentage point over two years.
    The effects of the surprise barely change (Table A17). At the r∗ step, unemployment rises 0.28
to 0.35 percentage point after 12 months, compared with 0.36 to 0.40 in the baseline, and the price
level falls 0.48 to 0.65 percent after 24 months, compared with 0.53 to 0.72. All are significant at
the 90 percent level for every lag length. The nominal and real steps are also close to the baseline.
So leaving out this spread does not drive the results. The test is limited. The spread is crude,


                                                   45
because it includes expected default, and the excess bond premium that purges it is available only
monthly. A better measure may behave differently.
     Entering the parts of the FCI separately. The FCI is replaced by its three weighted parts:
the 10-year yield, equity valuation (CAPE) and the real dollar, each in the same step (nominal,
real or r∗ -gap) as the FCI. The parts sum to the FCI. Each enters the daily and monthly equations
with its own coefficients, so their effects on unemployment and prices are no longer tied to the
weights. Their impacts per 1 percentage point move in the two-year yield are 0.32, 0.39 and 0.27
index units (together 0.99, as for the FCI).
     Signs and broad size agree with the baseline (Table A17), but precision falls. At the r∗ step,
unemployment rises 0.48, 0.33 and 0.18 percentage point after 12 months with 3, 6 and 12 lags. The
first two are significant at the 90 percent level; the third is significant only at 68 percent. The price
level falls 0.30 percent after 12 months with 12 lags and 0.50 percent after 24 months, significant
at the 90 percent level; with 3 and 6 lags the 24-month falls are 0.24 and 0.41, significant only at
the 68 percent level at 6 lags. At the nominal and real steps, unemployment is smaller and less
precise at long lags, and prices barely move. The loss of precision is expected. The three parts add
2L coefficients to each equation, for 382 months of data.
     Table A19 tests the restriction directly. The FCI model is nested in the model with the parts,
so an F test applies. It does not reject for unemployment at any step (p-values 0.10 to 0.67). For
inflation it is rejected at the 5 percent level at the nominal step with every lag length and at the real
step with 12 lags. At the r∗ step it is not rejected at 5 percent (p-values 0.08 to 0.24). The tests are
plain F tests with many restrictions and no correction for serial correlation, so they are indicative.
Taken together, the FCI weights, which come from the response to policy news, summarize how
unemployment responds well. For inflation they are a worse summary when rates are not measured
relative to r∗ , which fits the main result that the r∗ step matters most for prices.
     Adding the monthly excess bond premium. The excess bond premium (Gilchrist and
Zakrajšek, 2012) is the part of corporate bond spreads not explained by default risk. It is monthly,
so it enters the monthly block as a third variable, treated like unemployment and inflation. It
depends on its own lags and on lagged monthly averages of the two-year yield and the FCI. Within
a month, it is set before the financial markets move, so the daily model responds to its current
and lagged values. Its response to the surprise is not set to anything: as for unemployment and
inflation, it is zero in the announcement window and then evolves with the model. Table A18 gives
the results.
     Without the FCI, the premium removes the wrong-signed unemployment response. At the
nominal step, unemployment at 12 months is −0.27 with 12 lags in the baseline and +0.17 with
the premium; at the real step it goes from −0.20 to +0.06. With the FCI, adding the premium
changes little. At the r∗ step, unemployment at 12 months is 0.39 to 0.41, compared with 0.36 to
0.40, and the price level at 24 months falls 0.52 to 0.71 percent, compared with 0.53 to 0.72. The
prices result at the r∗ step without the FCI is also unchanged. The premium itself barely responds
to the surprise: its response is within 0.13 percentage point at 12 months in every case, and is not
significant with the FCI. So the FCI and the premium are substitutes for fixing the unemployment
puzzle, but the FCI does not depend on the premium. Another reading is that the premium absorbs
policy-driven moves in risk appetite, as in Bauer et al. (2026). On that reading, the FCI and the
premium work in a similar way: both keep moves in risk premia from being attributed to interest
rates.
