How much are interest rates, stocks, house prices and the dollar helping or hurting U.S. growth?

The Federal Reserve Board's index, FCI‑G, turns recent moves in the fed funds rate, long‑term interest rates, stock prices, house prices and the dollar into one number: how much they add to or take from real GDP growth over the next year, in percentage points. Positive means a drag (tighter conditions). Negative means a boost. The Fed publishes it once a month; this page rebuilds it every weekday from public data.

How it works

The Fed's index adds up three‑month changes in each variable over the last 12 quarters (3 years), each multiplied by a weight the Fed publishes. The weights show how much a change made a given time ago still affects growth over the next year. A rise in interest rates or the dollar, or a fall in stock or house prices, pushes the index up (a drag).

  • Interest rates (fed funds, 10‑year, mortgage, BBB bond yield): change in the three‑month average.
  • Stocks and house prices: three‑month percentage change.
  • Dollar: percentage change in the three‑month average.

Are financial conditions tighter or easier than usual, and what changed?

Goldman Sachs publishes a financial conditions index that sits around 100. Higher means tighter conditions; lower means easier. It combines the fed funds rate, the 10‑year Treasury yield, the gap between corporate bond and Treasury yields, stock valuations and the dollar, using weights Goldman has published. This page rebuilds it every weekday from public data. The difference between two dates shows how much conditions tightened or eased in between.

How the index is built

Each part is multiplied by a weight Goldman has published, which reflects how much it matters for the economy, not how much it moves around. Interest rates and spreads are in percentage points. Stock valuations and the dollar are in percent (100 × the log change).

Index = 100 + 0.044 × fed funds rate + 0.451 × 10‑year Treasury yield + 0.396 × BBB bond spread − 0.049 × stock valuation (CAPE) + 0.060 × dollar (each measured as its difference from its 2000–2025 average)

The 2000–2025 averages are fixed, so past values do not change as new data arrive. The difference between two dates does not depend on that choice.

"One point" is how far a variable alone must move to change the index by one point.
