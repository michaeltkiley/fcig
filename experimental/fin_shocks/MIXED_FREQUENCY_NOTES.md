# Mixed frequencies and the identification of policy and financial shocks: literature and assumptions

31 Oct 2026. Tags: [read] = read in full text this project; [abstract] = checked against an abstract or catalogue page only; [recall] = from memory, not checked; [inference] = my own reasoning, not a citation. I read "identification is optional" as "identification is possible".

## 1. Short answer
- **The pieces exist, and the closest precedent is Faust, Swanson and Wright (2004)** [abstract] (futures-based high-frequency responses imposed on a VAR to identify the policy shock; the usual recursive identification is rejected). **I found no paper that combines the pieces in a mixed-frequency model.** There is a literature on temporal aggregation and structural inference, on mixed-frequency VARs, and on high-frequency (HF) identification with proxy SVARs and local projections. I searched for a paper that estimates a mixed-frequency VAR (MIDAS or state-space) in which intraday policy surprises identify the shock and monthly macro variables respond, and found none; that is a search gap, not proof that none exists.
- **Standard practice is to aggregate the HF surprise to the VAR frequency by a fixed linear rule and use it as an external instrument:** sum within the month (Miranda-Agrippino and Ricco; Bauer and Swanson) [read]; assign to the meeting month (Caldara and Herbst) [read]; cumulate over 31 days and average over the days of the month to match monthly-average rates (Gertler and Karadi) [read]. Bauer and Swanson criticise the 30-day moving average because it adds serial correlation and predictability [read].

## 2. Literature
- **Christiano and Eichenbaum (1987)** [abstract]: temporal aggregation bias can distort parameter estimates and hypothesis tests, including producing spurious Granger causality (money and output).
- **Marcellino (1999)** [recall; I could not confirm the title or abstract]: consequences of temporal aggregation for empirical (VAR) analysis.
- **Foroni and Marcellino (2014, JAE)** [abstract]: when the model's time scale and the data's differ, there are identification problems, estimation bias and distorted policy conclusions; a mixed-frequency estimation strategy alleviates them (tested on two monetary policy models).
- **Ghysels (2016, J. Econometrics)** [abstract]: a class of observation-driven mixed-frequency VARs (MIDAS-inspired parameterisations) measuring the effect of high-frequency data on low-frequency data and the reverse; characterises the misspecification of a common low-frequency VAR and of its impulse responses.
- **Schorfheide and Song (2015, JBES)** [abstract]: state-space mixed-frequency (monthly/quarterly) VAR, Bayesian with a Minnesota-type prior; evaluated for real-time forecasting.
- **Stock and Watson (2018)** [recall; conditions as summarised in Bauer and Swanson, read]: external instrument must be relevant, contemporaneously exogenous to the other shocks, and, for local projections, lead-lag exogenous.
- **Plagborg-Moller and Wolf (2021, Econometrica)** [abstract]: local projections and VARs estimate the same impulse responses; with an instrument ordered first in a recursive system, identification works even under non-invertibility.
- **Gertler and Karadi (2015)** [read]: VAR estimated over a long sample, instrument window shorter (1991-2012); monthly instrument built to match monthly-average rates.
- **Bauer and Swanson (2023)** [read]: monthly instrument is the sum of the month's surprises; end-of-month yields; orthogonalisation to pre-announcement data; more events raise relevance.
- **Miranda-Agrippino and Ricco (2021)** [read]: sum within the month, then remove the autoregressive component (12 lags) on non-zero months.
- **Nakamura and Steinsson (2018)** [read]: a longer window for the outcome "adds noise to the regression without biasing the coefficient" (event study, 30-minute shock window, one-day outcome).
- **Rigobon and Sack (2003, QJE)** [abstract]: identification through the heteroskedasticity of returns on policy days, applied to the reaction of policy to the stock market; the variance of monetary shocks is higher on FOMC days. **Faust, Swanson and Wright (2004, JME)** [abstract]: uses federal funds futures to measure the response of the expected path to the policy surprise and imposes it on a standard VAR.
- **Paul (2018)** [secondhand, from a search summary]: surprises as an exogenous regressor; consistent contemporaneous relative responses without zero restrictions. **Nunes (2023)** [search summary]: proxy SVAR separating monetary from central-bank-information shocks. **Kanzig (2021)** [recall]: daily oil-futures surprises aggregated to a monthly instrument.

## 3. When is identification possible? Assumptions by frequency pair
(HF = the policy-announcement window, minutes; the table is [inference] built on the sources above.)
| Frequency of the outcome | What is identified | Assumptions needed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same window (event study) | Asset-price effect of policy news | The surprise is policy news only; no other news in the window; prices efficient; futures risk premia do not move in the window | Nakamura-Steinsson 30 minutes; Rigobon-Sack heteroskedasticity needs weaker assumptions |
| Day | Same | Same; other-news noise grows but is uncorrelated with the surprise | Bauer-Swanson: orthogonalising changes event-study estimates little |
| Week (e.g. NFCI, weekly index) | Same, with overlapping-window inference | Same; HAC errors | |
| Month, proxy-SVAR or LP-IV | Dynamic macro effects | (i) relevance; (ii) contemporaneous exogeneity; (iii) lead-lag exogeneity for LP; (iv) the aggregation of the instrument matches the aggregation of the variable (monthly averages need exposure weights; end-of-month values do not); (v) no systematic policy shock in months with no announcement, or such shocks uncorrelated with the instrument (otherwise only relevance falls); (vi) the surprise is not predictable from public data that predates it (Bauer-Swanson) or the predictable part is removed; (vii) invertibility is needed for the SVAR-IV route, not if the instrument is ordered first in a recursive LP or VAR (Plagborg-Moller and Wolf) | Relevance falls with the window length (section 4) |
| Mixed-frequency VAR (state space or MIDAS-VAR) with an HF instrument | HF shock timing from the instrument; propagation from the model | The latent HF process is a linear time-invariant VAR; aggregation equations known (stock or flow); instrument exogenous and relevant at event dates; and the HF dynamics are themselves identified from the aggregated data | The instrument pins down the shock and its date but does not by itself identify HF propagation; aggregation can hide HF dynamics (Christiano-Eichenbaum; Foroni-Marcellino), so priors or restrictions on the HF VAR (as in Schorfheide-Song) are needed |
**Gain from keeping event dates (mixed-frequency) rather than summing within the month [inference]:** the instrument is non-zero on only about 8 days a year; the monthly sum discards the date, so the exposure of a monthly average to the shock (the share of the month remaining) is lost; Gertler-Karadi's cumulation restores it. In this project the exposure-weighted version did not raise the first stage (`mp_var_fci/04b_why_weak.log`: NS 3.5 to 2.9).

## 4. Evidence from this project's data: relevance by outcome window (`ladder.py`, `ladder.log`)
NS surprise (253 events, 1994-2026), outcome = change from the close before the event to the close h trading days after the event day, slope b, first-stage F, R2:
| Outcome | h = 0 | h = 1 | h = 5 | h = 10 | h = 21 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2-year yield (pp) | +0.88, F 56, 42% | +0.91, F 37, 28% | +0.75, F 19, 11% | +0.90, F 12, 7% | +0.72, F 4.4, 2.3% |
| 10-year yield (pp) | +0.42, F 20, 10% | +0.51, F 8.6, 7% | +0.55, F 6.4, 4.5% | +0.66, F 5.2, 3.8% | +0.43, F 1.2, 0.7% |
| GS-style FCI (index points) | +0.54, F 27, 15% | +1.04, F 45, 20% | +1.02, F 26, 9.5% | +1.41, F 37, 12% | +1.36, F 11, 5.2% |
Monthly counterpart (monthly-average 2-year change on this month's NS sum and last month's): +1.07 (t 3.3) and +0.47 (t 1.5), R2 5.8%.
**Reading.** The slope barely changes with the window (the effect persists), while the signal-to-noise falls roughly in proportion to the window length: at one month the 2-year F is about 4, the same order as the VAR-residual first stage of 3 to 6 in `mp_var_fci`. The weak monthly first stage is therefore a frequency-aggregation property of an event-based instrument, not a defect of its construction. The broad index (GS-style) keeps F above 10 out to a month because its response grows with the horizon.

## 5. Implication and a possible fifth proposal (P5, optional; not started)
**P5. Frequency ladder and bridge.** (i) Identify the financial effect at weekly frequency where the instrument is strong (index, 2-year, NFCI weekly); (ii) bridge to monthly macro by using the identified HF index response as the instrument for the index in a monthly unemployment equation or LP (LP-IV with the index as the endogenous regressor); (iii) optionally a small mixed-frequency model (weekly financial block, monthly macro) in the Schorfheide-Song or MIDAS-VAR style as a feasibility check, with the identifying assumptions of section 3. Risk: the bridge reuses the same weak monthly link, so it may only relocate the weakness; value: it shows exactly where identification is lost as the frequency falls.
