\begin{abstract}
\noindent Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy, but estimates of its effects have been unreliable. In samples that start in the mid-1990s, standard methods applied to surprises around Federal Reserve announcements give unemployment that falls after a tightening and prices that do not respond. I keep the surprises and change how their effects are carried through the economy. I measure how the two-year yield and a broad financial conditions index move in the half hour around each announcement, and carry those moves forward in a daily and a monthly model. Both are measured relative to the neutral rate. Carried through the two-year yield alone, the surprises give the familiar wrong signs. Carried through the index relative to the neutral rate, a tightening raises unemployment and lowers the price level, and the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response is of conventional size. The main results hold across alternative samples, measures of the neutral rate, trends, credit conditions and surprises.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy. Its use rests on estimates of how much a change in policy moves unemployment and inflation. In samples that start after the early 1980s, standard methods often give a surprise tightening small effects on unemployment, or effects of the wrong sign \citep{ramey2016}. Two concerns follow. One is that policy has become less powerful \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010}. The other is that inflation responds little to slack, so that lowering inflation requires a large and lasting rise in unemployment \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020}. I ask whether these findings reflect how policy works or how its effects are measured.

The question matters in practice. From 2022 to 2023 the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by more than 5 percentage points. Policymakers expected costs: in August 2022 the Chair warned of ``some softening of labor market conditions'' and called these ``the unfortunate costs of reducing inflation'' \citep{powell2022}. They also described policy as working through broad financial conditions: in November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}. The standard tools for measuring the effects of policy leave those conditions out.

The answer is that measurement matters. I use the policy-news surprise of \citet{nakamura2018} around 252 FOMC announcements from 1996 to 2026, and I change only how its effects are carried through the economy. Carried through the two-year yield alone, the surprises give the result of the standard methods: unemployment falls after a tightening, by \blabs{st:c2y96:u:12} percentage point after a year in a recursive VAR and \blabs{st:prox:u:12} in a proxy VAR, and prices do not respond. Carried through a broad financial conditions index, with the index and the yield both measured relative to the neutral rate $\rs$, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises unemployment by \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} percentage point after two years and lowers the core price level by \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24} percent. Lowering the price level 1 percent costs about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment (one point of unemployment for one year), a conventional sacrifice ratio. These responses have the signs that theory predicts, where the standard methods give the wrong ones (Figure~\ref{fig:intro}). In the terms of \citet{barnichon2021}, the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} after three years, against about $-0.15$ for 1990--2008 in their estimates.

The approach has three features.

First, I measure the effect of each surprise in a short window around the announcement (30 to about 100 minutes), in which unemployment and inflation cannot move. A daily model then traces how the two-year yield and financial conditions evolve after each announcement, and monthly unemployment and core inflation respond to their monthly averages, as in a standard vector autoregression (VAR). As in \citet{jacobson2026}, the model takes into account when each announcement falls within its month.

Second, financial conditions enter as a whole \citep{boivin2010}. The financial conditions index (FCI) combines the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the real dollar, weighted by their responses to policy news. A policy surprise raises the FCI about 1 unit per percentage point on the two-year yield. An ordinary monthly rise of 1 percentage point in the two-year yield comes with a rise of only \bl{ord:real} to \bl{ord:rstar} unit. A model with the yield alone therefore traces the ordinary move in the yield, which carries much less of the change in financial conditions than a policy surprise does.

Finally, I measure the yield and the index relative to $\rs$, the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. The neutral rate fell from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}, and interest rates trended down with it while activity trended up, so a model that links the level of rates to activity partly picks up these opposite trends. Inflation depends on how long real rates stay above or below $\rs$, so the adjustment should matter most for prices. The FCI and the three versions (nominal, real, then relative to $\rs$) come from a companion note on financial conditions and were fixed before this analysis \citep{fcinote}.

Earlier fixes improve the surprise or the timing of the outcome. I leave both alone and change the channel: policy reaches the economy through long rates, stock prices and the dollar, and these have to be measured relative to $\rs$. The main results hold across alternative samples, measures of $\rs$, trends, credit conditions and surprises (Section~\ref{sec:rob}).

\subsection{Connection to the literature}
\textbf{Surprises and their effects.} Interest-rate surprises around FOMC announcements were meant to settle the measurement problem: the decision precedes the announcement, so market moves in a narrow window around it cannot have caused it \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005}. They measure the effects of policy on asset prices precisely \citep{bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018,swanson2021}, but for unemployment and inflation the old puzzles often return. Four explanations come with fixes. Announcements reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and separating it gives larger price effects \citep{romer2000,nakamura2018,jarocinski2020,miranda2021}. Part of each surprise is predictable, and removing it sharpens the estimates \citep{bauer2023,bauer2023aer}. Markets react in minutes while unemployment and inflation are monthly averages, and measuring inflation daily removes most of the wrong sign \citep{jacobson2026}. And credit costs, stock prices and the dollar carry part of the effect \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019,kroencke2021,bauer2023jep,boehm2024,bauer2026,boehm2026}. Most of this work traces the surprise through a single nominal interest rate. The choice of surprise series \citep{bu2021,brennan2025} and of lags \citep{coibion2012} can matter. Futures-based surprises in monthly models go back to \citet{faust2004}, and related work on timing and mixed frequencies includes \citet{kilian2024,chudik2022,francis2011,ghysels2016,gazzani2020,chudik2026}.

\textbf{What is new.} My paper is closest in spirit to \citet{jacobson2026}. Like them, I hold the surprise and the method fixed and change one measurement. They change how often the outcome is measured; I change how the channel from policy to the economy is measured, and my main result is for activity. \citet{bauer2023} and \citet{jarocinski2020} improve the surprise itself. In my design a surprise enters only through its immediate effect on the two-year yield and the FCI and through its timing, so their fixes and mine can in principle be combined.

\textbf{Financial channels and risk appetite.} \citet{gertler2015} and \citet{caldara2019} are closest in emphasizing financial channels. I use a broad index measured relative to $\rs$, and the baseline leaves credit spreads out. Studies of the ``non-yield'' shock treat policy-driven moves in stock prices and the dollar as a second shock \citep{bauer2026,boehm2024,boehm2026}; I treat them as part of financial conditions, and keep only the part of those moves that comes with interest-rate news. The response of stock prices to long rates has also changed over time \citep{kiley2014}. \citet{caballero2026} propose a neutral level of the FCI itself, FCI$^*$. I keep $\rs$ for the rates inside the two-year yield and the index; gaps to FCI$^*$ are a natural extension.

\textbf{The power of policy and the Phillips curve.} The effects of policy shocks became smaller after the early 1980s, which the literature attributes to a more systematic policy response and better-anchored expectations \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010,primiceri2005}, or to a failure of standard identification once policy became more forward-looking \citep{barakchian2013}. Recent work finds a small Phillips-curve slope \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020,ball2019,fitzgerald2024}, hard to identify when policy offsets demand shocks \citep{mcleay2020} and understated by conventional methods \citep{barnichon2020}. Policy shocks offer one way to identify it. \citet{barnichon2021} measure the tradeoff by the ratio of cumulative inflation to cumulative unemployment after such shocks and find it much flatter after 1990 than before. I add evidence of this kind and show that the measured tradeoff depends on how the stance of policy is measured.


\section{Empirical approach}\label{sec:data}
The model has three pieces. A surprise moves the two-year yield and the FCI by amounts measured in the window: the vector $b$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. A daily VAR, equation~\eqref{eq:daily}, carries those two series forward. Their monthly averages then enter a monthly VAR for unemployment and inflation, equation~\eqref{eq:monthly}. Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the construction of the series, the algorithm for tracing responses and the bootstrap, and Appendix~\ref{app:data} lists the data.

\subsection{Measuring financial conditions}\label{sec:measure}
The FCI is the market index of \citet{fcinote}: a weighted sum of the 10-year Treasury yield, stock valuations and the real broad dollar. Indexes of this kind are weighted sums of financial variables \citep{hatzius2010}. They differ mainly in where the weights come from. FCI-G takes them from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}. Mine come from the event-study regressions long used to measure how asset prices respond to policy \citep{kuttner2001,bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018}.

\textbf{Policy news.} Let $\Delta X_a$ be the $5\times 1$ vector of changes in the current- and next-meeting federal funds futures and the second to fourth eurodollar futures in the window around announcement $a$. Following \citet{nakamura2018}, let $p_a$ be the first principal component of these changes, signed so that it moves with the fourth eurodollar future. Policy news is $p_a$ rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield $y^{2}$ by 1 percentage point:
\begin{equation}
s_a = \gamma\, p_a, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\sum_a p_a\, \Delta y^{2}_a}{\sum_a p_a^{2}}. \label{eq:ns}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Weights.} Let $\Delta A_a$ be the vector of changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar index (DXY) in the window. The companion note treats these changes as
\begin{equation}
\Delta A_a = \psi\, s_a + \eta_a, \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ holds the policy responses and $\eta_a$ is other news, with covariance matrix $\Sigma$. It estimates $\psi$ by regressing each element of $\Delta A_a$ on $s_a$ and a constant, and $\Sigma$ from the residuals, over 253 announcements from January 1996 to September 2026. The weights are those that recover the policy component most precisely (one-factor generalized least squares):
\begin{equation}
w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\psi}{\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi}. \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Because $w'\psi=1$ and $s_a$ is scaled to the two-year yield, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit. The weights are 0.61 on the 10-year yield, $-0.051$ per percent on stock valuations and 0.065 per percent on the dollar. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy.

\textbf{Three versions.} In month $t$, the index in version $v$ is
\begin{equation}
F_t^{v} = w_B\, B_t^{v} + w_Q\, Q_t^{v} + w_D\, D_t, \label{eq:fci}
\end{equation}
measured as a deviation from its sample mean. Here $D_t$ is 100 times the log of the real broad dollar in every version. With $y^{10}_t$ the 10-year yield, $\pi^{10}_t$ 10-year expected inflation, and $(E/P)_t$ the inverse of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE), the other two terms are:
\begin{align}
\text{Nominal:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:nom}\\
\text{Real:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:real}\\
\text{$\rs$-gap (baseline):}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t - \rs_t, & Q_t &= -\frac{100}{\overline{E/P}}\left[(E/P)_t - \rs_t\right]. \label{eq:rstar}
\end{align}
In the $\rs$-gap version, stocks are measured by the gap between their earnings yield and $\rs$. The term $100/\overline{E/P}$, with $\overline{E/P}$ the sample average, converts this gap to the units of the log CAPE (the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio), so that the weight on stocks is the same in every version. CAPE is a real valuation measure, so stocks do not change between the nominal and real versions. For $\rs$ I use the New York Fed's current Laubach--Williams estimates \citep{holston2023}. These ``one-sided'' estimates use only data up to each date, with the model's parameters estimated on the full sample.

\textbf{The two-year yield.} The two-year yield is adjusted in the same three versions. With $y^{2}_t$ the nominal yield and $\pi^{2}_t$ two-year expected inflation \citep[from the Cleveland Fed;][]{haubrich2012},
\begin{equation}
R_t^{\text{nominal}} = y^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\text{real}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\rs\text{-gap}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t - \rs_t. \label{eq:twoyear}
\end{equation}
Expected inflation and $\rs$ are monthly or quarterly and move slowly. The daily series add their interpolated monthly values to daily market prices, so day-to-day moves come from markets (Appendix~\ref{app:details}).


\subsection{Why financial conditions and $r^*$}\label{sec:background}
Policy reaches spending and prices through many interest rates, asset prices and the dollar. A standard VAR with one interest rate can miss most of that route, and the omission can produce the puzzles.

\subsubsection{The standard transmission mechanism}
Monetary policy sets the short-term interest rate today and, through its communications, shapes what people expect that rate to be in the future. Few households and businesses borrow at the policy rate. They face the rates on consumer credit, mortgages and business debt, which arbitrage links to current and expected short-term rates. The same rise in rates raises the return investors require on stocks. For businesses, a higher cost of capital raises the user cost of new equipment and buildings, and investment falls \citep{jorgenson1963,hall1967}. The value of existing capital relative to its replacement cost falls as well \citep{tobin1969}. For households, lower stock prices lower wealth, and consumption depends on wealth as well as on current income \citep{ando1963,friedman1957}. Higher rates also strengthen the dollar, which shifts spending toward imports and reduces exports \citep{fleming1962,mundell1963}.

Interest rates of many maturities, equity prices and the dollar therefore form the core of the transmission mechanism \citep{boivin2010}. The same links appear in modern models of the business cycle, in closed and open economies \citep{christiano2005,smets2007,gali2005}. Models with heterogeneous households give more weight to the indirect effects that work through income \citep{kaplan2018,auclert2019}. But in all of these models the short-term rate matters mainly because of what it does to the prices and rates that people actually face. The effects come with a lag \citep{friedman1961}.

What matters in these models is not the nominal rate but the real rate relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. The same holds for the other parts of financial conditions: a given yield or stock valuation is tighter when $\rs$ is low than when it is high. The distinction matters most for inflation. Spending responds to financial conditions within months. Inflation responds to the slack that builds up over time, so it depends on how long, and by how much, conditions stay tight relative to $\rs$. A measure of conditions that ignores a falling $\rs$ will overstate how tight policy has been, and by more the longer the period. That error is largest for the cumulative effects that drive inflation.

\subsubsection{What standard VARs leave out}
The workhorse evidence on monetary policy comes from VARs with a few macroeconomic variables and one interest rate \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. Some studies add a credit spread \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019}, and factor models bring in many more series \citep{bernanke2005favar}. But long-term yields, equity valuations and the dollar are usually absent from the small models used to measure the effects of policy, although some recent work adds the dollar and stock-market volatility \citep{boehm2026}. These are the variables that, on the mechanism above, carry those effects. Concern about missing variables is not new: \citet{sims1992} argued that the price puzzle arises because the Federal Reserve responds to information that small VARs do not contain \citep[see also][]{castelnuovo2010}. My concern is different. Here the missing variables are the channel itself.

A simple two-equation model (Appendix~\ref{app:toy}) shows what this omission can do. If unemployment responds to financial conditions, and financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, a VAR in unemployment and the policy rate lets the rate stand in for financial conditions. Its error contains past financial shocks, which a VAR with few lags cannot capture, so the VAR is misspecified \citep[on missing variables in VARs, see][]{fernandez2007} and the effects of policy are harder to measure.

A second problem matters more for the size and sign of the response: the omission changes the experiment being carried through. A policy surprise that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the FCI about 1 unit in the window. An ordinary rise of 1 percentage point in the yield comes with a rise of only \bl{ord:real} to \bl{ord:rstar} unit in the real and $\rs$ versions (\bl{ord:nom} in the nominal version; Appendix~\ref{app:details}). A VAR without financial conditions carries through the ordinary move in the yield, which comes with a much smaller change in financial conditions than a policy surprise does.


High-frequency surprises help identify the shock, but they do not by themselves fix how it is carried forward. My design therefore puts financial conditions in the system, so that they carry the effects of the surprise forward, and measures the immediate effect of the surprise on the two-year yield and on financial conditions directly in the window, where policy news is the main force at work. The measurement of financial conditions also matters. If activity responds to conditions relative to $\rs$, a measure that ignores $\rs$ differs from the true one by a slow-moving error, and that error enters the equation for unemployment.

\subsubsection{Financial conditions and real activity}
A separate literature asks how financial conditions are related to economic activity. Asset prices help forecast output and inflation, but which ones help changes over time \citep{stock2003}. Credit spreads, and especially their excess bond premium component, are strong predictors of activity \citep{gilchrist2012}. Broad indexes summarize many such series and are used to assess the outlook \citep{hatzius2010,brave2011}. Financial conditions are also linked to the risks around growth, not only to its average \citep{adrian2019}, and their relationship with unemployment may be nonlinear \citep{kiley2020ml}. Federal Reserve staff now publish a measure of the effect of financial conditions on growth, with weights from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}.

This work has a limit that the event-study literature does not. A correlation between financial conditions and later activity need not reflect a causal effect, because financial conditions also respond to the economy. \citet{hatzius2010} address part of this problem by removing the effects of past growth and inflation from their indicators. The companion note uses the timing in a VAR for the same purpose \citep{fcinote}. The two literatures have complementary strengths. Work on high-frequency surprises identifies policy shocks cleanly but carries them through a small set of variables. Work on financial conditions studies a broad set of variables but does not isolate policy shocks. I bring them together: the weights in my index come from how its components respond to policy news, and the surprise is carried forward through the estimated relationship between that index and the economy.


\subsubsection{Policy news beyond interest rates, and risk appetite}\label{sec:scope}
Recent work finds that announcements move stock prices and the dollar much more than interest-rate surprises can explain \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. My windows show the same pattern. The policy-news surprise explains \bl{r2:y2} percent of the variance of the move in the two-year yield, but only \bl{r2:y10} percent for the 10-year yield, \bl{r2:sp} percent for the S\&P 500, \bl{r2:usd} percent for the dollar and \bl{r2:fci} percent for the FCI. The part of the FCI's move that the surprise does not explain is therefore large.

There are two readings of this unexplained part. On one, it is mostly news unrelated to policy that happens to arrive in the window, and my estimates capture the effects of policy. On the other, much of it is policy news that does not move interest rates, as \citet{boehm2024} and \citet{bauer2026} argue. Then my estimates are the effects of one dimension of policy news, and the effects of all policy news through financial conditions could be larger. Both readings agree on the channel: policy news reaches the economy through broad financial conditions.

\subsection{Timing assumptions and identification}\label{sec:timing}
The shock is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy-news surprise, $s_a$ in equation~\eqref{eq:ns}, from the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The window runs from 10 minutes before the statement to 60 minutes after the press conference starts, or 30 minutes around the statement when there is no press conference. Identification comes from timing, at three frequencies. Table~\ref{tab:timing} summarizes the assumptions.

\textbf{The window.} The window move in the FCI is computed with the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F_a = w_B\, \Delta y^{10}_a + w_Q\, \Delta \text{SP}_a + w_D\, \Delta \text{DXY}_a. \label{eq:winfci}
\end{equation}
These are the measures available within the day, and the ones used to estimate the weights. Percent changes in the S\&P 500 equal percent changes in CAPE within a window, because earnings are fixed. I express all effects per percentage point on the two-year yield in the window. The effect on the FCI is the ratio of two regression slopes on the surprise, an instrumental-variables estimate with $s_a$ as the instrument:
\begin{equation}
b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ b_F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b_F = \frac{\sum_a (\Delta F_a - \overline{\Delta F})\,(s_a-\bar s)}{\sum_a (\Delta y^{2}_a - \overline{\Delta y^{2}})\,(s_a-\bar s)} = 0.99. \label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
This is close to one because the weights are normalized that way. It is not exactly one because this regression includes a constant, which the scaling in equation~\eqref{eq:ns} does not, and because it uses one fewer announcement. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly, so I take the window move to be the same in every version.

\textbf{The day.} On the announcement day, the two-year yield and the FCI shift by the vector $b$. I use the window move rather than the change over the whole day, because the full-day change contains other news. On later days, the two series move as equation~\eqref{eq:daily} implies. They affect each other only through the daily lags, so no ordering between them is needed within the day. The window fixes their joint move.

\textbf{The month.} Within each month, unemployment and inflation are set first, and markets then respond to them. So in the month of the announcement, unemployment and inflation have not moved. They respond from the next month on, through the monthly average of the daily financial series. That average depends on when in the month the announcement falls: an announcement late in a month affects few days of that month's average. This is how the model takes the timing of announcements into account, as \citet{jacobson2026} recommend. The ordering is the familiar recursive one, in which slow-moving macroeconomic variables come before financial variables \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. It also means that the daily equations include the current month's unemployment and inflation, even though those figures are published later. I treat this as a modeling convention: the monthly values summarize conditions that markets observe as the month unfolds.

\textbf{The key assumptions.} Two assumptions carry the results. The first is the usual one in a VAR. Once the shock has been identified, the economy responds to a change in the financial variables in the same way whatever caused it. A policy shock identified by a Cholesky ordering or an external instrument is carried through the same estimated dynamics as every other shock, and I do the same. This has a familiar cost. Stock prices partly anticipate future activity, so a move that policy news pushes through stocks need not act on the economy like a move with another cause. This is the same problem as the information effect in any VAR with a high-frequency shock; as in the companion note, I set it aside.

The second assumption is specific to this paper and has two parts. A standard VAR estimates the effect of the shock on every variable in the system. Here, the surprise reaches unemployment and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and has no direct effect on them in the window. Anything else it moves, such as credit spreads or expectations, is left out. So are the moves in stock prices and the dollar in the window that are unrelated to $s_a$; the surprise explains only about a third of those moves (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). The non-yield shock of \citet{boehm2024} is uncorrelated with policy-news surprises, so such moves do not bias $b_F$. But the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights} treat them as noise, and part of them may be policy news \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. In addition, the monthly equations see the FCI as a single index. Its weights show how the three components respond to policy news, not how the economy responds to each. So I assume that the three affect unemployment and prices in proportion to those weights.


\subsection{A mixed-frequency VAR}\label{sec:midas}
\textbf{Structure.} The model has a daily block and a monthly block. Let $x_\tau=(R_\tau,F_\tau)'$ hold the two-year yield and the FCI on day $\tau$, in the same version. Let $m_t=(U_t,\pi_t)'$ hold the two monthly variables. The unemployment gap $U_t$ is the unemployment rate minus the CBO estimate of its natural rate. The CBO estimate does not respond to the surprise, so the response of the gap equals that of the unemployment rate. Inflation is $\pi_t=1200\,\Delta\ln P_t$, where $P_t$ is the price index for personal consumption expenditures excluding food and energy (core PCE).

The daily block is a VAR that uses averages of the past day, week, month and quarter as regressors \citep{corsi2009}:
\begin{equation}
x_\tau = \alpha + \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_k\, \bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, m_{t(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, m_{t(\tau)-1} + \xi_\tau, \label{eq:daily}
\end{equation}
where $\bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)}$ is the average of $x$ over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$, and $t(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$. Averages over about a day, a week, a month and a quarter capture persistence with few coefficients. When the FCI is left out, $x_\tau$ is the two-year yield alone.

The monthly block is
\begin{equation}
m_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, m_{t-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \bar x_{t-l} + \zeta_t, \qquad \bar x_{t}=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{\tau\in t} x_\tau, \label{eq:monthly}
\end{equation}
with $n_t$ the number of weekdays in month $t$. The model is a mixed-frequency VAR \citep{ghysels2016}. The equation contains no current-month term in $\bar x_t$, which is the timing assumption of Table~\ref{tab:timing}.

A monthly VAR with the same variables and a recursive ordering is the special case in which every announcement falls on the first day of its month (Appendix~\ref{app:relation}).

\textbf{The time trend.} The baseline monthly equations contain a constant and no time trend. Over 1996--2026, unemployment, inflation and interest rates contain slow movements that a short monthly model may not capture: rates drifted down along with $\rs$, inflation was low in the 2010s and rose after 2020, and the natural rate of unemployment may have shifted in ways the CBO estimate misses.

\textbf{Estimation and inference.} I estimate each block by ordinary least squares, on the same sample: January 1996, the month of the first announcement with all window measures, to September 2026 (\bl{nobs} months, and the weekdays of those months for the daily block). The lagged daily averages and the monthly lags may use earlier observations. 2020 is kept in both blocks. The baseline has $L=12$ monthly lags, fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:lags} shows 3 and 6). Responses are computed recursively, as deviations from a path with no surprise, and averaged over the positions of the announcements within their months (Appendix~\ref{app:details}); the response of the price level after $h$ months is $\sum_{j=0}^{h}\tilde\pi_j/12$. Intervals come from a block bootstrap with 500 draws \citep{kunsch1989,jentsch2019} that resamples calendar months in blocks of 12 and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn (Appendix~\ref{app:boot}). A response is significant at the 5 or 10 percent level when its 95 or 90 percent interval excludes zero. I report 90 percent intervals, as recent studies in this area do \citep{bauer2023,caldara2019,miranda2021,ramey2016}, and 68 percent intervals, which \citet{jarocinski2020} report alongside them. The FCI weights and $\rs$ are held fixed.

\subsection{Standard methods after the early 1980s}\label{sec:standard}
Standard identification schemes have not delivered conventional effects of monetary policy in samples that start after the early 1980s. \citet{ramey2016} estimates the specification of \citet{christiano1999} on 1983--2007 and finds that ``the results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the unemployment rate.'' A hybrid VAR with the Romer--Romer shock \citep{romer2004} gives, on the same later sample, contractionary shocks that ``appear to be expansionary,'' as \citet{barakchian2013} found for 1988--2007, and a proxy SVAR with the Romer--Romer shock, estimated on 1969--2007, gives a ``pronounced, statistically significant price puzzle'' (her Figures~1 and~2). \citet{caldara2019} find, for 1994--2007 with a proxy SVAR that uses high-frequency surprises, that without a credit spread the response of industrial production is weak and uncertain (about $-0.2$ percent, with a credible set that includes positive values), against about $-0.75$ percent when the excess bond premium is added.

I start from the same two standard methods, estimated on my own data and units, so that they can be compared directly with the model below. Both use the monthly unemployment gap, core PCE inflation and the end-of-month two-year Treasury yield, with 12 lags and no trend. The first is the recursive (Cholesky) VAR of \citet{christiano1999}, with the policy rate ordered last, so that unemployment and inflation do not respond to it within the month. The second is a proxy VAR \citep{stock2018,mertens2013,gertler2015}, which uses the surprise as an instrument for the policy rate: the instrument is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy news \citep{nakamura2018} summed over the announcements of the month, the same 252 announcements used throughout. All responses are to a 1 percentage point rise in the policy rate. Figure~\ref{fig:standard} and Table~\ref{tab:standard} show the results.

In both, unemployment \emph{falls} after a tightening: by \blabs{st:c2y96:u:12} percentage point after 12 months in the Cholesky VAR and by \blabs{st:prox:u:12} in the proxy VAR. The responses are still negative after 24 months, and the Cholesky VAR gives the same answer when the sample starts in 1984 (\bl{st:c2y84:u:12} after 12 months). The price level does not respond in any of them. With the federal funds rate on a 1984--2007 sample, close to the 1983--2007 sample of \citet{ramey2016}, the Cholesky VAR gives the same sign in smaller numbers (\bl{st:cffr:u:12} after 12 months), because a funds-rate innovation is short-lived. Adding a commodity price index, the standard remedy for the price puzzle \citep{sims1992,ramey2016}, leaves the two-year-yield response negative (\bl{st:c2ycom:u:12}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:standardcom}). The first-stage $F$ statistic of the proxy VAR is \bl{st:prox:F}, in the range in which weak-instrument problems matter \citep{lewis2026,bauer2026}, so I report exact Anderson--Rubin sets \citep{montiel2021,lunsford2015}, intervals that stay valid when the instrument is weak. These are the responses when the surprises are carried through the two-year yield alone.

\subsection{The implied inflation-unemployment tradeoff}\label{sec:tradeoff}
What matters for policy is the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response. Following \citet{barnichon2021}, I measure it by the Phillips multiplier at horizon $H$, the cumulative price response per unit of cumulative unemployment response:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}_H = \frac{\tilde P_H}{\tilde U_H}, \qquad \tilde U_H=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\tilde U_h, \label{eq:pm}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde P_H$ is the response of the core price level after $H$ months, in percent, and $\tilde U_H$ is the cumulative response of the unemployment gap, in percentage-point years. Because the price level cumulates inflation, $\mathcal{P}_H$ is the cumulative change in inflation per unit of cumulative unemployment. Its inverse, $-1/\mathcal{P}_H$, is a sacrifice ratio: the percentage-point years of unemployment needed to lower the price level 1 percent. A more negative $\mathcal{P}_H$ means a steeper Phillips curve.

$\tilde U_H$ can be close to zero, and then the ratio is not well defined. So I form 90 percent sets for $\mathcal{P}_H$ in the spirit of \citet{anderson1949}: the set contains every value $\mathcal{P}$ for which the 90 percent bootstrap interval of $\tilde P_H-\mathcal{P}\,\tilde U_H$ contains zero. The sets are computed exactly, not on a grid, and can be unbounded, which shows when the data cannot pin down the tradeoff. Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} reports $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{36}$ for each version and for the main sample variations.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_tradeoff}

The tradeoff can be measured in the $\rs$ version with the FCI. With the two-year yield and FCI relative to $\rs$, $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24} with a 90 percent set of [\bllo{tr:rstar:yes:24}, \blhi{tr:rstar:yes:24}] and $\mathcal{P}_{36}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} with [\bllo{tr:rstar:yes:36}, \blhi{tr:rstar:yes:36}]; both sets exclude zero. A sacrifice ratio is then \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment per 1 percent of price level after 24 months.

Elsewhere the data pin the tradeoff down less well. In the nominal and real versions with the FCI, and for the yield alone relative to $\rs$, the sets are unbounded: the cumulative unemployment response is too close to zero to pin down the ratio, which shows that the adjustment for $\rs$ matters for the tradeoff. With the nominal or real yield alone the multiplier is close to zero and the sets include zero: a tightening that does not raise unemployment says nothing about the cost of lowering inflation. Across the variations in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}, the set excludes zero when 2020 is dropped (\bl{trs:R1b:24} after 24 months from 1996 and \bl{trs:R1c:24} from 1973) and, after 24 months, for the sample from 1973 that ends in 2019. It is unbounded for the sample from 1973 and for the sample from 1996 that ends in 2019. With Kiley's $\rs$ the set from 1996 includes zero, and with the spline trend and real measures it is unbounded. The last row gives the difference between the multipliers of the version relative to $\rs$ and the nominal version; its interval includes zero.

For comparison, \citet{barnichon2021} report a multiplier of about $-0.15$ after three to four years for 1990--2008, estimated with high-frequency surprises, and a multiplier that diverges before 1990 and is about $-3$ after five years, estimated with Romer--Romer shocks. Their multiplier is the ratio of the cumulative response of inflation to the cumulative response of unemployment, as in equation~\eqref{eq:pm}. At the baseline horizons it lies between the two: \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} after three years.

\subsection{Estimating the impact from monthly data}\label{sec:monthlyvar}
A standard proxy VAR can also carry the FCI. It estimates the immediate effect of the surprise on every variable from monthly residuals and the instrument, instead of measuring it in the window. A window-impact VAR takes the effect on the two-year yield and the FCI from the windows, as the main model does, and sets the effect on unemployment and inflation to zero within the month. Table~\ref{tab:monthlycompact} gives the results for the yield and FCI relative to $\rs$; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:mainmonthly} and~\ref{tab:appmonthly} give all three versions.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_monthly_compact}

The proxy VAR has the signs of the main model: unemployment rises \bl{mo:rstar:yes:P1:u:12} after 12 months and the price level falls \blabs{mo:rstar:yes:P1:P:24} percent after 24. Its sets are wide, because the surprise is a weak instrument for monthly residuals (first-stage $F$ of \bl{mo:rstar:yes:F}). The window-impact VAR is close to the main model. A simulation shows that this is what to expect if the model is the true process. In 1,000 samples generated by the baseline model, with the data's calendar and announcement days, the proxy VAR with the FCI is centred on the true unemployment response (median \bl{sim:proxy_fci:u12:med} against \bl{sim:proxy_fci:u12:truth}) with a wide range, the proxy VAR without the FCI is biased toward zero, and the window-impact VAR recovers the true response with a narrower range (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:proxysim}). Measuring the impact in the window gives the same answer with less noise.


\section{Robustness}\label{sec:rob}
A result about the transmission of monetary policy can rest on one sample, one estimate of $\rs$, one choice of trend, one set of omitted variables or one surprise series. I vary each in turn. Each variation changes one feature of the baseline and keeps the rest: the shocks are the same announcements (except where the surprise itself changes), the monthly equations have 12 lags, and responses are to a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point in the window. Table~\ref{tab:robsummary} shows the baseline and the 15 variations. Thirteen of them keep the baseline yield and FCI relative to $\rs$; the other two replace the FCI with its three components.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_rob_summary}

With the FCI, the wrong-signed unemployment response disappears in every variation. Without the FCI, unemployment after 12 months falls in \bl{rob:no_uneg} of the 13 variations that keep the FCI relative to $\rs$; with it, unemployment rises in \bl{rob:upos} and never falls significantly. The price level falls after 24 months in all 13, and the fall is significant at the 5 percent level in \bl{rob:Psig5}. The three exceptions are all from 1996: the sample that ends in 2019, Kiley's $\rs$ and the spline trend. When the FCI is replaced by its three components, the price response after 24 months is not significant from either start. The subsections say what each variation tests and what it shows.

\subsection{The sample}\label{sec:recent}
Three features of the sample could drive the result. It is short, so the monetary environment since 1996 may be special. It contains the pandemic, when unemployment moved by more than in any other period. And it ends with 2022--24, the years of the largest tightening in the sample, so the result could rest on them. In the first variation the monthly equations start in 1973 rather than 1996; the months before 1990, for which there are no daily data, use monthly series in place of the daily block, the daily block is still estimated from 1996, and the shocks are still the announcements from 1996. In the second, March to December 2020 are dropped from the monthly and daily equations (2020 values still enter as lags). In the third, all equations end in December 2019. Each is run with the monthly equations starting in 1996 and in 1973.

From 1996 the price fall comes from the years after 2019. When the sample ends in 2019 the price response after 24 months is \bl{rb:R1d:yes:P:24}, with a 90 percent interval of [\bllo{rb:R1d:yes:P:24}, \blhi{rb:R1d:yes:P:24}]; from 1973 the same end date gives \bl{rb:R1e:yes:P:24}, significant at the 5 percent level. Starting in 1973 leaves the price response as in the baseline (\bl{rb:R1a:yes:P:24} after 24 months) and unemployment close to zero. Dropping 2020 leaves the price response (\bl{rb:R1b:yes:P:24}) and makes the unemployment response larger (\bl{rb:R1b:yes:u:24}, significant at the 5 percent level). When the monthly equations start in 1973 the yield alone moves unemployment little or raises it, so the wrong-signed response that the FCI removes belongs to the sample since 1996. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR1} gives the full results.

\subsection{The $r^*$ adjustment and trends}\label{sec:rstarrob}
The neutral rate is not observed, and the baseline uses one estimate of it, that of Laubach and Williams. Two variations check that the result does not depend on that choice. The first replaces it with Kiley's estimate, in the two-year yield and in the FCI alike. The second uses no $\rs$ measure at all: it keeps the real two-year yield and the real FCI and adds a natural cubic spline in time to every monthly equation, with knots at the sample ends and at the NBER business-cycle peaks inside the sample. The price result would be an artifact of the $\rs$ estimate if it vanished here.

The FCI removes the fall in unemployment in both. With Kiley's $\rs$ unemployment after 12 months is \bl{rb:R2a:no:u:12} without the FCI and \bl{rb:R2a:yes:u:12} with it; with real measures and the spline it is \bl{rb:R2c:no:u:12} and \bl{rb:R2c:yes:u:12}. The price response is smaller than in the baseline and, from 1996, not significant (\bl{rb:R2a:yes:P:24} and \bl{rb:R2c:yes:P:24} after 24 months). From 1973 it is significant at the 5 percent level in both (\bl{rb:R2b:yes:P:24} and \bl{rb:R2d:yes:P:24}). The choice of $\rs$ therefore matters for the size of the price response, and the sign pattern of unemployment does not depend on it. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR2} gives the full results.

\subsection{Credit conditions}\label{sec:credit}
Credit is the most important channel that the baseline leaves out \citep{gilchrist2012,caldara2019}, and a premium that responds to policy could carry part of what the FCI carries. The variation adds the monthly excess bond premium as a third monthly variable, treated like unemployment and inflation, so that the premium responds to the financial variables and they enter its equation as well.

With the FCI relative to $\rs$ the results are those of the baseline: unemployment rises (\bl{rb:R3a:yes:u:24} after 24 months) and the price level falls (\bl{rb:R3a:yes:P:24}); from 1973 the price level falls by \blabs{rb:R3b:yes:P:24} percent. Without the FCI the unemployment response moves toward zero (\bl{rb:R3a:no:u:12} after 12 months, against \bl{rstar:no:u:12} in the baseline), in the direction that adding the FCI moves it. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR3} gives the full results.

\subsection{The weights of the FCI}\label{sec:components}
The baseline assumes that the three parts of the FCI, the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar, affect unemployment and prices in proportion to the weights with which they respond to policy news (Section~\ref{sec:timing}). That is an assumption about the economy, not about the surprise, and it can be tested. I enter the three weighted components separately in place of the FCI, each with its own window impact, and test whether their coefficients are equal at each lag, with bootstrap $p$-values (Table~\ref{tab:weightstest}). The specification restricts the daily block to keep the number of parameters reasonable (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The monthly equations have 12 lags and start in 1996 or, as a further variation, in 1973.

From 1996 the price result does not carry over: after 24 months the price response is \bl{rb:R4:rstar:cmp:P:24}, with a 90 percent interval of [\bllo{rb:R4:rstar:cmp:P:24}, \blhi{rb:R4:rstar:cmp:P:24}]. The test agrees. The restriction is not rejected in the unemployment equation, and in the inflation equation it is rejected from 1996 (bootstrap $p$ of \bl{wt:rstar:pi:p} in the $\rs$ version) and not from 1973 (\bl{wt73:rstar:pi:p}). From 1996 the price result therefore depends on the assumption of proportional weights, and from 1973 it does not: from 1973 the price response is \bl{rb:R4b:rstar:cmp:P:12} after 12 months, significant at the 5 percent level, as with the FCI. Unemployment still rises in both samples (\bl{rb:R4:rstar:cmp:u:12} after 12 months from 1996 and \bl{rb:R4b:rstar:cmp:u:12} from 1973), with wider intervals because the variation adds many coefficients. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR4} gives the full results.

\subsection{The surprise}\label{sec:surprise}
The baseline uses the Nakamura--Steinsson policy news. An announcement can reveal the Fed's view of the economy as well as a change in policy, and part of each surprise can be predicted from public data. Two variations change the surprise. The first uses the orthogonalized surprise of \citet{bauer2023}, which removes the part of each surprise that public information predicts; it is available for announcements through 2019 (\bl{ev:bs} announcements). The second keeps the Nakamura--Steinsson surprise only for announcements in which the two-year yield and the S\&P 500 move in opposite directions in the window, the sign restriction of \citet{jarocinski2020} (\bl{ev:jk} announcements).

With the two-year yield alone the responses are the same as in the baseline, because the shock is scaled to the yield. With the FCI they are larger: unemployment rises \bl{rb:R5a:yes:u:24} and \bl{rb:R5b:yes:u:24} after 24 months, against \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} in the baseline, and the price level falls \blabs{rb:R5a:yes:P:24} and \blabs{rb:R5b:yes:P:24} percent, against \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24}. Both are significant at the 5 percent level. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR5} gives the full results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:policy}
Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements measure policy shocks well, at least the part of policy news that moves interest rates. The hard part has been tracing their effects on the economy. I kept the surprise as it is and changed how its effects are traced: through broad financial conditions rather than one interest rate, and relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. Traced this way, the responses of unemployment and prices have the standard signs, where the nominal two-year yield alone and the standard methods give the wrong ones. The ratio of the price response to the unemployment response is of conventional size: lowering the core price level 1 percent costs about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment over two years.

For policy, the results support judging the stance by financial conditions relative to $\rs$, as the FOMC did in 2023 \citep{powell2023}, and they explain why the same policy rate can be more or less restrictive at different times. In round numbers, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point lowers the core price level by \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24} percent after two years and raises unemployment by \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} percentage point. The estimates are effects of surprises, not a decomposition of any episode.

The broader lesson is that the fall in $\rs$ over the past three decades is more than a fact about the level of interest rates. Unless rates are measured relative to $\rs$, that fall also makes the effects of policy, and the cost of lowering inflation, harder to measure.

\section{A two-equation illustration}\label{app:toy}
Let $U_t$ be the unemployment gap, $i_t$ the policy rate and $f_t$ financial conditions, with a higher $f_t$ meaning tighter conditions:
\begin{align}
U_t &= \rho_U\,U_{t-1} + \kappa\,f_{t-1} + \varepsilon^{U}_t, \label{eq:y}\\
f_t &= \rho_f\,f_{t-1} + \lambda\,i_t + \varepsilon^{f}_t. \label{eq:f}
\end{align}
Unemployment responds to financial conditions and not directly to the policy rate. Financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, $\varepsilon^{f}_t$, such as changes in risk appetite. Some of these other forces may themselves be policy news that does not move interest rates \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. Suppose the economist observes only $U_t$ and $i_t$. To remove $f_t$, lag equation~\eqref{eq:y} one month, multiply it by $\rho_f$, subtract it from equation~\eqref{eq:y}, and use equation~\eqref{eq:f}. This gives
\begin{equation}
U_t = (\rho_U+\rho_f)\,U_{t-1} - \rho_U\rho_f\,U_{t-2} + \kappa\lambda\,i_{t-1} + \underbrace{\varepsilon^{U}_t - \rho_f\,\varepsilon^{U}_{t-1} + \kappa\,\varepsilon^{f}_{t-1}}_{\text{error}}. \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
The policy rate has no direct effect in this model. It appears in equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} because it stands in for financial conditions. The error is a moving average that includes past financial shocks, so a VAR in $U_t$ and $i_t$ with few lags cannot capture it. The VAR is misspecified. With the lags and samples available in practice, this can make the effects of monetary policy harder to measure.

\section{Relation to a monthly VAR and a proxy VAR}\label{app:relation}
Suppose the daily block were replaced by a monthly equation for $\bar x_t$, and every announcement occurred on the first day of the month. With $Z_t=(m_t',\bar x_t')'$, the model would then be a standard monthly VAR with a recursive ordering, the macroeconomic variables first, and impact vector $(0,0,b')'$. The daily block adds two things: the position of each announcement in its month, and fast dynamics of the financial variables, estimated on many more observations. A proxy VAR \citep{gertler2015,mertens2013,stock2018} instead estimates $Z_t=\sum_l C_l Z_{t-l}+\iota_t$ and uses as an instrument $z_t$ the surprises summed over the month. The effect on the FCI per unit effect on the two-year yield is then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{F,t},z_t)}{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{R,t},z_t)}. \label{eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
The window ratio $b_F$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact} has the same form, with three differences. It replaces monthly VAR residuals with changes measured inside the window, which do not depend on how strongly the surprise predicts monthly residuals. It sets the entries for unemployment and inflation to zero rather than estimating them. And it dates each shock to its day rather than summing shocks over a month. Section~\ref{sec:monthlyvar} estimates both monthly benchmarks. In large samples a VAR of this kind estimates the same effects as local projections \citep{plagborg2021}. In the language of mixed-data sampling, the monthly equation gives equal weights to the days within a month and unrestricted weights to the months \citep{ghysels2007,foroni2015}.

\section{The bootstrap}\label{app:boot}
Every interval in the note comes from a block bootstrap. Each draw resamples the \bl{nobs} calendar months from January 1996 in moving blocks of 12 months and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn, which keeps the dependence between them. The announcements drawn give new values of $b$ and new positions. The monthly equations are re-estimated on a series rebuilt recursively from the drawn residuals, with the macro lags taken from the simulated series and the financial lags from the data, and the daily block is re-estimated on the drawn daily observations. When the monthly equations start in 1973, the residuals before 1996 are drawn in blocks of 12 months of their own. A month without a monthly observation (2020 dropped, or a sample ending in 2019) contributes its daily observations and announcements but no residual. A simpler bootstrap resamples the three sources of sampling variation separately: the announcements and the monthly residuals independently across months (with the monthly lags held at their values in the data), and the daily observations in moving blocks of 66 days. I ran it with 12 lags and 500 draws for the nominal, real and $\rs$-relative two-year yield, each without and with the FCI of the same version. Across the \bl{bs:n} responses at 12, 24 and 36 months, its 90 percent intervals are wider than those of the block bootstrap in \bl{bs:wider}, by a median of \bl{bs:wr:med} times (range \bl{bs:wr:min} to \bl{bs:wr:max}). The two agree on whether a response is significant at the 10 percent level in \bl{bs:same10} of the \bl{bs:n} responses and at the 5 percent level in \bl{bs:same5}. The price response of the baseline is significant at the 5 percent level at every horizon under both. The 90 percent interval of the baseline unemployment response after 24 months is [\bl{bs:u24:lo5}, \bl{bs:u24:hi5}] under the block bootstrap and [\bl{bs:u24:lo}, \bl{bs:u24:hi}] under the simpler one.

\section{Lag length}\label{app:lags}
The baseline uses 12 monthly lags, fixed in advance. Table~\ref{tab:lags} repeats the main results with 3 and 6 lags. The sample, the announcements and the bootstrap are the same at every lag length: the block bootstrap with 500 draws. The tables that follow give every case at 3, 6 and 12 lags.

The direction of every result is the same at every lag length. With the FCI, the price level falls in all \bl{lag:n} responses (16 cases at 12, 24 and 36 months), and no response of unemployment is significantly negative. Unemployment rises in \bl{lag:upos:3} of the \bl{lag:n} responses with 3 lags, \bl{lag:upos:6} with 6 and \bl{lag:upos:12} with 12. Without the FCI, the Cholesky and proxy VARs show a fall in unemployment at every lag length; the Cholesky fall at 12 months is significant at all three. The weights test gives the same verdicts: in the sample from 1996 it rejects proportional weights in the inflation equation at the 10 percent level and does not reject them in the unemployment equation at the 5 percent level, and in the sample from 1973 it rejects in neither. In the simulation, the median proxy-VAR estimate of the unemployment response with the FCI is close to the true response at every lag length.

The size of the responses and the strength of the evidence increase with the lag length. The median price response with the FCI across the 48 responses is \bl{lag:med:P:3} with 3 lags, \bl{lag:med:P:6} with 6 and \bl{lag:med:P:12} with 12. The baseline price response at 36 months is \bl{lag:rstar:P36:3}, \bl{lag:rstar:P36:6} and \bl{lag:rstar:P36:12} percent. The number of significant price responses is \bl{lag:Psig:3}, \bl{lag:Psig:6} and \bl{lag:Psig:12} of \bl{lag:n}. The Phillips multiplier is bounded at 12 lags only; with 3 or 6 lags the data do not rule out arbitrarily steep trade-offs.

\section{Construction and estimation details}\label{app:details}
\textbf{Events.} I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available in the U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. As in the companion note, I leave out unscheduled announcements with no change in the policy rate, and March--April 2020. The announcements of September and October 2026 are left out because too few later days are available. The DXY index used in the window covers fewer currencies than the broad dollar used in the daily and monthly index, which I treat as a measurement difference.

\textbf{From monthly to daily.} Expected inflation and $\rs$ are not available daily, and they move slowly. I take the monthly gap between $R_t^{v}$ in equation~\eqref{eq:twoyear} and $y^{2}_t$, interpolate it linearly between the middles of adjacent months, and add it to the daily yield. The daily index is
\begin{equation}
F_\tau^{v} = w_B\, y^{10}_\tau + w_Q\, 100\ln\text{CAPE}_\tau + w_D\, 100\ln\text{DOL}_\tau + o_\tau^{v}, \label{eq:dailyfci}
\end{equation}
where $\text{DOL}_\tau$ is the daily nominal broad dollar. The offset $o_\tau^{v}$ is the monthly difference between the index in equation~\eqref{eq:fci} and the average of the first three terms, interpolated in the same way. It also absorbs the difference between the real and the nominal dollar. The monthly averages of the daily series are very close to the monthly measures: the root-mean-square difference is at most 0.02 unit for the FCI and 0.04 percentage point for the two-year yield. The offsets change little from day to day, so day-to-day moves in the index come from market prices. Table~\ref{tab:measures} shows the measures used at each frequency. The daily calendar has every weekday; missing values, such as on holidays, are filled from the previous observation for up to three days. Each daily equation has 13 coefficients with the FCI; each monthly equation has $1+4L$ with the FCI and $1+3L$ without it.

\textbf{Tracing the effect of a surprise.} The response is computed recursively, as a deviation from a path with no surprise. Let the announcement occur on weekday $d_0$ of the announcement month, and let $h=0,1,\ldots,36$ count months from then. Tildes denote responses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item In each month, unemployment and inflation come first: $\tilde m_0=0$, and for $h\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde m_h = \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, \tilde m_{h-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \tilde x^{M}_{h-l}, \label{eq:irfm}
\end{equation}
with terms dated before the announcement month equal to zero, and $\tilde x^{M}_{h}$ the response of the monthly average of $x$.
\item The days of the month follow. Before day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=0$. On day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=b$ from equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. On every later day, with $\tilde x^{(k)}_{\tau-1}$ the average of the responses over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_\tau = \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_{k}\, \tilde x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)-1}, \label{eq:irfd}
\end{equation}
where $h(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$, counted from the announcement month.
\item The monthly average $\tilde x^{M}_h$ then enters equation~\eqref{eq:irfm} for later months.
\end{enumerate}
Months after the announcement month have 21 weekdays. To avoid computing a path for every date, I form six groups of announcement dates by the weekday of the month, in blocks of four days, and compute the path at each group's average day and month length. With $\omega_g$ the share of announcements in group $g$, the reported response is $\sum_g \omega_g\, \tilde m_h^{(g)}$. Because the model is linear, these responses do not depend on the constant or the trend.

\textbf{Three-component specification.} Each component's equation in the daily block contains its own 1-, 5-, 22- and 66-day averages and those of the two-year yield, plus the monthly macro variables, and the two-year yield's equation its own averages and the macro variables. That is 48 coefficients in all, against 26 in the baseline daily block with the FCI. The three window impacts add up to the impact of the FCI.

\textbf{Ordinary-month response of the FCI.} The response of the FCI per percentage point on the two-year yield in ordinary months is the covariance of the FCI and yield residuals of the monthly VAR divided by the variance of the yield residual. The VAR contains unemployment, inflation, the end-of-month two-year yield and the end-of-month FCI of the same version, with 12 lags, over 1996 to 2026.

\textbf{Simulation.} The process is the estimated baseline model (relative to $\rs$, with the FCI; 12 lags), on the data's calendar of weekdays from January 1996 to September 2026 (369 months) and the actual announcement days. Within each month the macro variables are set first and the days of the month follow. Daily innovations are drawn with replacement from the model's daily residuals, separately for announcement days and other days; on announcement days the policy impact $b\,s_a$ is first removed from the residual and then added back with a drawn surprise. Monthly innovations are drawn jointly from the monthly residuals. Surprises are drawn with replacement from the 252 observed surprises. The twelve months before the sample and the 70 days before it are the actual 1995 data. On each sample I estimate the proxy VAR with and without the FCI, with the instrument and instrument months as in the data, and the window-impact VAR, taking the impact on the yield and the FCI from the model.

\textbf{Bootstrap.} The draws are those of Appendix~\ref{app:boot}: 500 block-bootstrap draws, from each of which I compute the response and report percentile intervals. The sets for the Phillips multiplier in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} use the same draws.
