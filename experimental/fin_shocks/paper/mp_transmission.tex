\begin{abstract}
Does the estimated effect of a monetary policy surprise depend on how its financial consequences are measured? It does. Standard methods applied to surprises around Federal Reserve announcements give unemployment that falls after a tightening and prices that barely respond. These methods trace the surprise through one interest rate. A surprise also moves long yields, stock prices and the dollar, and by much more than an ordinary change in the two-year yield does. I measure these moves in the half hour around each announcement and carry them forward in a daily and a monthly model, with rates and financial conditions measured relative to the neutral rate. A tightening then raises unemployment and lowers the price level. Most of the change comes from the size of the initial financial movement, not from the dynamics. In the baseline model, a 1 percent lower core price level after two years comes with about 0.9 percentage-point years of additional unemployment. The unemployment result holds across specifications. The price result is clearest when the long yield and the dollar enter in proportion to their weights in the index.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{JEL Classification:} E43, E44, E52, C32

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} monetary policy transmission, high-frequency identification, financial conditions, neutral interest rate, Phillips multiplier

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy, and its use rests on estimates of how policy moves unemployment and inflation. Those estimates are unreliable. In samples that start after the early 1980s, standard methods often give a tightening small effects on unemployment, or effects of the wrong sign \citep{ramey2016}. Two concerns follow. Policy may have become less powerful \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010}. Or inflation may respond so little to slack that lowering it requires a large and lasting rise in unemployment \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020}. The cost of lowering inflation in unemployment is an old question \citep{okun1978,tobin1980}. The joint responses of unemployment and prices to a monetary surprise measure that cost at a stated horizon, along the path policy takes to lower prices.

I ask whether these responses depend on how the financial consequences of a surprise are measured. Policymakers think they do. In November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}. A surprise that raises the two-year yield also raises longer yields and the dollar and lowers stock prices, and it does so by much more than an ordinary rise in the two-year yield does. A model that traces the surprise through the two-year yield alone misses most of this movement.

The answer is that the measurement matters. I use the policy-news surprise of \citet{nakamura2018} around 252 FOMC announcements from 1996 to 2026 and change only how its effects are carried through the economy. Carried through the nominal two-year yield alone, the surprises give the result of the standard methods: unemployment falls and prices do not respond. Carried through the yield and a broad financial conditions index, with both measured relative to the neutral rate $\rs$, a tightening raises unemployment and lowers the price level (Figure~\ref{fig:intro}). After two years the unemployment response rises from \bl{rstar:no:u:24} to \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} percentage point and the fall in the price level deepens from \blabs{rstar:no:P:24} to \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24} percent. Adding the index shifts the unemployment response by a similar amount when the yield is nominal or real. Most of the shift comes from the size of the initial financial movement, not from the dynamics that carry it forward (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}). In the baseline model, a 1 percent lower core price level after two years comes with about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of additional unemployment, one point of unemployment for one year.

The approach has three features. First, I measure the effect of each surprise in a short window around the announcement, when unemployment and inflation cannot move, and carry it forward in a daily and a monthly model estimated on ordinary data. As in \citet{jacobson2026}, the model takes into account when each announcement falls within its month. Second, financial conditions enter as a whole \citep{boivin2010}. The financial conditions index (FCI) combines the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the real dollar, weighted by their responses to policy news. Third, I measure the yield and the index relative to $\rs$, the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. The neutral rate fell from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}, and rates trended down with it while activity trended up. A model that links the level of rates to activity partly picks up these opposite trends. The adjustment should matter most for prices, which respond to how long conditions stay tight.

Two sections test the results. Section~\ref{sec:components} asks whether the three parts of the index should enter in proportion to their weights. Section~\ref{sec:rob} varies the sample, the measure of $\rs$, the trend, credit conditions and the surprise.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Surprises and their effects.} Interest-rate surprises around FOMC announcements were meant to settle the measurement problem. The decision precedes the announcement, so market moves in a narrow window around it cannot have caused it \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005}. They measure the effects of policy on asset prices well \citep{bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018,swanson2021}, but for unemployment and inflation the old puzzles often return. Four explanations come with fixes. Announcements reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and separating it gives larger price effects \citep{romer2000,nakamura2018,jarocinski2020,miranda2021}. Part of each surprise is predictable, and removing it sharpens the estimates \citep{bauer2023,bauer2023aer}. Markets react in minutes while unemployment and inflation are monthly averages, and measuring inflation daily removes most of the wrong sign \citep{jacobson2026}. And credit costs, stock prices and the dollar carry part of the effect \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019,kroencke2021,bauer2023jep,boehm2024,bauer2026,boehm2026}. Most of this work traces the surprise through a single nominal interest rate.

\textbf{What is new.} My paper is closest in spirit to \citet{jacobson2026}. Like them, I hold the surprise and the method fixed and change one measurement. They change how often the outcome is measured. I change how the channel from policy to the economy is measured, and my main result is for activity. \citet{bauer2023} and \citet{jarocinski2020} improve the surprise itself. In my design a surprise enters only through its immediate effect on the two-year yield and the FCI and through its timing, so their fixes and mine can be combined.

\textbf{Financial channels and risk appetite.} \citet{gertler2015} and \citet{caldara2019} emphasize financial channels too. I use a broad index measured relative to $\rs$ and leave credit spreads out of the baseline. Studies of the ``non-yield'' shock treat policy-driven moves in stock prices and the dollar as a second shock \citep{bauer2026,boehm2024}. \citet{boehm2026} select the combination of two yield-curve surprises and the non-yield shock that best accounts for macroeconomic fluctuations. They choose the direction of the shock for its macroeconomic content. I fix the conventional direction, news about the path of interest rates, and ask which financial variables are needed to trace it. \citet{caballero2026} propose a neutral level of the FCI itself; gaps to that level are a natural extension.

\textbf{The power of policy and the Phillips curve.} Policy shocks became less powerful after the early 1980s. The literature attributes this to a more systematic policy response and better-anchored expectations \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010,primiceri2005}, or to a failure of standard identification once policy became forward-looking \citep{barakchian2013}. Recent work finds a small Phillips-curve slope \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020,ball2019,fitzgerald2024}, hard to identify when policy offsets demand shocks \citep{mcleay2020} and understated by conventional methods \citep{barnichon2020}. \citet{barnichon2021} measure the tradeoff by the ratio of cumulative inflation to cumulative unemployment after policy shocks and find it much flatter after 1990 than before. I add evidence of this kind and show that the measured tradeoff depends on how the stance of policy is measured.

\section{Empirical Approach}\label{sec:data}
The model has three pieces. A surprise moves the two-year yield and the FCI by amounts measured in the window: the vector $b$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. A daily VAR, equation~\eqref{eq:daily}, carries those two series forward. Their monthly averages then enter a monthly VAR for unemployment and inflation, equation~\eqref{eq:monthly}. Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the construction of the series, the algorithm for tracing responses and the bootstrap, and Appendix~\ref{app:data} lists the data.

\subsection{Measuring Financial Conditions}\label{sec:measure}
The FCI is the market index of \citet{fcinote}: a weighted sum of the 10-year Treasury yield, stock valuations and the real broad dollar. Indexes of this kind differ mainly in where the weights come from \citep{hatzius2010}. FCI-G takes them from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}. Mine come from event-study regressions, long used to measure how asset prices respond to policy \citep{kuttner2001,bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018}.

Policy news is the first principal component of the changes in federal funds and eurodollar futures around the announcement, rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield 1 percentage point \citep{nakamura2018}. I regress the window changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar on it and weight the three by how precisely each recovers the policy component. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy (Table~\ref{tab:weights}). Because the weights are scaled to the policy news, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit.

The index comes in three versions. The nominal version uses the nominal 10-year yield and the log of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE). The real version subtracts 10-year expected inflation from the yield. The version relative to $\rs$ also subtracts $\rs$ from the yield, and it compares the earnings yield, the inverse of CAPE, with $\rs$: stocks are expensive, and conditions loose, when the earnings yield is low relative to the neutral rate. That comparison enters with its sign reversed and is rescaled to the units of log CAPE, so the stock weight keeps its meaning (Appendix~\ref{app:index}). The dollar is the real broad dollar in all three versions, and the two-year yield is adjusted in the same three ways.

For $\rs$ I use the New York Fed's one-sided Laubach--Williams estimate \citep{holston2023}. It is a filtered estimate that uses data only up to each quarter, from the August 2026 vintage with parameters estimated on the full sample, so it is not a real-time series. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly. The daily series add their interpolated monthly values to daily market prices, so day-to-day moves come from markets.

Figure~\ref{fig:fcis} plots the three versions on a common scale. They move together at business-cycle and higher frequencies; the correlation of 12-month changes is at least \bl{fci:c12:nrs}. The real version differs little from the nominal one. The version relative to $\rs$ differs more, and the difference is mostly slow: it is negative in the late 1990s and early 2000s and positive after 2010, as $\rs$ fell.

\subsection{Why Financial Conditions and $r^*$}\label{sec:background}
Policy reaches spending and prices through many interest rates, asset prices and the dollar. Few households and businesses borrow at the policy rate. They face rates on consumer credit, mortgages and business debt, which arbitrage links to current and expected short rates. Higher rates raise the cost of capital and lower the value of existing capital \citep{jorgenson1963,hall1967,tobin1969}. Lower stock prices lower wealth and consumption \citep{ando1963,friedman1957}. A stronger dollar shifts spending toward imports \citep{fleming1962,mundell1963}. Modern models of the business cycle contain the same links \citep{christiano2005,smets2007,gali2005,kaplan2018,auclert2019}, and the effects come with a lag \citep{friedman1961}.

\textbf{What standard VARs leave out.} The workhorse evidence comes from VARs with a few macroeconomic variables and one interest rate \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. Some studies add a credit spread \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019}, and factor models bring in many more series \citep{bernanke2005favar}. Long yields, equity valuations and the dollar are usually absent. These are the variables that carry the effects of policy. Missing variables are an old concern: \citet{sims1992} argued that the price puzzle arises because the Federal Reserve responds to information that small VARs do not contain \citep[see also][]{castelnuovo2010}. My concern is different, because here the missing variables are the channel itself. If unemployment responds to financial conditions, a VAR in unemployment and the policy rate lets the rate stand in for them. Its error contains past financial shocks, which a VAR with few lags cannot capture \citep[Appendix~\ref{app:toy};][]{fernandez2007}.

The omission also changes the experiment. A policy surprise that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the FCI by about \bl{dc:rstar:bF} unit in the window. An ordinary rise of 1 percentage point in the yield, on a day without an announcement, comes with a rise of only \bl{dc:rstar:ord} unit (90 percent interval [\bl{dc:rstar:ord:lo}, \bl{dc:rstar:ord:hi}]; Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The window loading is close to one because the weights are scaled to policy news. The ordinary loading is not scaled. It is an empirical fact, and it is much smaller. A VAR without financial conditions carries through the ordinary move in the yield. My design puts financial conditions in the system and measures the immediate effect of the surprise on the yield and on financial conditions directly in the window, where policy news is the main force at work.

\textbf{Why relative to $r^*$.} What matters in these models is the real rate relative to the neutral rate, not the nominal rate. The same holds for the other parts of financial conditions: a given yield or stock valuation is tighter when $\rs$ is low. The distinction matters most for inflation, which responds to the slack that builds up over time and so to how long conditions stay tight relative to $\rs$. A measure that ignores a falling $\rs$ overstates how tight policy has been, and by more the longer the period.

\textbf{Financial conditions and real activity.} Asset prices help forecast output and inflation, but which ones help changes over time \citep{stock2003}. Credit spreads, and especially their excess bond premium component, predict activity well \citep{gilchrist2012}. Broad indexes summarize many such series \citep{hatzius2010,brave2011}, and financial conditions are linked to the risks around growth \citep{adrian2019}, possibly nonlinearly \citep{kiley2020ml}. Federal Reserve staff publish a measure of the effect of financial conditions on growth \citep{ajello2023}. A correlation between financial conditions and later activity need not be causal, because financial conditions also respond to the economy. The two literatures have complementary strengths. Work on high-frequency surprises identifies policy shocks cleanly but carries them through few variables. Work on financial conditions studies many variables but does not isolate policy shocks. I bring them together.

\textbf{Policy news beyond interest rates.}\label{sec:scope} Announcements move stock prices and the dollar by more than interest-rate surprises explain \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. My windows show the same pattern. The policy-news surprise explains \bl{r2:y2} percent of the variance of the move in the two-year yield, but only \bl{r2:y10} percent for the 10-year yield, \bl{r2:sp} percent for the S\&P 500, \bl{r2:usd} percent for the dollar and \bl{r2:fci} percent for the FCI. The unexplained part is either news unrelated to policy, in which case my estimates capture the effects of policy, or policy news that does not move interest rates, in which case they capture one dimension of policy news and the effects of all of it could be larger. Both readings agree on the channel: policy news reaches the economy through broad financial conditions.

\subsection{Timing Assumptions and Identification}\label{sec:timing}
The shock is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy-news surprise, $s_a$ in equation~\eqref{eq:ns}, from the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The window runs from 10 minutes before the statement to 60 minutes after the press conference starts, or 30 minutes around the statement when there is no press conference. Identification comes from timing, at three frequencies. Table~\ref{tab:timing} summarizes the assumptions.

\textbf{The window.} The window move in the FCI is computed with the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F_a = w_B\, \Delta y^{10}_a + w_Q\, \Delta \text{SP}_a + w_D\, \Delta \text{DXY}_a. \label{eq:winfci}
\end{equation}
These are the measures available within the day, and the ones used to estimate the weights. Percent changes in the S\&P 500 equal percent changes in CAPE within a window, because earnings are fixed. I express all effects per percentage point on the two-year yield in the window. The effect on the FCI is the ratio of two regression slopes on the surprise, an instrumental-variables estimate with $s_a$ as the instrument:
\begin{equation}
b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ b_F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b_F = \frac{\sum_a (\Delta F_a - \overline{\Delta F})\,(s_a-\bar s)}{\sum_a (\Delta y^{2}_a - \overline{\Delta y^{2}})\,(s_a-\bar s)} = 0.99. \label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
This is close to one because the weights are normalized that way. It is not exactly one because this regression includes a constant, which the scaling in equation~\eqref{eq:ns} does not, and because it uses one fewer announcement. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly, so I take the window move to be the same in every version.

\textbf{The day.} On the announcement day, the two-year yield and the FCI shift by the vector $b$. I use the window move and not the change over the whole day, because the full-day change contains other news. On later days the two series move as equation~\eqref{eq:daily} implies. They affect each other only through the daily lags, so no ordering between them is needed within the day.

\textbf{The month.} Within each month, unemployment and inflation are set first, and markets then respond to them. In the month of the announcement, unemployment and inflation therefore have not moved. They respond from the next month on, through the monthly average of the daily financial series. That average depends on when in the month the announcement falls, since an announcement late in a month affects few days of that month's average. This is how the model takes the timing of announcements into account, as \citet{jacobson2026} recommend. The ordering is the familiar recursive one, in which slow-moving macroeconomic variables come before financial variables \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. The daily equations include the current month's unemployment and inflation, even though those figures are published later. I treat the monthly values as a summary of conditions that markets observe as the month unfolds.

\textbf{Three steps.} The design separates three steps. The first identifies announcement news: the surprise $s_a$ is policy news measured in a window short enough that news about the path of interest rates is the main systematic force correlated with it. The second measures the immediate financial effects of that news, the vector $b$. The third propagates those effects. After the window, the yield and the FCI evolve as the daily equations estimated on ordinary days imply, and unemployment and inflation respond as the monthly equations estimated on ordinary months imply. Only the first two steps use the announcements.

The third step carries the main assumption. Once the shock is identified, the economy responds to a change in the financial variables in the same way whatever caused it. A shock identified by a Cholesky ordering or an external instrument is carried through the same estimated dynamics as every other shock, and I do the same. Suppose stock prices fall 5 percent in an announcement window because the surprise raises the rate at which future earnings are discounted. On another day they fall 5 percent because earnings forecasts are cut. The first fall raises the cost of capital and lowers wealth with no news about demand. The second may also signal weaker demand, which would lower activity whatever the policy rate does. The model carries both through one equation. If the discount-rate fall is more or less contractionary than the average fall, the estimated propagation is wrong for the policy surprise. This problem differs from central-bank information, which concerns what $s_a$ reveals in step one and which I set aside, as in the companion note. Propagation could fail even if announcements revealed nothing about the economy.

Two further assumptions are specific to this paper. First, the surprise reaches unemployment and prices only through the yield and the FCI, and has no direct effect on them in the window. Other channels, such as credit spreads and expectations, are left out. So are moves in stock prices and the dollar that are unrelated to $s_a$. The non-yield shock of \citet{boehm2024} is uncorrelated with policy-news surprises, so such moves do not bias $b_F$. Second, the monthly equations see the FCI as a single index. Its weights show how the three components respond to policy news, not how the economy responds to each. I assume the three affect unemployment and prices in proportion to those weights, and Section~\ref{sec:components} tests that.

A valid external instrument can recover the total response of unemployment and prices with the transmission channels left unobserved, under the usual conditions of a proxy VAR. A yield-only model is not wrong by construction. My claim is narrower. In these data, with 12 lags, a yield-only model does not reproduce the response to the announcement surprise, because the surprise moves financial conditions by much more than the ordinary movement of the yield does. Comparing the two loadings shows that the experiments differ. It does not show how much the difference matters for unemployment and prices. Section~\ref{sec:decomp} does.


\subsection{A Mixed-Frequency VAR}\label{sec:midas}
\textbf{Structure.} The model has a daily block and a monthly block. Let $x_\tau=(R_\tau,F_\tau)'$ hold the two-year yield and the FCI on day $\tau$, in the same version. Let $m_t=(U_t,\pi_t)'$ hold the two monthly variables. The unemployment gap $U_t$ is the unemployment rate minus the CBO estimate of its natural rate. The CBO estimate does not respond to the surprise, so the response of the gap equals that of the unemployment rate. Inflation is $\pi_t=1200\,\Delta\ln P_t$, where $P_t$ is the price index for personal consumption expenditures excluding food and energy (core PCE).

The daily block is a VAR that uses averages of the past day, week, month and quarter as regressors \citep{corsi2009}:
\begin{equation}
x_\tau = \alpha + \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_k\, \bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, m_{t(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, m_{t(\tau)-1} + \xi_\tau, \label{eq:daily}
\end{equation}
where $\bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)}$ is the average of $x$ over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$, and $t(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$. Averages over about a day, a week, a month and a quarter capture persistence with few coefficients. When the FCI is left out, $x_\tau$ is the two-year yield alone.

The monthly block is
\begin{equation}
m_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, m_{t-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \bar x_{t-l} + \zeta_t, \qquad \bar x_{t}=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{\tau\in t} x_\tau, \label{eq:monthly}
\end{equation}
with $n_t$ the number of weekdays in month $t$. The model is a mixed-frequency VAR \citep{ghysels2016}. The equation contains no current-month term in $\bar x_t$, which is the timing assumption of Table~\ref{tab:timing}.

A monthly VAR with the same variables and a recursive ordering is the special case in which every announcement falls on the first day of its month (Appendix~\ref{app:relation}).

\textbf{The time trend.} The baseline monthly equations contain a constant and no time trend. Rates drifted down along with $\rs$ over 1996--2026, inflation was low in the 2010s and rose after 2020, and the natural rate of unemployment may have shifted in ways the CBO estimate misses. Section~\ref{sec:rstarrob} adds a spline trend.

\textbf{Estimation and inference.} I estimate each block by ordinary least squares, on the same sample: January 1996, the month of the first announcement with all window measures, to September 2026 (\bl{nobs} months, and the weekdays of those months for the daily block). The lagged daily averages and the monthly lags may use earlier observations. 2020 is kept in both blocks. The baseline has $L=12$ monthly lags, fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:lags} shows 3 and 6). Responses are computed recursively, as deviations from a path with no surprise, and averaged over the positions of the announcements within their months (Appendix~\ref{app:details}); the response of the price level after $h$ months is $\sum_{j=0}^{h}\tilde\pi_j/12$. Intervals come from a block bootstrap with 500 draws \citep{kunsch1989,jentsch2019} that resamples calendar months in blocks of 12 and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn (Appendix~\ref{app:boot}). A response is significant at the 5 or 10 percent level when its 95 or 90 percent interval excludes zero. I report 90 percent intervals, as recent studies in this area do \citep{bauer2023,caldara2019,miranda2021,ramey2016}, and 68 percent intervals, which \citet{jarocinski2020} report alongside them. The FCI weights and $\rs$ are held fixed.

\subsection{Standard Methods after the Early 1980s}\label{sec:standard}
Standard identification schemes have not delivered conventional effects of monetary policy in samples that start after the early 1980s. \citet{ramey2016} estimates the specification of \citet{christiano1999} on 1983--2007 and finds that ``the results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the unemployment rate.'' A hybrid VAR with the Romer--Romer shock \citep{romer2004} gives, on the same later sample, contractionary shocks that ``appear to be expansionary,'' as \citet{barakchian2013} found for 1988--2007, and a proxy SVAR with the Romer--Romer shock, estimated on 1969--2007, gives a ``pronounced, statistically significant price puzzle'' (her Figures~1 and~2). \citet{caldara2019} find, for 1994--2007 with a proxy SVAR that uses high-frequency surprises, that without a credit spread the response of industrial production is weak and uncertain (about $-0.2$ percent, with a credible set that includes positive values), against about $-0.75$ percent when the excess bond premium is added.

I start from two standard methods, estimated on my own data and units so that they compare directly with the model below. Both use the monthly unemployment gap, core PCE inflation and the end-of-month two-year Treasury yield, with 12 lags and no trend. The first is the recursive (Cholesky) VAR of \citet{christiano1999}, with the policy rate ordered last. The second is a proxy VAR \citep{stock2018,mertens2013,gertler2015} that uses the Nakamura--Steinsson surprise, summed over the announcements of the month, as an instrument for the policy rate. All responses are to a 1 percentage point rise in the policy rate (Figure~\ref{fig:standard} and Table~\ref{tab:standard}).

The first-stage $F$ statistic of the proxy VAR is \bl{st:prox:F}, so I report exact Anderson--Rubin sets, which stay valid when the instrument is weak \citep{montiel2021,lunsford2015,lewis2026,bauer2026}. These are the responses when the surprises are carried through the two-year yield alone.

\subsection{The FCI Model: Baseline Results}\label{sec:baseline}
Figure~\ref{fig:intro} shows the baseline. Table~\ref{tab:matched} compares the responses after 24 months with and without the FCI for the three versions of the yield, on common bootstrap draws. The units are the same in all results: the end-of-month two-year yield moves 0.92 to 1.01 of the window move, so a 1 percentage point move in the window and a 1 percentage point move in the end-of-month yield in the monthly VARs are the same shock to within about 8 percent.

With the nominal or real yield alone the model gives what the standard methods give. Unemployment falls and the price level does not respond. Measured relative to $\rs$, the yield alone does not raise unemployment but lowers the price level, by \blabs{rstar:no:P:24} percent after two years. Adding the FCI changes both responses in every version. Unemployment rises by \bl{dc:nom:AC:u:24}, \bl{dc:real:AC:u:24} and \bl{dc:rstar:AC:u:24} percentage point in the nominal, real and neutral-rate versions, and the price level falls by \bl{dc:nom:AC:P:24:abs}, \bl{dc:real:AC:P:24:abs} and \bl{dc:rstar:AC:P:24:abs} percent. Measuring both relative to $\rs$ gives the largest responses: unemployment peaks at \bl{path:rstar:yes:u:max} percentage point after \bl{path:rstar:yes:u:maxh} months and the price level falls steadily, by \blabs{rstar:yes:P:36} percent after 36 months.

\subsection{Estimating the Impact from Monthly Data}\label{sec:monthlyvar}
A standard proxy VAR can also carry the FCI. It estimates the immediate effect of the surprise on every variable from monthly residuals and the instrument, instead of measuring it in the window. A window-impact VAR takes the effect on the yield and the FCI from the windows, as the main model does, and sets the effect on unemployment and inflation to zero within the month. It has no daily block, so the timing of announcements within the month is lost. Table~\ref{tab:monthlycompact} gives the results for the yield and the FCI relative to $\rs$.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_monthly_compact}

The proxy VAR has the signs of the main model, with wide sets because the surprise is a weak instrument for monthly residuals (first-stage $F$ of \bl{mo:rstar:yes:F}). The window-impact VAR is close to the main model. Most of what the main model delivers is therefore already in a monthly model that takes the impact from the windows. The daily block adds the timing of announcements and a path for the financial variables at the frequency of the surprise (Section~\ref{sec:resolve} and Appendix~\ref{app:persist}). A simulation shows that this is what to expect if the model is the true process: the proxy VAR with the FCI is centred on the true response with a wide range, the proxy VAR without the FCI is biased toward zero, and the window-impact VAR recovers the true response with a narrower range (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:proxysim}). Measuring the impact in the window gives the same answer with less noise.

\subsection{Why Adding the FCI Changes the Estimated Response}\label{sec:decomp}
Adding the FCI changes the experiment and the model at once. A policy surprise raises the FCI by \bl{dc:rstar:bF} unit per percentage point on the yield. A yield-only model has no index to move, and it carries the yield through dynamics estimated without the index. To separate the two changes I compute three paths for the neutral-rate version, on the same sample, calendar, horizon and normalization.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Path A} is the baseline: the fitted model with the yield and the FCI, started by the yield rise and the window movement of the FCI.
\item \textbf{Path B} is the same fitted model, started by the same yield rise and the FCI movement that ordinarily accompanies it, \bl{dc:rstar:ord} unit.
\item \textbf{Path C} is the matched model with the yield alone.
\end{itemize}
The ordinary movement is the covariance of the FCI and yield innovations of the daily block divided by the variance of the yield innovation, on days without an announcement. Those are the ordinary observations nearest in timing to the window, but they are close-to-close changes over a whole day. Path B is therefore a model counterfactual, not an observed event.

A $-$ C is the effect of adding the FCI. A $-$ B isolates the initial FCI movement, holding the dynamics fixed. B $-$ C is what remains: the difference associated with including the financial state and re-estimating the propagation of the yield. It has no unique structural interpretation. All three paths use the same bootstrap draws, so A $-$ B and B $-$ C add to A $-$ C at every horizon.

The initial movement accounts for most of the effect. Adding the FCI raises unemployment after 24 months by \bl{dc:rstar:AC:u:24} percentage point and lowers the price level by \bl{dc:rstar:AC:P:24:abs} percent. The extra initial movement alone accounts for \bl{dc:rstar:AB:u:24} and \bl{dc:rstar:AB:P:24:abs}, and both intervals exclude zero. The remainder, B $-$ C, is \bl{dc:rstar:BC:u:24} and \bl{dc:rstar:BC:P:24:abs}, and neither interval excludes zero. The nominal and real versions give the same split (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). The timing agrees. The A $-$ B contrast for unemployment appears within the first quarter, peaks at about 12 months and fades as the financial movement fades. The contrast for the price level builds steadily, because the price level cumulates inflation. Paths B and C are nearly parallel throughout. The yield-only paths therefore follow path B, and the difference between the yield-only model and the baseline is largely the difference between the ordinary and the announcement movement of financial conditions.

Two points qualify this. The split depends on the ordinary loading. In monthly data it is \bl{dc:ordm}, not \bl{dc:rstar:ord}, and with that loading A $-$ B is \bl{dc:sens:u:phim} and B $-$ C is \bl{dc:sens:u:BCm} for unemployment; the initial movement remains the larger part. And the initial movement is not the whole story for the size of the baseline response, which also reflects how long the movement lasts. A shorter FCI path would lower the baseline unemployment response after 24 months from \bl{pe:rho:base:u24} to \bl{pe:rho:pt:u24}, though the data cannot tell that path from the model's (Appendix~\ref{app:persist}).

\subsection{The Implied Inflation-Unemployment Tradeoff}\label{sec:tradeoff}
What matters for policy is the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response. Following \citet{barnichon2021}, I measure it by the Phillips multiplier at horizon $H$, the cumulative price response per unit of cumulative unemployment response:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}_H = \frac{\tilde P_H}{\tilde U_H}, \qquad \tilde U_H=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\tilde U_h, \label{eq:pm}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde P_H$ is the response of the core price level after $H$ months, in percent, and $\tilde U_H$ is the cumulative response of the unemployment gap, in percentage-point years. Its inverse, $-1/\mathcal{P}_H$, is a sacrifice ratio: the percentage-point years of unemployment needed to lower the price level 1 percent. A more negative $\mathcal{P}_H$ means a steeper Phillips curve. When $\tilde U_H$ is close to zero the ratio is not well defined, so I form 90 percent sets for $\mathcal{P}_H$ in the spirit of \citet{anderson1949}: the set contains every value $\mathcal{P}$ for which the 90 percent bootstrap interval of $\tilde P_H-\mathcal{P}\,\tilde U_H$ contains zero. The sets can be unbounded, which shows when the data cannot pin down the tradeoff.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_tradeoff}

Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} gives the results. The tradeoff is measured in the baseline. With the yield and the FCI relative to $\rs$, $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24} with a set of [\bllo{tr:rstar:yes:24}, \blhi{tr:rstar:yes:24}], and $\mathcal{P}_{36}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36}. The sacrifice ratio after two years is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment per 1 percent lower price level, with a set of \bl{tr:sac:24:lo} to \bl{tr:sac:24:hi}. This is not the cost of a permanent one-point decline in annual inflation, and it differs from the three-year ratio of \bl{tr:sac:36}. In the other versions the data pin the tradeoff down less well. Where adding the FCI leaves the cumulative unemployment response close to zero, the sets are unbounded. A yield-only model that does not raise unemployment says nothing about the cost of lowering inflation.

\citet{barnichon2021} report a multiplier of about $-0.15$ after three to four years for 1990--2008, estimated with high-frequency surprises, and one that diverges before 1990 and is about $-3$ after five years, estimated with Romer--Romer shocks. The baseline, at \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} after three years, lies between the two.

\subsection{Which Financial Variables Carry the Response}\label{sec:components}
The baseline assumes that the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar affect unemployment and prices in proportion to the weights with which they respond to policy news. That is an assumption about the economy, and it can be relaxed and tested. I compare three specifications of the monthly equations. In the first the FCI enters as one variable. In the second equities enter separately and the 10-year yield and the dollar enter as one variable. In the third all three enter separately. The daily block is the same in all three, so the specifications differ only in the monthly equations and share their bootstrap draws (Appendix~\ref{app:details}).

\input{../baseline/report/tab_components}

The unemployment response does not depend on the specification. The price response does, in the sample from 1996. Freeing equities leaves most of it in place (\bl{cp:1996:E:P} percent after 24 months, against \bl{cp:1996:R:P} with the index as one variable). Freeing the dollar as well removes it (\bl{cp:1996:F:P}). The coefficients show why. In the inflation equation a tighter 10-year yield lowers inflation, as the baseline assumes, and a tighter dollar, holding the yield and equities fixed, has a conditional effect of the opposite sign (\bl{cp:coef:96:pi:y10} for the yield and \bl{cp:coef:96:pi:usd} for the dollar). A conditional coefficient of this kind does not identify the effect of an exogenous change in the dollar, because the dollar moves with the yield and with equities. It does show that the price result needs the two to enter in proportion.

The tests agree (Table~\ref{tab:weightstest}). Equality of the yield and dollar coefficients, with equities free, is rejected in the inflation equation from 1996 and not in the unemployment equation. In the sample from 1973 the three specifications give the same price response (\bl{cp:1973:R:P}, \bl{cp:1973:E:P} and \bl{cp:1973:F:P}) and no test rejects. Equities do not need to be tied to the yield and the dollar. The restriction that matters for prices is the one that ties the dollar to the 10-year yield.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:rob}
A result about the transmission of monetary policy can rest on one sample, one estimate of $\rs$, one trend, one set of omitted variables or one surprise series. I vary each in turn, changing one feature of the baseline and keeping the rest (Table~\ref{tab:robsummary}).

\input{../baseline/report/tab_rob_summary}

The main results hold across the variations. With the FCI, the wrong-signed unemployment response disappears everywhere: unemployment rises in \bl{rob:upos} of the 13 variations that keep the index relative to $\rs$ and never falls significantly, while without the FCI it falls in \bl{rob:no_uneg}. The price level falls in all 13, and significantly in most. The subsections say what each variation tests.

\subsection{The Sample}\label{sec:recent}
The sample is short, it contains the pandemic, and it ends with the largest tightening in the sample. I start the monthly equations in 1973 instead of 1996 (earlier months use monthly series in place of the daily block), drop March to December 2020, and end all equations in December 2019. In every case the announcements and their window impacts are those of the baseline.

The restrictions remove different data. Dropping 2020 removes ten months from the monthly and daily equations. Ending in 2019 removes 2020--26 from both, and neither isolates the inflation surge of 2021--22. With 2020 dropped or the start moved to 1973, the results are those of the baseline. From 1996, ending the sample in 2019 weakens the price response, from \bl{sm:base96:P:24} to \bl{sm:end96:P:24} percent after two years, with an interval that includes zero; from 1973 the response is \bl{sm:end73:P:24}, close to the baseline. Core inflation varied little before 2020 (a standard deviation of \bl{sm:pi:sd:9619} percentage points for 1996--2019, against \bl{sm:pi:sd:2026} for 2020--26). The shorter sample has little information on how inflation responds to financial conditions, and the pattern is consistent with the later years supplying it. Starting in 1973 leaves unemployment close to zero, so the wrong-signed response that the FCI removes belongs to the sample since 1996.

\subsection{The $r^*$ Adjustment and Trends}\label{sec:rstarrob}
The neutral rate is not observed. One variation replaces the Laubach--Williams estimate with Kiley's, in the yield and the FCI alike. Another uses no $\rs$ at all: it keeps the real yield and the real FCI and adds a natural cubic spline in time to every monthly equation. In both, the FCI removes the fall in unemployment. The price response is smaller from 1996 and as in the baseline from 1973. The choice of $\rs$ matters for the size of the price response, not for the sign pattern of unemployment.

\subsection{Credit Conditions}\label{sec:credit}
Credit is the most important channel the baseline leaves out \citep{gilchrist2012,caldara2019}, and a premium that responds to policy could carry part of what the FCI carries. I add the monthly excess bond premium as a third monthly variable. The results are those of the baseline. Without the FCI the unemployment response moves toward zero, in the direction that adding the FCI moves it.

\subsection{The Surprise}\label{sec:surprise}
An announcement can reveal the Fed's view of the economy as well as a change in policy, and part of each surprise can be predicted from public data. Two variations change the surprise. One uses the orthogonalized surprise of \citet{bauer2023}, which removes the predictable part and is available through 2019 (\bl{ev:bs} announcements). The other keeps the Nakamura--Steinsson surprise only when the two-year yield and the S\&P 500 move in opposite directions in the window, the sign restriction of \citet{jarocinski2020} (\bl{ev:jk} announcements). With the FCI the responses are somewhat larger than in the baseline.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:policy}
Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements measure policy shocks well, at least the part of policy news that moves interest rates. The hard part has been tracing their effects. I kept the surprise as it is and changed how its effects are traced: through broad financial conditions rather than one interest rate, and relative to the neutral rate. Traced this way, unemployment and prices respond with the standard signs, where the nominal two-year yield alone and the standard methods give the wrong ones. Most of the difference comes from the size of the financial movement that a policy surprise brings, more than three times the movement that accompanies an ordinary rise in the yield, and little from re-estimating the dynamics.

The most durable finding is about unemployment. Once policy news is traced through financial conditions, a tightening does not lower unemployment at any horizon in the baseline or in any variation. The policy interpretation is the cost of price restraint: about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment for each 1 percent lower core price level after two years. The results support judging the stance of policy by financial conditions relative to $\rs$, as the FOMC did in 2023 \citep{powell2023}, and they explain why the same policy rate can be more or less restrictive at different times. The estimates are effects of surprises, not a decomposition of any episode.

\section{The Financial Conditions Index}\label{app:index}
\textbf{Policy news.} Let $\Delta X_a$ be the $5\times 1$ vector of changes in the current- and next-meeting federal funds futures and the second to fourth eurodollar futures in the window around announcement $a$. Following \citet{nakamura2018}, let $p_a$ be the first principal component of these changes, signed so that it moves with the fourth eurodollar future. Policy news is $p_a$ rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield $y^{2}$ by 1 percentage point:
\begin{equation}
s_a = \gamma\, p_a, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\sum_a p_a\, \Delta y^{2}_a}{\sum_a p_a^{2}}. \label{eq:ns}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Weights.} Let $\Delta A_a$ be the vector of changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar index (DXY) in the window. The companion note treats these changes as
\begin{equation}
\Delta A_a = \psi\, s_a + \eta_a, \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ holds the policy responses and $\eta_a$ is other news, with covariance matrix $\Sigma$. It estimates $\psi$ by regressing each element of $\Delta A_a$ on $s_a$ and a constant, and $\Sigma$ from the residuals, over 253 announcements from January 1996 to September 2026. The weights are those that recover the policy component most precisely (one-factor generalized least squares):
\begin{equation}
w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\psi}{\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi}. \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Because $w'\psi=1$ and $s_a$ is scaled to the two-year yield, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit. The weights are 0.61 on the 10-year yield, $-0.051$ per percent on stock valuations and 0.065 per percent on the dollar. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy.

\textbf{Three versions.} In month $t$, the index in version $v$ is
\begin{equation}
F_t^{v} = w_B\, B_t^{v} + w_Q\, Q_t^{v} + w_D\, D_t, \label{eq:fci}
\end{equation}
measured as a deviation from its sample mean. Here $D_t$ is 100 times the log of the real broad dollar in every version. With $y^{10}_t$ the 10-year yield, $\pi^{10}_t$ 10-year expected inflation, and $(E/P)_t$ the inverse of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE), the other two terms are:
\begin{align}
\text{Nominal:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:nom}\\
\text{Real:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:real}\\
\text{$\rs$-gap (baseline):}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t - \rs_t, & Q_t &= -\frac{100}{\overline{E/P}}\left[(E/P)_t - \rs_t\right]. \label{eq:rstar}
\end{align}
In the $\rs$-gap version, stocks are measured by the gap between their earnings yield and $\rs$. The term $100/\overline{E/P}$, with $\overline{E/P}$ the sample average, converts this gap to the units of the log CAPE: since $d\ln\text{CAPE}=-d(E/P)/(E/P)$, a change in the earnings yield of $x$ points near the mean equals a change of $100x/\overline{E/P}$ in 100 ln CAPE with the opposite sign. The sign reversal and the scaling mean that the weight on stocks is the same in every version. CAPE is a real valuation measure, so stocks do not change between the nominal and real versions. For $\rs$ I use the New York Fed's current Laubach--Williams estimates \citep{holston2023}. These ``one-sided'' estimates use only data up to each date, with the model's parameters estimated on the full sample.

\textbf{The two-year yield.} The two-year yield is adjusted in the same three versions. With $y^{2}_t$ the nominal yield and $\pi^{2}_t$ two-year expected inflation \citep[from the Cleveland Fed;][]{haubrich2012},
\begin{equation}
R_t^{\text{nominal}} = y^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\text{real}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\rs\text{-gap}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t - \rs_t. \label{eq:twoyear}
\end{equation}
Expected inflation and $\rs$ are monthly or quarterly and move slowly. The daily series add their interpolated monthly values to daily market prices, so day-to-day moves come from markets (Appendix~\ref{app:details}).

\section{A Two-Equation Illustration}\label{app:toy}
Let $U_t$ be the unemployment gap, $i_t$ the policy rate and $f_t$ financial conditions, with a higher $f_t$ meaning tighter conditions:
\begin{align}
U_t &= \rho_U\,U_{t-1} + \kappa\,f_{t-1} + \varepsilon^{U}_t, \label{eq:y}\\
f_t &= \rho_f\,f_{t-1} + \lambda\,i_t + \varepsilon^{f}_t. \label{eq:f}
\end{align}
Unemployment responds to financial conditions and not directly to the policy rate. Financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, $\varepsilon^{f}_t$, such as changes in risk appetite. Some of these other forces may themselves be policy news that does not move interest rates \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. Suppose the economist observes only $U_t$ and $i_t$. To remove $f_t$, lag equation~\eqref{eq:y} one month, multiply it by $\rho_f$, subtract it from equation~\eqref{eq:y}, and use equation~\eqref{eq:f}. This gives
\begin{equation}
U_t = (\rho_U+\rho_f)\,U_{t-1} - \rho_U\rho_f\,U_{t-2} + \kappa\lambda\,i_{t-1} + \underbrace{\varepsilon^{U}_t - \rho_f\,\varepsilon^{U}_{t-1} + \kappa\,\varepsilon^{f}_{t-1}}_{\text{error}}. \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
The policy rate has no direct effect in this model. It appears in equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} because it stands in for financial conditions. The error is a moving average that includes past financial shocks, so a VAR in $U_t$ and $i_t$ with few lags cannot capture it. The VAR is misspecified. With the lags and samples available in practice, this can make the effects of monetary policy harder to measure.

\section{Relation to a Monthly VAR and a Proxy VAR}\label{app:relation}
Suppose the daily block were replaced by a monthly equation for $\bar x_t$, and every announcement occurred on the first day of the month. With $Z_t=(m_t',\bar x_t')'$, the model would then be a standard monthly VAR with a recursive ordering, the macroeconomic variables first, and impact vector $(0,0,b')'$. The daily block adds two things: the position of each announcement in its month, and fast dynamics of the financial variables, estimated on many more observations. A proxy VAR \citep{gertler2015,mertens2013,stock2018} instead estimates $Z_t=\sum_l C_l Z_{t-l}+\iota_t$ and uses as an instrument $z_t$ the surprises summed over the month. The effect on the FCI per unit effect on the two-year yield is then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{F,t},z_t)}{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{R,t},z_t)}. \label{eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
The window ratio $b_F$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact} has the same form, with three differences. It replaces monthly VAR residuals with changes measured inside the window, which do not depend on how strongly the surprise predicts monthly residuals. It sets the entries for unemployment and inflation to zero rather than estimating them. And it dates each shock to its day rather than summing shocks over a month. Section~\ref{sec:monthlyvar} estimates both monthly benchmarks. In large samples a VAR of this kind estimates the same effects as local projections \citep{plagborg2021}. In the language of mixed-data sampling, the monthly equation gives equal weights to the days within a month and unrestricted weights to the months \citep{ghysels2007,foroni2015}.

\section{The Bootstrap}\label{app:boot}
Every interval in the note comes from a block bootstrap. Each draw resamples the \bl{nobs} calendar months from January 1996 in moving blocks of 12 months and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn, which keeps the dependence between them. The announcements drawn give new values of $b$ and new positions. The monthly equations are re-estimated on a series rebuilt recursively from the drawn residuals, with the macro lags taken from the simulated series and the financial lags from the data, and the daily block is re-estimated on the drawn daily observations. When the monthly equations start in 1973, the residuals before 1996 are drawn in blocks of 12 months of their own. A month without a monthly observation (2020 dropped, or a sample ending in 2019) contributes its daily observations and announcements but no residual. A simpler bootstrap resamples the three sources of sampling variation separately: the announcements and the monthly residuals independently across months (with the monthly lags held at their values in the data), and the daily observations in moving blocks of 66 days. I ran it with 12 lags and 500 draws for the nominal, real and $\rs$-relative two-year yield, each without and with the FCI of the same version. Across the \bl{bs:n} responses at 12, 24 and 36 months, its 90 percent intervals are wider than those of the block bootstrap in \bl{bs:wider}, by a median of \bl{bs:wr:med} times (range \bl{bs:wr:min} to \bl{bs:wr:max}). The two agree on whether a response is significant at the 10 percent level in \bl{bs:same10} of the \bl{bs:n} responses and at the 5 percent level in \bl{bs:same5}. The price response of the baseline is significant at the 5 percent level at every horizon under both. The 90 percent interval of the baseline unemployment response after 24 months is [\bl{bs:u24:lo5}, \bl{bs:u24:hi5}] under the block bootstrap and [\bl{bs:u24:lo}, \bl{bs:u24:hi}] under the simpler one.

\section{Lag Length}\label{app:lags}
The baseline uses 12 monthly lags, fixed in advance. Table~\ref{tab:lags} repeats the main results with 3 and 6 lags. The sample, the announcements and the bootstrap are the same at every lag length: the block bootstrap with 500 draws. The tables that follow give every case at 3, 6 and 12 lags.

The direction of every result is the same at every lag length. With the FCI, the price level falls in all \bl{lag:n} responses (16 cases at 12, 24 and 36 months), and one response of unemployment is significantly negative, at 3 lags only (Kiley's $\rs$ from 1973, after 24 months: \bl{lag:k3:u24} [\bl{lag:k3:u24:lo}, \bl{lag:k3:u24:hi}]). Unemployment rises in \bl{lag:upos:3} of the \bl{lag:n} responses with 3 lags, \bl{lag:upos:6} with 6 and \bl{lag:upos:12} with 12. Without the FCI, the Cholesky and proxy VARs show a fall in unemployment at every lag length; the Cholesky fall at 12 months is significant at all three. The weights test gives the same verdicts: in the sample from 1996 it rejects proportional weights in the inflation equation at the 10 percent level and does not reject them in the unemployment equation at the 5 percent level, and in the sample from 1973 it rejects in neither. In the simulation, the median proxy-VAR estimate of the unemployment response with the FCI is close to the true response at every lag length.

The size of the responses and the strength of the evidence increase with the lag length. The median price response with the FCI across the 48 responses is \bl{lag:med:P:3} with 3 lags, \bl{lag:med:P:6} with 6 and \bl{lag:med:P:12} with 12. \section{Financial Persistence beyond the Announcement Window}\label{app:persist}
The monthly equations respond to the monthly average of the daily financial variables, so the model's answer depends on how long the window movement of the yield and the FCI lasts. The daily block (equation~\eqref{eq:daily}) is estimated on ordinary days. I check it against the data in the days after announcements.

\textbf{Definitions.} Day 0 is the announcement day and day $h$ is $h$ trading days later. The model sets the level of each variable on day 0 equal to its window movement and propagates it from there. I build the direct estimate on the same clock. The change on day $h$ is the window movement plus the change in the closing level from day 0 to day $h$, $D_h=w_a+(x_{a,h}-x_{a,0})$, so day 0 is the window movement itself. The endpoints are $D_5$, $D_{22}$ and $D_{66}$. The average is the mean of $D_0,\dots,D_{21}$, 22 trading days with the announcement day included, which corresponds to the first month of the monthly average. The model works on weekdays and the data on trading days, so 66 weekdays are about 63 to 65 trading days. As a check I also build the change from the close before the announcement, $x_{a,h}-x_{a,-1}$. The two differ by the part of the announcement day's close-to-close move that the window does not contain, the window-to-close gap in the notes of Table~\ref{tab:persist}.

\textbf{Estimation.} Each $D_h$ is regressed on the policy-news surprise over the \bl{pe:n} announcements with 66 trading days after them, the common set for all horizons, and the slope is divided by the slope of the window movement of the two-year yield on the same announcements. The unit is then a one-point rise in the yield in the window, as in the model. Announcements less than 66 trading days apart overlap. Two things address this. Intervals come from moving blocks of 12 calendar months, resampling the announcements of the months drawn and re-estimating the normalization, the scheme of the main bootstrap. And a second estimate adds the sum of the surprises of the announcements that follow within the horizon as a control. The model paths are those of the baseline, evaluated at the same trading-day horizons, with the same bootstrap draws.

\textbf{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:persist} and Figure~\ref{fig:persist} give the direct estimates and the model. For the two-year yield the direct estimates are \bl{pe:y:h5} at 5 days, \bl{pe:y:h22} at 22 days and \bl{pe:y:h66} at 66 days, against \bl{pe:y:h5:model}, \bl{pe:y:h22:model} and \bl{pe:y:h66:model} in the model; every model value lies inside the 90 percent interval of the direct estimate, and so does the model's first-month average (\bl{pe:y:avg22:model} against \bl{pe:y:avg22}, interval [\bl{pe:y:avg22:lo}, \bl{pe:y:avg22:hi}]). For the FCI the direct estimates are \bl{pe:f:h5} at 5 days and \bl{pe:f:h22} at 22 days against the model's \bl{pe:f:h5:model} and \bl{pe:f:h22:model}, again inside the intervals. At 66 days the direct estimate is \bl{pe:f:h66} with an interval of [\bl{pe:f:h66:lo}, \bl{pe:f:h66:hi}], which contains the model's \bl{pe:f:h66:model} and also a complete reversal: the data cannot distinguish persistence from decay at that horizon. Adding the surprises of later announcements as controls changes none of these conclusions.

The one difference is the first-month average of the FCI. On the model's clock the direct estimate is \bl{pe:f:avg22} (interval [\bl{pe:f:avg22:lo}, \bl{pe:f:avg22:hi}]) and the model's value \bl{pe:f:avg22:model} lies below it. The difference comes from the announcement day. The close-to-close move of the FCI on the announcement day is \bl{pe:f:gap} [\bl{pe:f:gap:lo}, \bl{pe:f:gap:hi}] below the window move: part of the window's movement in stock prices, long yields and the dollar has reversed by the close. Built from the close before the announcement, the average is \bl{pe:f:avg22:base} and the model lies inside the interval. The window-to-close gap is also a statement about the starting point: if the FCI started the model at the closing level and not at the window level, the 24-month responses would be \bl{pe:sens:gap:u24} percentage point for unemployment and \bl{pe:sens:gap:P24} percent for the price level different, which is a third of path A $-$ C in Section~\ref{sec:decomp} and does not change its sign.

\textbf{What the differences imply for the macro response.} A sensitivity calculation traces the macro response through the monthly equations of the baseline, holding the daily block fixed. If the event month's average of the FCI and the yield are those of the direct estimate on the model's clock (the FCI \bl{pe:sens:w:df} higher and the yield \bl{pe:sens:w:dy} lower), the responses after 24 months change by \bl{pe:sens:w:u24} percentage point for unemployment and \bl{pe:sens:w:P24} percent for the price level. If the same excess lasted through the first three months, the changes are \bl{pe:sens:w:u24x3} and \bl{pe:sens:w:P24x3}. The first-month average therefore does not matter for the results. Persistence at longer horizons would. If the FCI path from the second month on were scaled by the ratio of the direct to the model estimate at 66 days (\bl{pe:rho:pt}), the responses after 24 months would be \bl{pe:rho:pt:u24} for unemployment and \bl{pe:rho:pt:P24} for the price level, against \bl{pe:rho:base:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:base:P24}; with the ratio at the ends of the 90 percent interval (\bl{pe:rho:lo} and \bl{pe:rho:hi}) they would be \bl{pe:rho:lo:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:lo:P24}, and \bl{pe:rho:hi:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:hi:P24}. The data at 66 days cannot narrow that range. The check shows that the model's persistence is consistent with the days after announcements. It cannot show that it is right beyond the first month.

\section{A Direct Projection on the Surprise}\label{app:direct}
The main model imposes that the surprise reaches unemployment and prices through the yield and the FCI. A direct projection imposes nothing: all channels operate, and no variable of the financial block enters. The surprise, summed over the announcements of a month, is the regressor, and the unemployment gap, or the cumulated core inflation of the months from the announcement month to month $t+h$, is the outcome. The specification, the controls and the inference are in the notes of Table~\ref{tab:direct}. The existing local-projection code of this project (\texttt{experimental/rstar\_case/lp\_unemp.py}) projects unemployment on past values of an index, not on the surprise, and the earlier distributed-lag regressions of the surprise on the monthly outcome (\texttt{experimental/fin\_shocks/legacy/direct\_mfdl.py}) use event-day shocks with a restricted lag shape. Neither instruments a monthly yield innovation. The projection here is the direct response, scaled by the window slope of the yield, with no first stage.

\textbf{Results.} The direct projection has the features of the earlier direct estimates. With macro lags the unemployment response is \bl{dp:m:u:12} percentage point after 12 months and \bl{dp:m:u:24} after 24, and the price level \bl{dp:m:P:12} and \bl{dp:m:P:24} percent. The sign of unemployment is the wrong one and the price response is not negative. The estimates are noisy: the 90 percent bootstrap intervals include zero for all four ([\bl{dp:m:u:12:lo}, \bl{dp:m:u:12:hi}] for unemployment after 12 months), and Newey--West intervals exclude zero for unemployment at both horizons and for the price level after 24 months. When the lags of the yield and the FCI are added to the controls, the estimates change sign for unemployment (\bl{dp:f:u:12} and \bl{dp:f:u:24}) and the price level stays above zero (\bl{dp:f:P:12} and \bl{dp:f:P:24}); no interval excludes zero. The main model's responses lie inside the bootstrap intervals of both projections at both horizons, except for the price level after 24 months in the first projection.

The direct projection allows all transmission channels to operate, including the exchange rate, expectations and the response of policy to the economy after the announcement, and nothing is imposed. A perverse estimate does not show that omitting the FCI biases the result, and I do not control for the contemporaneous FCI. It shows that the direct response of the economy to the surprise, taken at face value, does not have the signs that the theory gives, and that the data do not pin it down. The sign change when lagged financial conditions are added is a feature of the estimates, not a test of the model. The comparison is informative for identification. The announcement surprise by itself gives an imprecise direct macro estimate; the paper obtains a more informative estimate by imposing a model of financial propagation. The proxy VAR of Table~\ref{tab:monthlycompact} is the benchmark that keeps the monthly dynamics and is therefore the comparison for the main model.

\section{Construction and Estimation Details}\label{app:details}
\textbf{Events.} I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available in the U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. As in the companion note, I leave out unscheduled announcements with no change in the policy rate, and March--April 2020. The announcements of September and October 2026 are left out because too few later days are available. The DXY index used in the window covers fewer currencies than the broad dollar used in the daily and monthly index, which I treat as a measurement difference.

\textbf{From monthly to daily.} Expected inflation and $\rs$ are not available daily, and they move slowly. I take the monthly gap between $R_t^{v}$ in equation~\eqref{eq:twoyear} and $y^{2}_t$, interpolate it linearly between the middles of adjacent months, and add it to the daily yield. The daily index is
\begin{equation}
F_\tau^{v} = w_B\, y^{10}_\tau + w_Q\, 100\ln\text{CAPE}_\tau + w_D\, 100\ln\text{DOL}_\tau + o_\tau^{v}, \label{eq:dailyfci}
\end{equation}
where $\text{DOL}_\tau$ is the daily nominal broad dollar. The offset $o_\tau^{v}$ is the monthly difference between the index in equation~\eqref{eq:fci} and the average of the first three terms, interpolated in the same way. It also absorbs the difference between the real and the nominal dollar. The monthly averages of the daily series are very close to the monthly measures: the root-mean-square difference is at most 0.02 unit for the FCI and 0.04 percentage point for the two-year yield. The offsets change little from day to day, so day-to-day moves in the index come from market prices. Table~\ref{tab:measures} shows the measures used at each frequency. The daily calendar has every weekday; missing values, such as on holidays, are filled from the previous observation for up to three days. Each daily equation has 13 coefficients with the FCI; each monthly equation has $1+4L$ with the FCI and $1+3L$ without it.

\textbf{Tracing the effect of a surprise.} The response is computed recursively, as a deviation from a path with no surprise. Let the announcement occur on weekday $d_0$ of the announcement month, and let $h=0,1,\ldots,36$ count months from then. Tildes denote responses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item In each month, unemployment and inflation come first: $\tilde m_0=0$, and for $h\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde m_h = \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, \tilde m_{h-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \tilde x^{M}_{h-l}, \label{eq:irfm}
\end{equation}
with terms dated before the announcement month equal to zero, and $\tilde x^{M}_{h}$ the response of the monthly average of $x$.
\item The days of the month follow. Before day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=0$. On day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=b$ from equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. On every later day, with $\tilde x^{(k)}_{\tau-1}$ the average of the responses over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_\tau = \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_{k}\, \tilde x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)-1}, \label{eq:irfd}
\end{equation}
where $h(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$, counted from the announcement month.
\item The monthly average $\tilde x^{M}_h$ then enters equation~\eqref{eq:irfm} for later months.
\end{enumerate}
Months after the announcement month have 21 weekdays. To avoid computing a path for every date, I form six groups of announcement dates by the weekday of the month, in blocks of four days, and compute the path at each group's average day and month length. With $\omega_g$ the share of announcements in group $g$, the reported response is $\sum_g \omega_g\, \tilde m_h^{(g)}$. Because the model is linear, these responses do not depend on the constant or the trend.

\textbf{Three-component specification.} Each component's equation in the daily block contains its own 1-, 5-, 22- and 66-day averages and those of the two-year yield, plus the monthly macro variables, and the two-year yield's equation its own averages and the macro variables. That is 48 coefficients in all, against 26 in the baseline daily block with the FCI. The three window impacts add up to the impact of the FCI.

\textbf{Ordinary movement of the FCI.} The ordinary loading is the covariance of the FCI and yield innovations divided by the variance of the yield innovation, in FCI units per percentage point on the yield, so it is comparable with the window loading $b_F$. For path B of Section~\ref{sec:decomp} I take the innovations from the daily block, the nearest ordinary observations to the announcement window: the residuals of equation~\eqref{eq:daily} on days without an announcement (conditioning on the 1-, 5-, 22- and 66-day averages of the yield and the FCI and on the monthly unemployment gap and inflation of this month and last). The 90 percent interval re-estimates the daily block and the loading on the days of each block-bootstrap draw. The earlier estimate in monthly data is the same ratio from the residuals of a monthly VAR in unemployment, inflation, the end-of-month two-year yield and the end-of-month FCI of the same version, with 12 lags, over 1996 to 2026. Neither measures an announcement. Daily innovations are close-to-close changes that include all of the day's news; monthly innovations add the dynamics within the month. Because the model is linear, the response to any initial FCI movement $\phi$ is the response to the yield alone plus $\phi$ times the response to the FCI, so path B can be recomputed for any loading: with the monthly loading (\bl{dc:ordm}) the A $-$ B contrast after 24 months is \bl{dc:sens:u:phim} for unemployment and \bl{dc:sens:P:phim} for the price level, and with no FCI movement at all (a loading of zero) \bl{dc:sens:u:phi0} and \bl{dc:sens:P:phi0}.

\textbf{Simulation.} The process is the estimated baseline model (relative to $\rs$, with the FCI; 12 lags), on the data's calendar of weekdays from January 1996 to September 2026 (369 months) and the actual announcement days. Within each month the macro variables are set first and the days of the month follow. Daily innovations are drawn with replacement from the model's daily residuals, separately for announcement days and other days; on announcement days the policy impact $b\,s_a$ is first removed from the residual and then added back with a drawn surprise. Monthly innovations are drawn jointly from the monthly residuals. Surprises are drawn with replacement from the 252 observed surprises. The twelve months before the sample and the 70 days before it are the actual 1995 data. On each sample I estimate the proxy VAR with and without the FCI, with the instrument and instrument months as in the data, and the window-impact VAR, taking the impact on the yield and the FCI from the model.

\textbf{Bootstrap.} The draws are those of Appendix~\ref{app:boot}: 500 block-bootstrap draws, from each of which I compute the response and report percentile intervals. The sets for the Phillips multiplier in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} use the same draws.
