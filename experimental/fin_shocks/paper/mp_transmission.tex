\begin{abstract}
Does the estimated effect of a monetary policy surprise depend on how its financial consequences are measured? In samples that start in the mid-1990s, standard methods applied to surprises around Federal Reserve announcements give unemployment that falls after a tightening and prices that barely respond. I keep the surprises and change how their effects are carried through the economy. A policy surprise moves the two-year yield and, with it, long yields, stock prices and the dollar. I measure how the yield and a broad financial conditions index move in the half hour around each announcement, and carry those moves forward in a daily and a monthly model estimated on ordinary data. Both are measured relative to the neutral rate. In the window the index rises about one unit per percentage point on the yield, against about 0.3 of a unit on ordinary days. Carried through the nominal two-year yield alone, the surprises give the familiar wrong signs. Carried through the yield and the index relative to the neutral rate, a tightening raises unemployment and lowers the price level, and most of the difference comes from the larger initial movement in financial conditions rather than from re-estimating the dynamics. A 1 percent lower core price level after two years comes with about 0.9 percentage-point years of additional unemployment. The price result requires that the long yield and the dollar enter in proportion to their weights in the index; the unemployment result does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilizing the economy. Its use rests on estimates of how much a change in policy moves unemployment and inflation. In samples that start after the early 1980s, standard methods often give a surprise tightening small effects on unemployment, or effects of the wrong sign \citep{ramey2016}. Two concerns follow. One is that policy has become less powerful \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010}. The other is that inflation responds little to slack, so that lowering inflation requires a large and lasting rise in unemployment \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020}. That concern gives the question its policy weight. The cost of lowering inflation in unemployment is an old question \citep{okun1978,tobin1980}, and the joint responses of unemployment and prices to a monetary surprise measure it at a stated horizon, along the path policy takes to lower prices. I ask whether the estimated effects of the same surprise depend on how its financial consequences are measured.

The question matters in practice. From 2022 to 2023 the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by more than 5 percentage points. Policymakers expected costs: in August 2022 the Chair warned of ``some softening of labor market conditions'' and called these ``the unfortunate costs of reducing inflation'' \citep{powell2022}. They also described policy as working through broad financial conditions: in November 2023 the Chair noted that ``Financial conditions have tightened significantly in recent months, driven by higher longer-term bond yields, among other factors'' \citep{powell2023}. The standard tools for measuring the effects of policy leave those conditions out.

The estimates depend on the measurement. I use the policy-news surprise of \citet{nakamura2018} around 252 FOMC announcements from 1996 to 2026, and I change only how its effects are carried through the economy. A surprise that raises the two-year yield also moves long yields, stock prices and the dollar. In the half hour around the announcement, a surprise that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises a broad financial conditions index by about 1 unit. On ordinary days a 1 percentage point rise in the yield comes with a rise of the index of only \bl{dc:rstar:ord} unit. Carried through the two-year yield alone, the surprises give the result of the standard methods: unemployment falls after a tightening, by \blabs{st:c2y96:u:12} percentage point after a year in a recursive VAR and \blabs{st:prox:u:12} in a proxy VAR, and prices do not respond. Carried through the yield and the index, both measured relative to the neutral rate $\rs$, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises unemployment by \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} percentage point after two years and lowers the core price level by \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24} percent (Figure~\ref{fig:intro}). Most of the difference comes from the initial financial movement and not from re-estimating the dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}). A 1 percent lower core price level after two years comes with about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of additional unemployment (one point of unemployment for one year), with a 90 percent set of \bl{tr:sac:24:lo} to \bl{tr:sac:24:hi}. In the terms of \citet{barnichon2021}, the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} after three years, between their estimates of about $-0.15$ for 1990--2008 and about $-3$ after five years before 1990.

The approach has three features.

First, I measure the effect of each surprise in a short window around the announcement (30 to about 100 minutes), in which unemployment and inflation cannot move, and carry it forward in a daily and a monthly model estimated on ordinary data, as in a standard vector autoregression (VAR). As in \citet{jacobson2026}, the model takes into account when each announcement falls within its month.

Second, financial conditions enter as a whole \citep{boivin2010}. The financial conditions index (FCI) combines the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the real dollar, weighted by their responses to policy news. A model with the yield alone traces the ordinary move in the yield, which carries much less of the change in financial conditions than a policy surprise does.

Finally, I measure the yield and the index relative to $\rs$, the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. The neutral rate fell from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,bauer2020,kiley2020}, and interest rates trended down with it while activity trended up, so a model that links the level of rates to activity partly picks up these opposite trends. Inflation depends on how long real rates stay above or below $\rs$, so the adjustment should matter most for prices.

Earlier fixes improve the surprise or the timing of the outcome. I leave both alone and change the channel: policy reaches the economy through long rates, stock prices and the dollar, which I measure relative to $\rs$. Section~\ref{sec:components} tests the assumption that the three components enter in proportion to their weights and shows which responses depend on it. Section~\ref{sec:rob} varies the sample, the measure of $\rs$, the trend, credit conditions and the surprise.

\subsection{Connection to the Literature}
\textbf{Surprises and their effects.} Interest-rate surprises around FOMC announcements were meant to settle the measurement problem: the decision precedes the announcement, so market moves in a narrow window around it cannot have caused it \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005}. They measure the effects of policy on asset prices precisely \citep{bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018,swanson2021}, but for unemployment and inflation the old puzzles often return. Four explanations come with fixes. Announcements reveal the Fed's view of the economy, and separating it gives larger price effects \citep{romer2000,nakamura2018,jarocinski2020,miranda2021}. Part of each surprise is predictable, and removing it sharpens the estimates \citep{bauer2023,bauer2023aer}. Markets react in minutes while unemployment and inflation are monthly averages, and measuring inflation daily removes most of the wrong sign \citep{jacobson2026}. And credit costs, stock prices and the dollar carry part of the effect \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019,kroencke2021,bauer2023jep,boehm2024,bauer2026,boehm2026}. Most of this work traces the surprise through a single nominal interest rate. The choice of surprise series \citep{bu2021,brennan2025} and of lags \citep{coibion2012} can matter. Futures-based surprises in monthly models go back to \citet{faust2004}, and related work on timing and mixed frequencies includes \citet{kilian2024,chudik2022,francis2011,ghysels2016,gazzani2020,chudik2026}.

\textbf{What is new.} My paper is closest in spirit to \citet{jacobson2026}. Like them, I hold the surprise and the method fixed and change one measurement. They change how often the outcome is measured; I change how the channel from policy to the economy is measured, and my main result is for activity. \citet{bauer2023} and \citet{jarocinski2020} improve the surprise itself. In my design a surprise enters only through its immediate effect on the two-year yield and the FCI and through its timing, so their fixes and mine can in principle be combined.

\textbf{Financial channels and risk appetite.} \citet{gertler2015} and \citet{caldara2019} are closest in emphasizing financial channels. I use a broad index measured relative to $\rs$, and the baseline leaves credit spreads out. Studies of the ``non-yield'' shock treat policy-driven moves in stock prices and the dollar as a second shock \citep{bauer2026,boehm2024}. \citet{boehm2026} go further and select the combination of two yield-curve surprises and the non-yield shock that best accounts for macroeconomic fluctuations; the combined shock leaves interest rates largely unchanged. They choose the direction of the shock for its macroeconomic content. I fix the conventional direction, news about the path of interest rates, and ask which financial variables are needed to trace it. In the window, stock prices and the dollar move with the yield in the policy-news direction; I treat that co-movement as part of financial conditions, and the part that is unrelated to rate news as noise. Their work and mine ask different questions, and the estimates are not substitutes. The response of stock prices to long rates has also changed over time \citep{kiley2014}. \citet{caballero2026} propose a neutral level of the FCI itself, FCI$^*$. I keep $\rs$ for the rates inside the two-year yield and the index; gaps to FCI$^*$ are a natural extension.

\textbf{The power of policy and the Phillips curve.} The effects of policy shocks became smaller after the early 1980s, which the literature attributes to a more systematic policy response and better-anchored expectations \citep{boivin2006,boivin2010,primiceri2005}, or to a failure of standard identification once policy became more forward-looking \citep{barakchian2013}. Recent work finds a small Phillips-curve slope \citep{hazell2022,delnegro2020,ball2019,fitzgerald2024}, hard to identify when policy offsets demand shocks \citep{mcleay2020} and understated by conventional methods \citep{barnichon2020}. Policy shocks offer one way to identify it. \citet{barnichon2021} measure the tradeoff by the ratio of cumulative inflation to cumulative unemployment after such shocks and find it much flatter after 1990 than before. I add evidence of this kind and show that the measured tradeoff depends on how the stance of policy is measured.

\section{Empirical Approach}\label{sec:data}
The model has three pieces. A surprise moves the two-year yield and the FCI by amounts measured in the window: the vector $b$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. A daily VAR, equation~\eqref{eq:daily}, carries those two series forward. Their monthly averages then enter a monthly VAR for unemployment and inflation, equation~\eqref{eq:monthly}. Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the construction of the series, the algorithm for tracing responses and the bootstrap, and Appendix~\ref{app:data} lists the data.

\subsection{Measuring Financial Conditions}\label{sec:measure}
The FCI is the market index of \citet{fcinote}: a weighted sum of the 10-year Treasury yield, stock valuations and the real broad dollar. Indexes of this kind are weighted sums of financial variables \citep{hatzius2010}. They differ mainly in where the weights come from. FCI-G takes them from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}. Mine come from the event-study regressions long used to measure how asset prices respond to policy \citep{kuttner2001,bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018}.

The weights come from the responses of the three variables to policy news in the window. Policy news is the first principal component of the changes in federal funds and eurodollar futures around the announcement, rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield 1 percentage point \citep{nakamura2018}. Regressing the window changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar index on it, and weighting the three by how precisely each recovers the policy component, gives the weights: 0.61 on the 10-year yield, $-0.051$ per percent on stock valuations and 0.065 per percent on the dollar. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy. Because the weights are scaled to the policy news, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit.

The index comes in three versions, which differ in how interest rates and stock prices are measured. In the nominal version the 10-year yield is the nominal yield and stock prices enter as the log of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE). The real version subtracts 10-year expected inflation from the yield. The version relative to $\rs$ also subtracts $\rs$ from the yield. For stocks it compares the cyclically adjusted earnings yield, the inverse of CAPE, with $\rs$: stocks are expensive, and conditions loose, when the earnings yield is low relative to the neutral rate. The comparison enters with the sign reversed and is rescaled to the units of log CAPE near the average earnings yield, so the stock weight keeps its meaning (Appendix~\ref{app:index} gives the formula). The dollar is the real broad dollar in all three versions. The two-year yield is adjusted in the same three versions.

For $\rs$ I use the New York Fed's Laubach--Williams estimate \citep{holston2023}, the one-sided series: a filtered estimate that uses data only up to each quarter, from the August 2026 data vintage with parameters estimated on the full sample, so it is not a real-time series. Quarterly values are placed in the middle month of the quarter and interpolated. The FCI and its three versions come from a companion note and were fixed before this analysis \citep{fcinote}. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly, and the daily series add their interpolated monthly values to daily market prices, so day-to-day moves come from markets. Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the daily construction.

Figure~\ref{fig:fcis} plots the three versions on a common scale, each minus its own mean over 1996--2026. They move together at business-cycle and higher frequencies: the correlation of 12-month changes is \bl{fci:c12:nr} between the nominal and real versions and \bl{fci:c12:rr} between the real version and the version relative to $\rs$. The real version differs little from the nominal one (standard deviation of the difference \bl{fci:sd:d1} unit, against \bl{fci:sd:nominal} for the nominal index). Relative to $\rs$ the index differs more (standard deviation of the difference from the real version \bl{fci:sd:d2}, largest in absolute value \bl{fci:max:d2}), and the difference is mostly slow: \bl{fci:slow:d2} percent of its variance is in a five-year moving average. It takes the form of a gradual rise of the version relative to $\rs$, the fall in $\rs$ since the 1990s and the earnings yield compared with it.

\subsection{Why Financial Conditions and $r^*$}\label{sec:background}
Policy reaches spending and prices through many interest rates, asset prices and the dollar. A standard VAR with one interest rate can miss most of that route, and the omission can produce the puzzles.

\subsubsection{The Standard Transmission Mechanism}
Monetary policy sets the short-term interest rate today and, through its communications, shapes what people expect that rate to be in the future. Few households and businesses borrow at the policy rate. They face the rates on consumer credit, mortgages and business debt, which arbitrage links to current and expected short-term rates. The same rise in rates raises the return investors require on stocks. For businesses, a higher cost of capital raises the user cost of new equipment and buildings, and investment falls \citep{jorgenson1963,hall1967}. The value of existing capital relative to its replacement cost falls as well \citep{tobin1969}. For households, lower stock prices lower wealth, and consumption depends on wealth as well as on current income \citep{ando1963,friedman1957}. Higher rates also strengthen the dollar, which shifts spending toward imports and reduces exports \citep{fleming1962,mundell1963}.

Interest rates of many maturities, equity prices and the dollar therefore form the core of the transmission mechanism \citep{boivin2010}. The same links appear in modern models of the business cycle, in closed and open economies \citep{christiano2005,smets2007,gali2005}. Models with heterogeneous households give more weight to the indirect effects that work through income \citep{kaplan2018,auclert2019}. But in all of these models the short-term rate matters mainly because of what it does to the prices and rates that people actually face. The effects come with a lag \citep{friedman1961}.

What matters in these models is not the nominal rate but the real rate relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. The same holds for the other parts of financial conditions: a given yield or stock valuation is tighter when $\rs$ is low than when it is high. The distinction matters most for inflation. Spending responds to financial conditions within months. Inflation responds to the slack that builds up over time, so it depends on how long, and by how much, conditions stay tight relative to $\rs$. A measure of conditions that ignores a falling $\rs$ will overstate how tight policy has been, and by more the longer the period. That error is largest for the cumulative effects that drive inflation.

\subsubsection{What Standard VARs Leave Out}
The workhorse evidence on monetary policy comes from VARs with a few macroeconomic variables and one interest rate \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. Some studies add a credit spread \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019}, and factor models bring in many more series \citep{bernanke2005favar}. But long-term yields, equity valuations and the dollar are usually absent from the small models used to measure the effects of policy, although some recent work adds the dollar and stock-market volatility \citep{boehm2026}. These are the variables that, on the mechanism above, carry those effects. Concern about missing variables is not new: \citet{sims1992} argued that the price puzzle arises because the Federal Reserve responds to information that small VARs do not contain \citep[see also][]{castelnuovo2010}. My concern is different. Here the missing variables are the channel itself.

A simple two-equation model (Appendix~\ref{app:toy}) shows what this omission can do. If unemployment responds to financial conditions, and financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, a VAR in unemployment and the policy rate lets the rate stand in for financial conditions. Its error contains past financial shocks, which a VAR with few lags cannot capture, so the VAR is misspecified \citep[on missing variables in VARs, see][]{fernandez2007} and the effects of policy are harder to measure.

A second problem matters more for the size and sign of the response: the omission changes the experiment being carried through. A policy surprise that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the FCI by about \bl{dc:rstar:bF} unit in the window. An ordinary rise of 1 percentage point in the yield on a day without an announcement comes with a rise of only \bl{dc:rstar:ord} unit in the FCI (90 percent interval [\bl{dc:rstar:ord:lo}, \bl{dc:rstar:ord:hi}]; \bl{dc:ordm} in monthly data; Appendix~\ref{app:details}). A VAR without financial conditions carries through the ordinary move in the yield, which comes with a much smaller change in financial conditions than a policy surprise does. That the two moves differ is an empirical finding, not a consequence of how the weights are scaled: the weights make the FCI move one for one with the yield in the window by construction, but nothing fixes its ordinary co-movement.

High-frequency surprises help identify the shock, but they do not by themselves fix how it is carried forward. My design therefore puts financial conditions in the system, so that they carry the effects of the surprise forward, and measures the immediate effect of the surprise on the two-year yield and on financial conditions directly in the window, where policy news is the main force at work. The measurement of financial conditions also matters. If activity responds to conditions relative to $\rs$, a measure that ignores $\rs$ differs from the true one by a slow-moving error, and that error enters the equation for unemployment.

\subsubsection{Financial Conditions and Real Activity}
A separate literature asks how financial conditions are related to economic activity. Asset prices help forecast output and inflation, but which ones help changes over time \citep{stock2003}. Credit spreads, and especially their excess bond premium component, are strong predictors of activity \citep{gilchrist2012}. Broad indexes summarize many such series and are used to assess the outlook \citep{hatzius2010,brave2011}. Financial conditions are also linked to the risks around growth, not only to its average \citep{adrian2019}, and their relationship with unemployment may be nonlinear \citep{kiley2020ml}. Federal Reserve staff now publish a measure of the effect of financial conditions on growth, with weights from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}.

This work has a limit that the event-study literature does not. A correlation between financial conditions and later activity need not reflect a causal effect, because financial conditions also respond to the economy. \citet{hatzius2010} address part of this problem by removing the effects of past growth and inflation from their indicators. The companion note uses the timing in a VAR for the same purpose \citep{fcinote}. The two literatures have complementary strengths. Work on high-frequency surprises identifies policy shocks cleanly but carries them through a small set of variables. Work on financial conditions studies a broad set of variables but does not isolate policy shocks. I bring them together: the weights in my index come from how its components respond to policy news, and the surprise is carried forward through the estimated relationship between that index and the economy.


\subsubsection{Policy News Beyond Interest Rates, and Risk Appetite}\label{sec:scope}
Recent work finds that announcements move stock prices and the dollar much more than interest-rate surprises can explain \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. My windows show the same pattern. The policy-news surprise explains \bl{r2:y2} percent of the variance of the move in the two-year yield, but only \bl{r2:y10} percent for the 10-year yield, \bl{r2:sp} percent for the S\&P 500, \bl{r2:usd} percent for the dollar and \bl{r2:fci} percent for the FCI. The part of the FCI's move that the surprise does not explain is therefore large.

There are two readings of this unexplained part. On one, it is mostly news unrelated to policy that happens to arrive in the window, and my estimates capture the effects of policy. On the other, much of it is policy news that does not move interest rates, as \citet{boehm2024} and \citet{bauer2026} argue. Then my estimates are the effects of one dimension of policy news, and the effects of all policy news through financial conditions could be larger. Both readings agree on the channel: policy news reaches the economy through broad financial conditions.

\subsection{Timing Assumptions and Identification}\label{sec:timing}
The shock is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy-news surprise, $s_a$ in equation~\eqref{eq:ns}, from the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The window runs from 10 minutes before the statement to 60 minutes after the press conference starts, or 30 minutes around the statement when there is no press conference. Identification comes from timing, at three frequencies. Table~\ref{tab:timing} summarizes the assumptions.

\textbf{The window.} The window move in the FCI is computed with the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F_a = w_B\, \Delta y^{10}_a + w_Q\, \Delta \text{SP}_a + w_D\, \Delta \text{DXY}_a. \label{eq:winfci}
\end{equation}
These are the measures available within the day, and the ones used to estimate the weights. Percent changes in the S\&P 500 equal percent changes in CAPE within a window, because earnings are fixed. I express all effects per percentage point on the two-year yield in the window. The effect on the FCI is the ratio of two regression slopes on the surprise, an instrumental-variables estimate with $s_a$ as the instrument:
\begin{equation}
b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ b_F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b_F = \frac{\sum_a (\Delta F_a - \overline{\Delta F})\,(s_a-\bar s)}{\sum_a (\Delta y^{2}_a - \overline{\Delta y^{2}})\,(s_a-\bar s)} = 0.99. \label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
This is close to one because the weights are normalized that way. It is not exactly one because this regression includes a constant, which the scaling in equation~\eqref{eq:ns} does not, and because it uses one fewer announcement. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly, so I take the window move to be the same in every version.

\textbf{The day.} On the announcement day, the two-year yield and the FCI shift by the vector $b$. I use the window move rather than the change over the whole day, because the full-day change contains other news. On later days, the two series move as equation~\eqref{eq:daily} implies. They affect each other only through the daily lags, so no ordering between them is needed within the day. The window fixes their joint move.

\textbf{The month.} Within each month, unemployment and inflation are set first, and markets then respond to them. So in the month of the announcement, unemployment and inflation have not moved. They respond from the next month on, through the monthly average of the daily financial series. That average depends on when in the month the announcement falls: an announcement late in a month affects few days of that month's average. This is how the model takes the timing of announcements into account, as \citet{jacobson2026} recommend. The ordering is the familiar recursive one, in which slow-moving macroeconomic variables come before financial variables \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. It also means that the daily equations include the current month's unemployment and inflation, even though those figures are published later. I treat this as a modeling convention: the monthly values summarize conditions that markets observe as the month unfolds.

\textbf{Three steps.} The design separates three steps. The first identifies announcement news: the surprise $s_a$ is policy news measured in a window short enough that, I assume, news about the path of interest rates is the main systematic force correlated with it. The second measures the immediate financial effects of that news, the vector $b$ of equation~\eqref{eq:impact}, in the same window. The third propagates those effects. After the window, the two-year yield and the FCI evolve as the daily equations estimated on ordinary days imply, and unemployment and inflation respond as the monthly equations estimated on ordinary months imply. Only the first two steps use the announcements. The third uses all of the data.

The third step is the assumption that matters most. Once the shock has been identified, the economy responds to a change in the financial variables in the same way whatever caused it. A policy shock identified by a Cholesky ordering or an external instrument is carried through the same estimated dynamics as every other shock, and I do the same. The assumption has a familiar cost. Suppose stock prices fall 5 percent in an announcement window because the surprise raises the rate at which future earnings are discounted. On another day they fall 5 percent because earnings forecasts are cut. The first fall raises the cost of capital and lowers wealth without any news about demand. The second may also reflect weaker demand, which would lower activity whatever the policy rate does. The model carries both through one equation. If the discount-rate fall is less (or more) contractionary than the average fall, the estimated propagation is wrong for the policy surprise. This is a different problem from central-bank information, which concerns what the surprise $s_a$ reveals in step one and which I set aside, as in the companion note. The propagation assumption could fail even if announcements revealed nothing about the economy.

The second assumption is specific to this paper. A standard VAR estimates the effect of the shock on every variable in the system. Here the surprise reaches unemployment and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and has no direct effect on them in the window. Anything else it moves, such as credit spreads or expectations, is left out. So are the moves in stock prices and the dollar in the window that are unrelated to $s_a$; the surprise explains only about a third of those moves (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). The non-yield shock of \citet{boehm2024} is uncorrelated with policy-news surprises, so such moves do not bias $b_F$. But the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights} treat them as noise, and part of them may be policy news \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}.

The third assumption is the scalar-index restriction, separate from the other two. The monthly equations see the FCI as a single index. Its weights show how the three components respond to policy news, not how the economy responds to each. So I assume that the three affect unemployment and prices in proportion to those weights. Section~\ref{sec:components} tests this restriction and shows where it matters.

\textbf{What a yield-only model can and cannot do.} A valid external instrument can recover the total response of unemployment and prices to a shock with the transmission channels left unobserved, under the usual conditions of a proxy VAR: the instrument is correlated with the shock, uncorrelated with other shocks, and the VAR has enough variables and lags to span the dynamics. A model with the yield alone is not wrong by construction. My claim is about the experiment, the finite-lag dynamics and the evidence studied here. In these data, with 12 lags, a model with the yield alone does not reproduce the response to the announcement surprise, because the surprise moves financial conditions by much more than the ordinary movement of the yield does. Comparing the announcement response of the FCI with its ordinary co-movement with the yield shows that the two experiments differ. It does not show how much that difference matters for unemployment and prices. Section~\ref{sec:decomp} does: it separates the effect of the initial financial movement from the effect of the financial state on the estimated dynamics.

\subsection{A Mixed-Frequency VAR}\label{sec:midas}
\textbf{Structure.} The model has a daily block and a monthly block. Let $x_\tau=(R_\tau,F_\tau)'$ hold the two-year yield and the FCI on day $\tau$, in the same version. Let $m_t=(U_t,\pi_t)'$ hold the two monthly variables. The unemployment gap $U_t$ is the unemployment rate minus the CBO estimate of its natural rate. The CBO estimate does not respond to the surprise, so the response of the gap equals that of the unemployment rate. Inflation is $\pi_t=1200\,\Delta\ln P_t$, where $P_t$ is the price index for personal consumption expenditures excluding food and energy (core PCE).

The daily block is a VAR that uses averages of the past day, week, month and quarter as regressors \citep{corsi2009}:
\begin{equation}
x_\tau = \alpha + \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_k\, \bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, m_{t(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, m_{t(\tau)-1} + \xi_\tau, \label{eq:daily}
\end{equation}
where $\bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)}$ is the average of $x$ over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$, and $t(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$. Averages over about a day, a week, a month and a quarter capture persistence with few coefficients. When the FCI is left out, $x_\tau$ is the two-year yield alone.

The monthly block is
\begin{equation}
m_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, m_{t-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \bar x_{t-l} + \zeta_t, \qquad \bar x_{t}=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{\tau\in t} x_\tau, \label{eq:monthly}
\end{equation}
with $n_t$ the number of weekdays in month $t$. The model is a mixed-frequency VAR \citep{ghysels2016}. The equation contains no current-month term in $\bar x_t$, which is the timing assumption of Table~\ref{tab:timing}.

A monthly VAR with the same variables and a recursive ordering is the special case in which every announcement falls on the first day of its month (Appendix~\ref{app:relation}).

\textbf{The time trend.} The baseline monthly equations contain a constant and no time trend. Over 1996--2026, unemployment, inflation and interest rates contain slow movements that a short monthly model may not capture: rates drifted down along with $\rs$, inflation was low in the 2010s and rose after 2020, and the natural rate of unemployment may have shifted in ways the CBO estimate misses.

\textbf{Estimation and inference.} I estimate each block by ordinary least squares, on the same sample: January 1996, the month of the first announcement with all window measures, to September 2026 (\bl{nobs} months, and the weekdays of those months for the daily block). The lagged daily averages and the monthly lags may use earlier observations. 2020 is kept in both blocks. The baseline has $L=12$ monthly lags, fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:lags} shows 3 and 6). Responses are computed recursively, as deviations from a path with no surprise, and averaged over the positions of the announcements within their months (Appendix~\ref{app:details}); the response of the price level after $h$ months is $\sum_{j=0}^{h}\tilde\pi_j/12$. Intervals come from a block bootstrap with 500 draws \citep{kunsch1989,jentsch2019} that resamples calendar months in blocks of 12 and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn (Appendix~\ref{app:boot}). A response is significant at the 5 or 10 percent level when its 95 or 90 percent interval excludes zero. I report 90 percent intervals, as recent studies in this area do \citep{bauer2023,caldara2019,miranda2021,ramey2016}, and 68 percent intervals, which \citet{jarocinski2020} report alongside them. The FCI weights and $\rs$ are held fixed.

\subsection{Standard Methods after the Early 1980s}\label{sec:standard}
Standard identification schemes have not delivered conventional effects of monetary policy in samples that start after the early 1980s. \citet{ramey2016} estimates the specification of \citet{christiano1999} on 1983--2007 and finds that ``the results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the unemployment rate.'' A hybrid VAR with the Romer--Romer shock \citep{romer2004} gives, on the same later sample, contractionary shocks that ``appear to be expansionary,'' as \citet{barakchian2013} found for 1988--2007, and a proxy SVAR with the Romer--Romer shock, estimated on 1969--2007, gives a ``pronounced, statistically significant price puzzle'' (her Figures~1 and~2). \citet{caldara2019} find, for 1994--2007 with a proxy SVAR that uses high-frequency surprises, that without a credit spread the response of industrial production is weak and uncertain (about $-0.2$ percent, with a credible set that includes positive values), against about $-0.75$ percent when the excess bond premium is added.

I start from the same two standard methods, estimated on my own data and units, so that they can be compared directly with the model below. Both use the monthly unemployment gap, core PCE inflation and the end-of-month two-year Treasury yield, with 12 lags and no trend. The first is the recursive (Cholesky) VAR of \citet{christiano1999}, with the policy rate ordered last, so that unemployment and inflation do not respond to it within the month. The second is a proxy VAR \citep{stock2018,mertens2013,gertler2015}, which uses the surprise as an instrument for the policy rate: the instrument is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy news \citep{nakamura2018} summed over the announcements of the month, the same 252 announcements used throughout. All responses are to a 1 percentage point rise in the policy rate. Figure~\ref{fig:standard} and Table~\ref{tab:standard} show the results.

\subsection{Why Adding the FCI Changes the Estimated Response}\label{sec:decomp}
Adding the FCI changes the experiment and the model at once. A policy surprise that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the FCI by \bl{dc:rstar:bF} unit in the window. A yield-only model has no financial index to move. It carries the yield through dynamics estimated without the FCI, where the yield stands in for the whole of financial conditions. To separate the two changes I compute three paths for the neutral-rate version, on the same sample, calendar, horizon and normalization.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Path A} is the baseline: the fitted model with the yield and the FCI, started by a one-point rise in the yield and the window movement of the FCI (\bl{dc:rstar:bF}).
\item \textbf{Path B} is exactly the same fitted model, started by the same yield rise and the movement of the FCI that ordinarily accompanies it: \bl{dc:rstar:ord} units per percentage point (90 percent interval [\bl{dc:rstar:ord:lo}, \bl{dc:rstar:ord:hi}]; Appendix~\ref{app:details}).
\item \textbf{Path C} is the matched model with the yield alone, started by the same yield rise.
\end{itemize}
The ordinary movement is the covariance of the FCI and yield innovations of the daily block divided by the variance of the yield innovation, on days without an announcement. These are the observations nearest to the announcement window in timing, but they are close-to-close changes over a whole day rather than changes in 30 to about 100 minutes. Path B is therefore a model counterfactual, not an observed event. The loading of about one in the window follows partly from the normalization of the weights to policy news. The ordinary loading is empirical evidence, and it is much smaller.

The contrasts are as follows. A $-$ C is the effect of adding the FCI. A $-$ B isolates the initial FCI movement, holding the dynamics fixed. B $-$ C is what remains: the difference associated with including the financial state in the model and re-estimating the propagation of the yield. It has no unique structural interpretation. All three paths and their differences use the same bootstrap draws, with the impact slopes re-estimated in each, so A $-$ B and B $-$ C add to A $-$ C at every horizon and in every draw. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the paths and Table~\ref{tab:decomp} the magnitudes after 24 months.

\subsection{The Implied Inflation-Unemployment Tradeoff}\label{sec:tradeoff}
A flat Phillips curve raises the concern that lowering inflation requires a large and lasting rise in unemployment. The question of what disinflation costs is an old one, asked in the 1970s by \citet{okun1978} and \citet{tobin1980}. The joint responses of unemployment and prices to a monetary surprise give one answer. They measure a tradeoff at a stated horizon, along the path that policy actually takes to lower prices, and so can reflect the exchange rate and expectations as well as the slope of the Phillips curve. What matters for policy is the ratio of the price response to the unemployment response. Following \citet{barnichon2021}, I measure it by the Phillips multiplier at horizon $H$, the cumulative price response per unit of cumulative unemployment response:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}_H = \frac{\tilde P_H}{\tilde U_H}, \qquad \tilde U_H=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\tilde U_h, \label{eq:pm}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde P_H$ is the response of the core price level after $H$ months, in percent, and $\tilde U_H$ is the cumulative response of the unemployment gap, in percentage-point years. Because the price level cumulates inflation, $\mathcal{P}_H$ is the cumulative change in inflation per unit of cumulative unemployment. Its inverse, $-1/\mathcal{P}_H$, is a sacrifice ratio: the percentage-point years of unemployment needed to lower the price level 1 percent. A more negative $\mathcal{P}_H$ means a steeper Phillips curve.

$\tilde U_H$ can be close to zero, and then the ratio is not well defined. So I form 90 percent sets for $\mathcal{P}_H$ in the spirit of \citet{anderson1949}: the set contains every value $\mathcal{P}$ for which the 90 percent bootstrap interval of $\tilde P_H-\mathcal{P}\,\tilde U_H$ contains zero. The sets are computed exactly, not on a grid, and can be unbounded, which shows when the data cannot pin down the tradeoff. Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} reports $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{36}$ for each version and for the main sample variations.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_tradeoff}

The tradeoff can be measured in the $\rs$ version with the FCI. With the two-year yield and FCI relative to $\rs$, $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24} with a 90 percent set of [\bllo{tr:rstar:yes:24}, \blhi{tr:rstar:yes:24}] and $\mathcal{P}_{36}$ is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} with [\bllo{tr:rstar:yes:36}, \blhi{tr:rstar:yes:36}]; both sets exclude zero. The sacrifice ratio, the inverse of $\mathcal{P}_{24}$ in absolute value, is \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment per 1 percent lower price level after 24 months, with a 90 percent set of \bl{tr:sac:24:lo} to \bl{tr:sac:24:hi}. It is distinct from the three-year multiplier of \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} (a ratio of \bl{tr:sac:36} percentage-point years per 1 percent after 36 months) and from the cost of a permanent one-point decline in annual inflation. Where cumulative unemployment is imprecise the ratio is not pinned down, as the unbounded sets below show.

Elsewhere the data pin the tradeoff down less well. In the nominal and real versions with the FCI, and for the yield alone relative to $\rs$, the sets are unbounded: the cumulative unemployment response is too close to zero to pin down the ratio, which shows that the adjustment for $\rs$ matters for the tradeoff. With the nominal or real yield alone the multiplier is close to zero and the sets include zero: a tightening that does not raise unemployment says nothing about the cost of lowering inflation. Across the variations in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}, the set excludes zero when 2020 is dropped (\bl{trs:R1b:24} after 24 months from 1996 and \bl{trs:R1c:24} from 1973) and, after 24 months, for the sample from 1973 that ends in 2019. It is unbounded for the sample from 1973 and for the sample from 1996 that ends in 2019. With Kiley's $\rs$ the set from 1996 includes zero, and with the spline trend and real measures it is unbounded. The last row gives the difference between the multipliers of the version relative to $\rs$ and the nominal version; its interval includes zero.

For comparison, \citet{barnichon2021} report a multiplier of about $-0.15$ after three to four years for 1990--2008, estimated with high-frequency surprises, and a multiplier that diverges before 1990 and is about $-3$ after five years, estimated with Romer--Romer shocks. Their multiplier is the ratio of the cumulative response of inflation to the cumulative response of unemployment, as in equation~\eqref{eq:pm}. At the baseline horizons it lies between the two: \bl{tr:rstar:yes:36} after three years.

\subsection{Which Financial Variables Carry the Response}\label{sec:components}
The baseline assumes that the three parts of the FCI, the 10-year yield, equity valuation and the dollar, affect unemployment and prices in proportion to the weights with which they respond to policy news. That is an assumption about the economy, not about the surprise, and it can be relaxed and tested. I compare three specifications of the monthly equations. In the first the FCI enters as one variable. In the second equities enter separately, and the 10-year yield and the dollar enter as one variable, their sum. In the third all three enter separately. The daily block is the same in all three: the two-year yield and the three weighted components, each with its own window impact (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The specifications therefore differ only in the monthly equations, and the three share their bootstrap draws. The first column of Table~\ref{tab:components} is the baseline with its own daily block; the second shows that using the components' daily block changes little.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_components}

Freeing equities leaves most of the price response in place, and freeing the dollar as well removes it, from 1996. After 24 months the price response is \bl{cp:1996:R:P} percent with the FCI as one variable (90 percent interval [\bl{cp:1996:R:P:lo}, \bl{cp:1996:R:P:hi}]), \bl{cp:1996:E:P} with equities free ([\bl{cp:1996:E:P:lo}, \bl{cp:1996:E:P:hi}]) and \bl{cp:1996:F:P} with all three free ([\bl{cp:1996:F:P:lo}, \bl{cp:1996:F:P:hi}]). The difference between the last and the middle specification, \bl{cp:1996:FE:P} ([\bl{cp:1996:FE:P:lo}, \bl{cp:1996:FE:P:hi}]), is what separating the dollar from the yield does; freeing equities alone changes the response by \bl{cp:1996:ER:P} ([\bl{cp:1996:ER:P:lo}, \bl{cp:1996:ER:P:hi}]). In the inflation equation the lagged coefficients on the three weighted components sum to \bl{cp:coef:96:pi:y10} for the 10-year yield (standard error \bl{cp:coef:96:pi:y10:se}), \bl{cp:coef:96:pi:eq} for equities (\bl{cp:coef:96:pi:eq:se}) and \bl{cp:coef:96:pi:usd} for the dollar (\bl{cp:coef:96:pi:usd:se}). A conditional coefficient of this kind, or the response simulated from it, does not identify the effect of an exogenous change in the dollar, because the dollar's coefficient conditions on the yield and on equities, which move with it. It does show that the price result needs the dollar and the yield to enter in proportion.

The tests in Table~\ref{tab:weightstest} agree. With equities free, equality of the yield and dollar coefficients is rejected in the inflation equation from 1996 at the 10 percent level in the nominal, real and neutral-rate versions (bootstrap $p$ of \bl{wt2:96:yd:nom:pi}, \bl{wt2:96:yd:real:pi} and \bl{wt2:96:yd:rstar:pi}; the nominal version at the 5 percent level) and not in the unemployment equation (\bl{wt2:96:yd:nom:u}, \bl{wt2:96:yd:real:u} and \bl{wt2:96:yd:rstar:u}). With the yield and dollar equal, equality with equities is rejected at the 10 percent level in the neutral-rate version only (\bl{wt2:96:eq:rstar:pi}). From 1973 the three versions of the price response are alike (\bl{cp:1973:R:P}, \bl{cp:1973:E:P} and \bl{cp:1973:F:P}) and no test rejects (the smallest $p$ in the inflation equation is \bl{wt2:73:yd:real:pi}). So the restriction that matters for prices is the one that ties the dollar to the 10-year yield. Equities do not need to be tied to them, and unemployment does not need the restriction in either sample: the unemployment response is \bl{cp:1996:R:u}, \bl{cp:1996:E:u} and \bl{cp:1996:F:u} percentage point after 24 months from 1996, and the differences between the specifications are not significant.

\subsection{Financial Conditions in the Window}\label{sec:resolve}
The model assumes that policy reaches unemployment and prices through the two-year yield and the FCI, the broad index of the 10-year yield, equity valuations and the real dollar, weighted by their responses to policy news (Table~\ref{tab:weights}). In the window the FCI moves with the two-year yield: a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the FCI by \bl{bF} (in the same units). A model with the two-year yield alone omits this channel. Figure~\ref{fig:financial} shows the responses of the two-year yield and the FCI to the policy move in the baseline. \section{Robustness}\label{sec:rob}
A result about the transmission of monetary policy can rest on one sample, one estimate of $\rs$, one choice of trend, one set of omitted variables or one surprise series. I vary each in turn. Each variation changes one feature of the baseline and keeps the rest: the shocks are the same announcements (except where the surprise itself changes), the monthly equations have 12 lags, and responses are to a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point in the window. Table~\ref{tab:robsummary} shows the baseline and the 15 variations. Thirteen of them keep the baseline yield and FCI relative to $\rs$; the other two replace the FCI with its three components, which Section~\ref{sec:components} examines.

\input{../baseline/report/tab_rob_summary}

With the FCI, the wrong-signed unemployment response disappears in every variation. Without the FCI, unemployment after 12 months falls in \bl{rob:no_uneg} of the 13 variations that keep the FCI relative to $\rs$; with it, unemployment rises in \bl{rob:upos} after 24 months and never falls significantly at that horizon. The price level falls after 24 months in all 13 variations, and the fall is significant at the 5 percent level in \bl{rob:Psig5}. The three exceptions are all from 1996: the sample that ends in 2019, Kiley's $\rs$ and the spline trend. The subsections say what each variation tests and what it shows. When the three components enter separately, the price response from 1996 is not negative, and Section~\ref{sec:components} shows which restriction accounts for that.

\subsection{The Sample}\label{sec:recent}
Three features of the sample could drive the result. It is short, so the monetary environment since 1996 may be special. It contains the pandemic, when unemployment moved by more than in any other period. And it ends with 2022--24, the years of the largest tightening in the sample, so the result could rest on them. I vary the start and the end. In the first variation the monthly equations start in 1973 rather than 1996; the months before 1990, for which there are no daily data, use monthly series in place of the daily block, the daily block is still estimated from 1996, and the shocks are still the announcements from 1996. In the second, March to December 2020 are dropped. In the third, all equations end in December 2019. Each is run with the monthly equations starting in 1996 and in 1973.

The two restrictions remove different data from different blocks. Dropping March to December 2020 removes those ten months from the monthly equations for unemployment and inflation and their days from the daily block. The 2020 values still enter as lags, and the announcements, their window impacts and the bootstrap draws are unchanged. Ending the sample in December 2019 removes all of January 2020 to September 2026 from the same two blocks. It leaves the announcements unchanged: the \bl{sm:nev:post} announcements after 2019 (of \bl{sm:nev:all}) still define the surprise and its immediate effect on the yield and the FCI, and enter the bootstrap draws. The exercises therefore differ in how many months of macro data they remove, and neither isolates the inflation surge of 2021--22.

From 1996 the price response depends on the years after 2019. With the sample ending in 2019 the price response after 24 months is \bl{sm:end96:P:24} percent, with a 90 percent interval of [\bl{sm:end96:P:24:lo}, \bl{sm:end96:P:24:hi}], against \bl{sm:base96:P:24} in the full sample. When the monthly equations start in 1973 the corresponding numbers are \bl{sm:end73:P:24}, significant at the 5 percent level, and \bl{sm:base73:P:24}. Core inflation varied little in the shorter sample: the standard deviation of monthly annualized inflation is \bl{sm:pi:sd:9619} percentage points for 1996--2019, \bl{sm:pi:sd:7319} for 1973--2019 and \bl{sm:pi:sd:2026} for 2020--2026. With little variation in inflation before 2020, the monthly equations of the shorter sample have little information about how inflation responds to financial conditions, and the pattern is consistent with the 2020--26 months, among them 2021--22, contributing that information. Dropping 2020 alone leaves the price response (\bl{sm:out96:P:24} from 1996 and \bl{sm:out73:P:24} from 1973, both significant at the 5 percent level) and makes the unemployment response larger from 1996 (\bl{rb:R1b:yes:u:24}, significant at the 5 percent level). Starting in 1973 leaves unemployment close to zero (\bl{rb:R1a:yes:u:24} after 24 months), and the yield alone moves it little or raises it, so the wrong-signed response that the FCI removes belongs to the sample since 1996. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR1} gives the full results.

\subsection{The $r^*$ Adjustment and Trends}\label{sec:rstarrob}
The neutral rate is not observed, and the baseline uses one estimate of it, that of Laubach and Williams. Two variations check that the result does not depend on that choice. The first replaces it with Kiley's estimate, in the two-year yield and in the FCI alike. The second uses no $\rs$ measure at all: it keeps the real two-year yield and the real FCI and adds a natural cubic spline in time to every monthly equation, with knots at the sample ends and at the NBER business-cycle peaks inside the sample. The price result would be an artifact of the $\rs$ estimate if it vanished here.

The FCI removes the fall in unemployment in both. With Kiley's $\rs$ unemployment after 12 months is \bl{rb:R2a:no:u:12} without the FCI and \bl{rb:R2a:yes:u:12} with it; with real measures and the spline it is \bl{rb:R2c:no:u:12} and \bl{rb:R2c:yes:u:12}. The price response is smaller than in the baseline and, from 1996, not significant (\bl{rb:R2a:yes:P:24} and \bl{rb:R2c:yes:P:24} after 24 months). From 1973 it is significant at the 5 percent level in both (\bl{rb:R2b:yes:P:24} and \bl{rb:R2d:yes:P:24}). The choice of $\rs$ therefore matters for the size of the price response, and the sign pattern of unemployment does not depend on it. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR2} gives the full results.

\subsection{Credit Conditions}\label{sec:credit}
Credit is the most important channel that the baseline leaves out \citep{gilchrist2012,caldara2019}, and a premium that responds to policy could carry part of what the FCI carries. The variation adds the monthly excess bond premium as a third monthly variable, treated like unemployment and inflation, so that the premium responds to the financial variables and they enter its equation as well.

With the FCI relative to $\rs$ the results are those of the baseline: unemployment rises (\bl{rb:R3a:yes:u:24} after 24 months) and the price level falls (\bl{rb:R3a:yes:P:24}); from 1973 the price level falls by \blabs{rb:R3b:yes:P:24} percent. Without the FCI the unemployment response moves toward zero (\bl{rb:R3a:no:u:12} after 12 months, against \bl{rstar:no:u:12} in the baseline), in the direction that adding the FCI moves it. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR3} gives the full results.

\subsection{The Surprise}\label{sec:surprise}
The baseline uses the Nakamura--Steinsson policy news. An announcement can reveal the Fed's view of the economy as well as a change in policy, and part of each surprise can be predicted from public data. Two variations change the surprise. The first uses the orthogonalized surprise of \citet{bauer2023}, which removes the part of each surprise that public information predicts; it is available for announcements through 2019 (\bl{ev:bs} announcements). The second keeps the Nakamura--Steinsson surprise only for announcements in which the two-year yield and the S\&P 500 move in opposite directions in the window, the sign restriction of \citet{jarocinski2020} (\bl{ev:jk} announcements).

With the two-year yield alone the responses are the same as in the baseline, because the shock is scaled to the yield. With the FCI they are larger: unemployment rises \bl{rb:R5a:yes:u:24} and \bl{rb:R5b:yes:u:24} after 24 months, against \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} in the baseline, and the price level falls \blabs{rb:R5a:yes:P:24} and \blabs{rb:R5b:yes:P:24} percent, against \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24}. The price response is significant at the 5 percent level and the unemployment response at the 10 percent level. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mainR5} gives the full results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:policy}
Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements measure policy shocks well, at least the part of policy news that moves interest rates. The hard part has been tracing their effects on the economy. I kept the surprise as it is and changed how its effects are traced: through broad financial conditions rather than one interest rate, and relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. Traced this way, the responses of unemployment and prices have the standard signs, where the nominal two-year yield alone and the standard methods give the wrong ones. Most of the difference comes from the size of the financial movement that a policy surprise brings in the window, more than three times the movement that accompanies an ordinary rise in the yield, and little from re-estimating the dynamics.

For policy, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point lowers the core price level by \blabs{rstar:yes:P:24} percent after two years and raises unemployment by \bl{rstar:yes:u:24} percentage point. Lowering the core price level 1 percent costs about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment over two years, and the ratio lies between earlier estimates for the periods after and before 1990. The results support judging the stance by financial conditions relative to $\rs$, as the FOMC did in 2023 \citep{powell2023}, and they explain why the same policy rate can be more or less restrictive at different times. The estimates are effects of surprises, not a decomposition of any episode.

The most durable finding is about unemployment. Once policy news is traced through financial conditions, a tightening does not lower unemployment significantly at 12, 24 or 36 months in the baseline or in any of the variations I tried. The policy interpretation is the cost of price restraint: about \bl{tr:rstar:yes:24:sac} percentage-point years of unemployment for each 1 percent lower core price level after two years.

\section{The Financial Conditions Index}\label{app:index}
\textbf{Policy news.} Let $\Delta X_a$ be the $5\times 1$ vector of changes in the current- and next-meeting federal funds futures and the second to fourth eurodollar futures in the window around announcement $a$. Following \citet{nakamura2018}, let $p_a$ be the first principal component of these changes, signed so that it moves with the fourth eurodollar future. Policy news is $p_a$ rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield $y^{2}$ by 1 percentage point:
\begin{equation}
s_a = \gamma\, p_a, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\sum_a p_a\, \Delta y^{2}_a}{\sum_a p_a^{2}}. \label{eq:ns}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Weights.} Let $\Delta A_a$ be the vector of changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the dollar index (DXY) in the window. The companion note treats these changes as
\begin{equation}
\Delta A_a = \psi\, s_a + \eta_a, \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ holds the policy responses and $\eta_a$ is other news, with covariance matrix $\Sigma$. It estimates $\psi$ by regressing each element of $\Delta A_a$ on $s_a$ and a constant, and $\Sigma$ from the residuals, over 253 announcements from January 1996 to September 2026. The weights are those that recover the policy component most precisely (one-factor generalized least squares):
\begin{equation}
w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\psi}{\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi}. \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Because $w'\psi=1$ and $s_a$ is scaled to the two-year yield, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit. The weights are 0.61 on the 10-year yield, $-0.051$ per percent on stock valuations and 0.065 per percent on the dollar. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy.

\textbf{Three versions.} In month $t$, the index in version $v$ is
\begin{equation}
F_t^{v} = w_B\, B_t^{v} + w_Q\, Q_t^{v} + w_D\, D_t, \label{eq:fci}
\end{equation}
measured as a deviation from its sample mean. Here $D_t$ is 100 times the log of the real broad dollar in every version. With $y^{10}_t$ the 10-year yield, $\pi^{10}_t$ 10-year expected inflation, and $(E/P)_t$ the inverse of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE), the other two terms are:
\begin{align}
\text{Nominal:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:nom}\\
\text{Real:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:real}\\
\text{$\rs$-gap (baseline):}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t - \rs_t, & Q_t &= -\frac{100}{\overline{E/P}}\left[(E/P)_t - \rs_t\right]. \label{eq:rstar}
\end{align}
In the $\rs$-gap version, stocks are measured by the gap between their earnings yield and $\rs$. The term $100/\overline{E/P}$, with $\overline{E/P}$ the sample average, converts this gap to the units of the log CAPE: since $d\ln\text{CAPE}=-d(E/P)/(E/P)$, a change in the earnings yield of $x$ points near the mean equals a change of $100x/\overline{E/P}$ in 100 ln CAPE with the opposite sign. The sign reversal and the scaling mean that the weight on stocks is the same in every version. CAPE is a real valuation measure, so stocks do not change between the nominal and real versions. For $\rs$ I use the New York Fed's current Laubach--Williams estimates \citep{holston2023}. These ``one-sided'' estimates use only data up to each date, with the model's parameters estimated on the full sample.

\textbf{The two-year yield.} The two-year yield is adjusted in the same three versions. With $y^{2}_t$ the nominal yield and $\pi^{2}_t$ two-year expected inflation \citep[from the Cleveland Fed;][]{haubrich2012},
\begin{equation}
R_t^{\text{nominal}} = y^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\text{real}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\rs\text{-gap}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t - \rs_t. \label{eq:twoyear}
\end{equation}
Expected inflation and $\rs$ are monthly or quarterly and move slowly. The daily series add their interpolated monthly values to daily market prices, so day-to-day moves come from markets (Appendix~\ref{app:details}).

\section{A Two-Equation Illustration}\label{app:toy}
Let $U_t$ be the unemployment gap, $i_t$ the policy rate and $f_t$ financial conditions, with a higher $f_t$ meaning tighter conditions:
\begin{align}
U_t &= \rho_U\,U_{t-1} + \kappa\,f_{t-1} + \varepsilon^{U}_t, \label{eq:y}\\
f_t &= \rho_f\,f_{t-1} + \lambda\,i_t + \varepsilon^{f}_t. \label{eq:f}
\end{align}
Unemployment responds to financial conditions and not directly to the policy rate. Financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, $\varepsilon^{f}_t$, such as changes in risk appetite. Some of these other forces may themselves be policy news that does not move interest rates \citep{boehm2024,bauer2026}. Suppose the economist observes only $U_t$ and $i_t$. To remove $f_t$, lag equation~\eqref{eq:y} one month, multiply it by $\rho_f$, subtract it from equation~\eqref{eq:y}, and use equation~\eqref{eq:f}. This gives
\begin{equation}
U_t = (\rho_U+\rho_f)\,U_{t-1} - \rho_U\rho_f\,U_{t-2} + \kappa\lambda\,i_{t-1} + \underbrace{\varepsilon^{U}_t - \rho_f\,\varepsilon^{U}_{t-1} + \kappa\,\varepsilon^{f}_{t-1}}_{\text{error}}. \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
The policy rate has no direct effect in this model. It appears in equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} because it stands in for financial conditions. The error is a moving average that includes past financial shocks, so a VAR in $U_t$ and $i_t$ with few lags cannot capture it. The VAR is misspecified. With the lags and samples available in practice, this can make the effects of monetary policy harder to measure.

\section{Relation to a Monthly VAR and a Proxy VAR}\label{app:relation}
Suppose the daily block were replaced by a monthly equation for $\bar x_t$, and every announcement occurred on the first day of the month. With $Z_t=(m_t',\bar x_t')'$, the model would then be a standard monthly VAR with a recursive ordering, the macroeconomic variables first, and impact vector $(0,0,b')'$. The daily block adds two things: the position of each announcement in its month, and fast dynamics of the financial variables, estimated on many more observations. A proxy VAR \citep{gertler2015,mertens2013,stock2018} instead estimates $Z_t=\sum_l C_l Z_{t-l}+\iota_t$ and uses as an instrument $z_t$ the surprises summed over the month. The effect on the FCI per unit effect on the two-year yield is then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{F,t},z_t)}{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{R,t},z_t)}. \label{eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
The window ratio $b_F$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact} has the same form, with three differences. It replaces monthly VAR residuals with changes measured inside the window, which do not depend on how strongly the surprise predicts monthly residuals. It sets the entries for unemployment and inflation to zero rather than estimating them. And it dates each shock to its day rather than summing shocks over a month. Section~\ref{sec:monthlyvar} estimates both monthly benchmarks. In large samples a VAR of this kind estimates the same effects as local projections \citep{plagborg2021}. In the language of mixed-data sampling, the monthly equation gives equal weights to the days within a month and unrestricted weights to the months \citep{ghysels2007,foroni2015}.

\section{The Bootstrap}\label{app:boot}
Every interval in the note comes from a block bootstrap. Each draw resamples the \bl{nobs} calendar months from January 1996 in moving blocks of 12 months and uses together the monthly residuals, the daily observations and the announcements of the months drawn, which keeps the dependence between them. The announcements drawn give new values of $b$ and new positions. The monthly equations are re-estimated on a series rebuilt recursively from the drawn residuals, with the macro lags taken from the simulated series and the financial lags from the data, and the daily block is re-estimated on the drawn daily observations. When the monthly equations start in 1973, the residuals before 1996 are drawn in blocks of 12 months of their own. A month without a monthly observation (2020 dropped, or a sample ending in 2019) contributes its daily observations and announcements but no residual. A simpler bootstrap resamples the three sources of sampling variation separately: the announcements and the monthly residuals independently across months (with the monthly lags held at their values in the data), and the daily observations in moving blocks of 66 days. I ran it with 12 lags and 500 draws for the nominal, real and $\rs$-relative two-year yield, each without and with the FCI of the same version. Across the \bl{bs:n} responses at 12, 24 and 36 months, its 90 percent intervals are wider than those of the block bootstrap in \bl{bs:wider}, by a median of \bl{bs:wr:med} times (range \bl{bs:wr:min} to \bl{bs:wr:max}). The two agree on whether a response is significant at the 10 percent level in \bl{bs:same10} of the \bl{bs:n} responses and at the 5 percent level in \bl{bs:same5}. The price response of the baseline is significant at the 5 percent level at every horizon under both. The 90 percent interval of the baseline unemployment response after 24 months is [\bl{bs:u24:lo5}, \bl{bs:u24:hi5}] under the block bootstrap and [\bl{bs:u24:lo}, \bl{bs:u24:hi}] under the simpler one.

\section{Financial Persistence beyond the Announcement Window}\label{app:persist}
The monthly equations respond to the monthly average of the daily financial variables, so the model's answer depends on how long the window movement of the yield and the FCI lasts. The daily block (equation~\eqref{eq:daily}) is estimated on ordinary days. I check it against the data in the days after announcements.

\textbf{Definitions.} Day 0 is the announcement day and day $h$ is $h$ trading days later. The model sets the level of each variable on day 0 equal to its window movement and propagates it from there. I build the direct estimate on the same clock. The change on day $h$ is the window movement plus the change in the closing level from day 0 to day $h$, $D_h=w_a+(x_{a,h}-x_{a,0})$, so day 0 is the window movement itself. The endpoints are $D_5$, $D_{22}$ and $D_{66}$. The average is the mean of $D_0,\dots,D_{21}$, 22 trading days with the announcement day included, which corresponds to the first month of the monthly average. The model works on weekdays and the data on trading days, so 66 weekdays are about 63 to 65 trading days. As a check I also build the change from the close before the announcement, $x_{a,h}-x_{a,-1}$. The two differ by the part of the announcement day's close-to-close move that the window does not contain, the window-to-close gap in the notes of Table~\ref{tab:persist}.

\textbf{Estimation.} Each $D_h$ is regressed on the policy-news surprise over the \bl{pe:n} announcements with 66 trading days after them, the common set for all horizons, and the slope is divided by the slope of the window movement of the two-year yield on the same announcements. The unit is then a one-point rise in the yield in the window, as in the model. Announcements less than 66 trading days apart overlap. Two things address this. Intervals come from moving blocks of 12 calendar months, resampling the announcements of the months drawn and re-estimating the normalization, the scheme of the main bootstrap. And a second estimate adds the sum of the surprises of the announcements that follow within the horizon as a control. The model paths are those of the baseline, evaluated at the same trading-day horizons, with the same bootstrap draws.

\textbf{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:persist} and Figure~\ref{fig:persist} give the direct estimates and the model. For the two-year yield the direct estimates are \bl{pe:y:h5} at 5 days, \bl{pe:y:h22} at 22 days and \bl{pe:y:h66} at 66 days, against \bl{pe:y:h5:model}, \bl{pe:y:h22:model} and \bl{pe:y:h66:model} in the model; every model value lies inside the 90 percent interval of the direct estimate, and so does the model's first-month average (\bl{pe:y:avg22:model} against \bl{pe:y:avg22}, interval [\bl{pe:y:avg22:lo}, \bl{pe:y:avg22:hi}]). For the FCI the direct estimates are \bl{pe:f:h5} at 5 days and \bl{pe:f:h22} at 22 days against the model's \bl{pe:f:h5:model} and \bl{pe:f:h22:model}, again inside the intervals. At 66 days the direct estimate is \bl{pe:f:h66} with an interval of [\bl{pe:f:h66:lo}, \bl{pe:f:h66:hi}], which contains the model's \bl{pe:f:h66:model} and also a complete reversal: the data cannot distinguish persistence from decay at that horizon. Adding the surprises of later announcements as controls changes none of these conclusions.

The one difference is the first-month average of the FCI. On the model's clock the direct estimate is \bl{pe:f:avg22} (interval [\bl{pe:f:avg22:lo}, \bl{pe:f:avg22:hi}]) and the model's value \bl{pe:f:avg22:model} lies below it. The difference comes from the announcement day. The close-to-close move of the FCI on the announcement day is \bl{pe:f:gap} [\bl{pe:f:gap:lo}, \bl{pe:f:gap:hi}] below the window move: part of the window's movement in stock prices, long yields and the dollar has reversed by the close. Built from the close before the announcement, the average is \bl{pe:f:avg22:base} and the model lies inside the interval. The window-to-close gap is also a statement about the starting point: if the FCI started the model at the closing level and not at the window level, the 24-month responses would be \bl{pe:sens:gap:u24} percentage point for unemployment and \bl{pe:sens:gap:P24} percent for the price level different, which is a third of path A $-$ C in Section~\ref{sec:decomp} and does not change its sign.

\textbf{What the differences imply for the macro response.} A sensitivity calculation traces the macro response through the monthly equations of the baseline, holding the daily block fixed. If the event month's average of the FCI and the yield are those of the direct estimate on the model's clock (the FCI \bl{pe:sens:w:df} higher and the yield \bl{pe:sens:w:dy} lower), the responses after 24 months change by \bl{pe:sens:w:u24} percentage point for unemployment and \bl{pe:sens:w:P24} percent for the price level. If the same excess lasted through the first three months, the changes are \bl{pe:sens:w:u24x3} and \bl{pe:sens:w:P24x3}. The first-month average therefore does not matter for the results. Persistence at longer horizons would. If the FCI path from the second month on were scaled by the ratio of the direct to the model estimate at 66 days (\bl{pe:rho:pt}), the responses after 24 months would be \bl{pe:rho:pt:u24} for unemployment and \bl{pe:rho:pt:P24} for the price level, against \bl{pe:rho:base:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:base:P24}; with the ratio at the ends of the 90 percent interval (\bl{pe:rho:lo} and \bl{pe:rho:hi}) they would be \bl{pe:rho:lo:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:lo:P24}, and \bl{pe:rho:hi:u24} and \bl{pe:rho:hi:P24}. The data at 66 days cannot narrow that range. The check shows that the model's persistence is consistent with the days after announcements. It cannot show that it is right beyond the first month.

\section{A Direct Projection on the Surprise}\label{app:direct}
The main model imposes that the surprise reaches unemployment and prices through the yield and the FCI. A direct projection imposes nothing: all channels operate, and no variable of the financial block enters. The surprise, summed over the announcements of a month, is the regressor, and the unemployment gap, or the cumulated core inflation of the months from the announcement month to month $t+h$, is the outcome. The specification, the controls and the inference are in the notes of Table~\ref{tab:direct}. The existing local-projection code of this project (\texttt{experimental/rstar\_case/lp\_unemp.py}) projects unemployment on past values of an index, not on the surprise, and the earlier distributed-lag regressions of the surprise on the monthly outcome (\texttt{experimental/fin\_shocks/legacy/direct\_mfdl.py}) use event-day shocks with a restricted lag shape. Neither instruments a monthly yield innovation. The projection here is the direct response, scaled by the window slope of the yield, with no first stage.

\textbf{Results.} The direct projection has the features of the earlier direct estimates. With macro lags the unemployment response is \bl{dp:m:u:12} percentage point after 12 months and \bl{dp:m:u:24} after 24, and the price level \bl{dp:m:P:12} and \bl{dp:m:P:24} percent. The sign of unemployment is the wrong one and the price response is not negative. The estimates are noisy: the 90 percent bootstrap intervals include zero for all four ([\bl{dp:m:u:12:lo}, \bl{dp:m:u:12:hi}] for unemployment after 12 months), and Newey--West intervals exclude zero for unemployment at both horizons and for the price level after 24 months. When the lags of the yield and the FCI are added to the controls, the estimates change sign for unemployment (\bl{dp:f:u:12} and \bl{dp:f:u:24}) and the price level stays above zero (\bl{dp:f:P:12} and \bl{dp:f:P:24}); no interval excludes zero. The main model's responses lie inside the bootstrap intervals of both projections at both horizons, except for the price level after 24 months in the first projection.

The direct projection allows all transmission channels to operate, including the exchange rate, expectations and the response of policy to the economy after the announcement, and nothing is imposed. A perverse estimate does not show that omitting the FCI biases the result, and I do not control for the contemporaneous FCI. It shows that the direct response of the economy to the surprise, taken at face value, does not have the signs that the theory gives, and that the data do not pin it down. The sign change when lagged financial conditions are added is a feature of the estimates, not a test of the model. The proxy VAR of Table~\ref{tab:monthlycompact} is the benchmark that keeps the monthly dynamics and is therefore the comparison for the main model.

\section{Construction and Estimation Details}\label{app:details}
\textbf{Events.} I use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available in the U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. As in the companion note, I leave out unscheduled announcements with no change in the policy rate, and March--April 2020. The announcements of September and October 2026 are left out because too few later days are available. The DXY index used in the window covers fewer currencies than the broad dollar used in the daily and monthly index, which I treat as a measurement difference.

\textbf{From monthly to daily.} Expected inflation and $\rs$ are not available daily, and they move slowly. I take the monthly gap between $R_t^{v}$ in equation~\eqref{eq:twoyear} and $y^{2}_t$, interpolate it linearly between the middles of adjacent months, and add it to the daily yield. The daily index is
\begin{equation}
F_\tau^{v} = w_B\, y^{10}_\tau + w_Q\, 100\ln\text{CAPE}_\tau + w_D\, 100\ln\text{DOL}_\tau + o_\tau^{v}, \label{eq:dailyfci}
\end{equation}
where $\text{DOL}_\tau$ is the daily nominal broad dollar. The offset $o_\tau^{v}$ is the monthly difference between the index in equation~\eqref{eq:fci} and the average of the first three terms, interpolated in the same way. It also absorbs the difference between the real and the nominal dollar. The monthly averages of the daily series are very close to the monthly measures: the root-mean-square difference is at most 0.02 unit for the FCI and 0.04 percentage point for the two-year yield. The offsets change little from day to day, so day-to-day moves in the index come from market prices. Table~\ref{tab:measures} shows the measures used at each frequency. The daily calendar has every weekday; missing values, such as on holidays, are filled from the previous observation for up to three days. Each daily equation has 13 coefficients with the FCI; each monthly equation has $1+4L$ with the FCI and $1+3L$ without it.

\textbf{Tracing the effect of a surprise.} The response is computed recursively, as a deviation from a path with no surprise. Let the announcement occur on weekday $d_0$ of the announcement month, and let $h=0,1,\ldots,36$ count months from then. Tildes denote responses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item In each month, unemployment and inflation come first: $\tilde m_0=0$, and for $h\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde m_h = \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, \tilde m_{h-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \tilde x^{M}_{h-l}, \label{eq:irfm}
\end{equation}
with terms dated before the announcement month equal to zero, and $\tilde x^{M}_{h}$ the response of the monthly average of $x$.
\item The days of the month follow. Before day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=0$. On day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=b$ from equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. On every later day, with $\tilde x^{(k)}_{\tau-1}$ the average of the responses over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_\tau = \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_{k}\, \tilde x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)-1}, \label{eq:irfd}
\end{equation}
where $h(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$, counted from the announcement month.
\item The monthly average $\tilde x^{M}_h$ then enters equation~\eqref{eq:irfm} for later months.
\end{enumerate}
Months after the announcement month have 21 weekdays. To avoid computing a path for every date, I form six groups of announcement dates by the weekday of the month, in blocks of four days, and compute the path at each group's average day and month length. With $\omega_g$ the share of announcements in group $g$, the reported response is $\sum_g \omega_g\, \tilde m_h^{(g)}$. Because the model is linear, these responses do not depend on the constant or the trend.

\textbf{Three-component specification.} Each component's equation in the daily block contains its own 1-, 5-, 22- and 66-day averages and those of the two-year yield, plus the monthly macro variables, and the two-year yield's equation its own averages and the macro variables. That is 48 coefficients in all, against 26 in the baseline daily block with the FCI. The three window impacts add up to the impact of the FCI.

\textbf{Ordinary movement of the FCI.} The ordinary loading is the covariance of the FCI and yield innovations divided by the variance of the yield innovation, in FCI units per percentage point on the yield, so it is comparable with the window loading $b_F$. For path B of Section~\ref{sec:decomp} I take the innovations from the daily block, the nearest ordinary observations to the announcement window: the residuals of equation~\eqref{eq:daily} on days without an announcement (conditioning on the 1-, 5-, 22- and 66-day averages of the yield and the FCI and on the monthly unemployment gap and inflation of this month and last). The 90 percent interval re-estimates the daily block and the loading on the days of each block-bootstrap draw. The earlier estimate in monthly data is the same ratio from the residuals of a monthly VAR in unemployment, inflation, the end-of-month two-year yield and the end-of-month FCI of the same version, with 12 lags, over 1996 to 2026. Neither measures an announcement. Daily innovations are close-to-close changes that include all of the day's news; monthly innovations add the dynamics within the month. Because the model is linear, the response to any initial FCI movement $\phi$ is the response to the yield alone plus $\phi$ times the response to the FCI, so path B can be recomputed for any loading: with the monthly loading (\bl{dc:ordm}) the A $-$ B contrast after 24 months is \bl{dc:sens:u:phim} for unemployment and \bl{dc:sens:P:phim} for the price level, and with no FCI movement at all (a loading of zero) \bl{dc:sens:u:phi0} and \bl{dc:sens:P:phi0}.

\textbf{Simulation.} The process is the estimated baseline model (relative to $\rs$, with the FCI; 12 lags), on the data's calendar of weekdays from January 1996 to September 2026 (369 months) and the actual announcement days. Within each month the macro variables are set first and the days of the month follow. Daily innovations are drawn with replacement from the model's daily residuals, separately for announcement days and other days; on announcement days the policy impact $b\,s_a$ is first removed from the residual and then added back with a drawn surprise. Monthly innovations are drawn jointly from the monthly residuals. Surprises are drawn with replacement from the 252 observed surprises. The twelve months before the sample and the 70 days before it are the actual 1995 data. On each sample I estimate the proxy VAR with and without the FCI, with the instrument and instrument months as in the data, and the window-impact VAR, taking the impact on the yield and the FCI from the model.

\textbf{Bootstrap.} The draws are those of Appendix~\ref{app:boot}: 500 block-bootstrap draws, from each of which I compute the response and report percentile intervals. The sets for the Phillips multiplier in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} use the same draws.
