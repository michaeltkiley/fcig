\begin{abstract}
\noindent Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements are a clean measure of monetary policy shocks. But their estimated effects on unemployment and inflation since 1994 are often weak or have the wrong sign. We keep the surprise as it is and change only how its effects are traced through the economy. If the surprise is traced through the two-year yield alone, the wrong signs appear. If it is traced through financial conditions as a whole, measured relative to the neutral interest rate $\rs$, the effects have the expected signs and are precisely estimated. A policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the unemployment rate about 0.4 percentage point and lowers industrial production about 1 percent within a year. It lowers core prices about 0.6 percent after two years. Including financial conditions matters most for the unemployment result. Measuring them relative to $\rs$ matters most for the price result, which relies on data since 2020.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
How does monetary policy affect the economy? Standard theory says that a surprise tightening should raise unemployment and lower inflation. Yet empirical work often finds the opposite signs, or effects too imprecise to tell. The problem is worst in samples that start after the mid-1980s, when policy followed the economy more closely and left fewer clear shocks to learn from \citep{ramey2016}.

Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements were meant to solve this. The FOMC makes its decision before the announcement, so market moves in a narrow window around the announcement cannot have caused the decision. The change in interest-rate futures and Treasury yields in that window is then news about policy \citep{kuttner2001,gurkaynak2005}. These surprises measure the effects of policy on asset prices precisely \citep{bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018,swanson2021}. But when they are used to measure effects on unemployment and inflation, the old puzzles often return.

Recent work offers three explanations, each with a fix.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Information.} An announcement reveals the Fed's view of the economy as well as its policy. A surprise tightening can then signal good news about the economy \citep{romer2000,nakamura2018}. \citet{jarocinski2020} separate the two using a simple idea: a pure policy tightening should lower stock prices, so when rates and stocks rise together, the surprise mostly carries information. The pure policy part gives a larger fall in prices \citep[see also][]{miranda2021}.
\item \emph{Predictable surprises.} Part of each surprise can be predicted from public data released before the announcement, so it is not a clean shock. And after 1984 there are few large surprises to learn from. \citet{bauer2023} add surprises from speeches by the Fed Chair and remove the predictable part. Their effects on the economy are larger and more precise \citep[see also][]{bauer2023aer}.
\item \emph{Timing.} Markets react within minutes, but the economist sees unemployment and inflation only as monthly averages. \citet{jacobson2026} show that this mismatch alone can produce wrong signs. When they measure inflation daily, using online prices from 2008 to 2015 and keeping the same surprises and the same method, most of the wrong-signed response disappears.
\end{itemize}
The first two fixes change the surprise. The third changes how the outcome is measured. \citet{gertler2015} and \citet{caldara2019} add a fourth point: credit costs carry part of the effect, and leaving out credit spreads weakens it.

This note offers a new take. We keep the surprise as it is, and we keep the outcomes and the method fixed. We change only how we measure the variables that carry policy to the economy. Standard models carry the surprise forward through one interest rate, measured in nominal terms. That leaves out two basic parts of how monetary policy works.

First, policy affects spending through financial conditions as a whole: the cost of long-term credit, the value of wealth and the exchange rate \citep{boivin2010}. A surprise tightening moves long-term yields, stock prices and the dollar, not only the two-year yield. Policy analysts often summarize these conditions in a financial conditions index \citep{hatzius2010,ajello2023}. \citet{hatzius2010} make the same point: financial conditions respond to policy but also to other forces, so they can diverge from the policy rate, as in 2004--06, when conditions stayed easy while the funds rate rose.

Second, what matters for spending and inflation is the level of real interest rates compared with the neutral rate $\rs$. The neutral rate is the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. It fell a lot from the 1990s to the 2010s \citep{laubach2003,holston2017,holston2023,hamilton2016,kiley2020}, and it shaped the long-run trend in yields \citep{bauer2020}. Since 1994, interest rates have trended down along with $\rs$, while activity has trended up. A model that links the level of rates to activity then partly picks up these opposite trends. It finds that higher rates go with stronger activity, which looks like a wrong-signed effect of policy.

The two parts need not matter equally for every outcome. Activity responds to changes in financial conditions. Inflation depends on how long real rates stay above or below $\rs$, through the slack this creates. So measuring rates relative to $\rs$ should matter most for prices.

Our design is simple. The shock is the policy-news surprise of \citet{nakamura2018}, measured in narrow windows around 252 FOMC announcements from 1996 to 2026. We read its effect on the two-year yield, and on a market-based financial conditions index (FCI), from these windows. We assume that unemployment and inflation do not move within the windows. A daily model carries the two-year yield and the FCI forward from the announcement day. Monthly unemployment and core inflation then respond to the monthly averages of these daily series, as in a standard monthly model (a vector autoregression, or VAR). Like \citet{jacobson2026}, we take the timing of each announcement within its month into account. The FCI and the order of steps---nominal, real, then relative to $\rs$---come from a companion note on financial conditions \citep{fcinote}. We fixed them before this analysis.

The evidence builds in four steps. Every step includes a smooth time trend (a quadratic) in the monthly equations.
\begin{itemize}
\item With the nominal two-year yield as the only channel, the post-1994 puzzles appear. The unemployment response changes with the number of past months in the model (lags). With 12 lags it is negative, and its 90 percent interval excludes zero. Below, ``significant'' means that the 90 percent interval excludes zero. Prices barely move, and industrial production rises.
\item Adding the FCI makes the unemployment response standard, large and significant. But prices fall only a little, and industrial production does not fall.
\item Measuring rates in real terms changes little.
\item Measuring the two-year yield and the FCI relative to $\rs$ completes the picture. Unemployment rises 0.36 to 0.40 percentage point after 12 months. The core price level falls 0.53 to 0.72 percent after 24 months. Industrial production falls about 1 percent after 12 months. All are significant for every number of lags.
\end{itemize}
Compared with the benchmark of \citet{bauer2023}, our price effect is similar for two years and larger after that. Our effect on industrial production is somewhat smaller. Our effect on unemployment is about twice as large after one year and lasts longer.

The robustness checks show what drives these results. Financial conditions are essential for unemployment. Without the FCI, no adjustment of the two-year yield gives a reliable unemployment response. With the FCI, every adjustment does. Measuring conditions relative to $\rs$ is essential for prices. The price effect is strong whenever the FCI is measured relative to the Laubach--Williams $\rs$, however the two-year yield is adjusted. The unemployment response holds up however we treat the pandemic, and it is larger in a sample that ends in 2019. The significant price effect, by contrast, depends mainly on data since 2020. The results are the same or slightly stronger with the cleaned surprises of \citet{bauer2023} and the pure-policy surprises of \citet{jarocinski2020}. They also hold with a fuller method for computing uncertainty.

\subsection{Connection to the literature}
Our paper is closest in spirit to \citet{jacobson2026}. Like them, we hold the surprise and the method fixed and change one measurement. They change how often the outcome is measured. We change how the channel from policy to the economy is measured. They study inflation only; they do not estimate effects on activity, which is where our main result lies. Both changes remove wrong-signed responses, and both point to a mismatch between what the economist measures and what drives decisions: daily rather than monthly information in their case, financial conditions relative to $\rs$ rather than a single nominal rate in ours. Our model also uses the timing of announcements within the month, which is central to their argument. Related work on timing includes \citet{kilian2024}, \citet{chudik2022}, \citet{francis2011}, \citet{ghysels2016} and \citet{gazzani2020}.

\citet{bauer2023} and \citet{jarocinski2020} improve the surprise itself. In our design, a surprise enters only through its immediate effect on the two-year yield and the FCI, and through its timing. Using their cleaned surprises in our model changes the results little and makes them slightly stronger. So their fixes and ours work in the same direction and can be combined. Our approach also changes which assumptions the estimates rest on. It depends less on how strongly the surprise moves rates over a month, and less on its link to earlier news. But it relies on a different assumption: the economy responds to a given change in financial conditions in the same way, whatever caused the change. \citet{gertler2015} and \citet{caldara2019} are closest in emphasizing financial channels; we use a broad index and measure it relative to $\rs$, but we leave out credit spreads. The use of futures-based surprises in monthly models goes back to \citet{faust2004}, and the choice of surprise series can matter for the results \citep{bu2021,brennan2025}. \citet{caballero2026} make a related point about the neutral level: because $\rs$ must absorb asset-price movements that a financial conditions index already captures, they propose a neutral level of the index itself, FCI$^*$, estimated in a Laubach--Williams-type model for 1990--2024 with FCI-G. We keep $\rs$ for the rates inside the two-year yield and the index and do not estimate FCI$^*$; using gaps to FCI$^*$ instead is a natural extension. To our knowledge, no earlier study traces the effects of these surprises through a broad financial conditions index measured relative to $\rs$.

\section{Background: how policy reaches the economy}\label{sec:background}
Before turning to the data, we set out the reasoning behind this design. We first describe how monetary policy is thought to reach spending. We then show why a standard VAR can miss this route, and why that could produce the puzzles. Finally, we connect the argument to work on financial conditions and economic activity.

\subsection{The standard transmission mechanism}
Monetary policy sets the short-term interest rate today and, through its communications, shapes what people expect that rate to be in the future. But few households and businesses borrow at the policy rate. What they face are the rates on consumer credit, mortgages and business debt. Arbitrage links these rates to current and expected short-term rates. So when the expected path of policy rises, the rates that households and businesses pay rise too, and spending falls.

The same rise in interest rates also raises the return that investors require on stocks. For businesses, the costs of debt and equity together make up the cost of capital. A higher cost of capital raises the user cost of new equipment and buildings, and investment falls \citep{jorgenson1963,hall1967}. The value of existing capital relative to its replacement cost falls as well \citep{tobin1969}.

For households, a higher required return lowers stock prices and so lowers wealth. Consumption depends on wealth as well as on current income \citep{ando1963,friedman1957}, so spending falls. Higher interest rates pass through to the value of other assets, such as houses, in a similar way.

Higher interest rates also strengthen the dollar. A stronger dollar shifts spending away from goods produced at home and toward imports, and it reduces exports \citep{fleming1962,mundell1963}.

Interest rates of many maturities, equity prices and the dollar therefore form the core of the transmission mechanism \citep{boivin2010}. These links run from the early work cited above to modern models of the business cycle, in closed and open economies \citep{christiano2005,smets2007,gali2005}. Models with heterogeneous households change the relative importance of the links, giving more weight to the indirect effects that work through income \citep{kaplan2018,auclert2019}. But in all of these models the short-term rate matters mainly because of what it does to the prices and rates that people actually face.

What matters for spending in these models is not the nominal rate, but the real rate relative to the neutral rate $\rs$. The same holds for the other parts of financial conditions. A given yield or stock valuation is tighter when $\rs$ is low than when it is high.

\subsection{What standard VARs leave out}
The workhorse evidence on monetary policy comes from VARs with a few macroeconomic variables and one interest rate \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. Some studies add a credit spread \citep{gertler2015,caldara2019}, and factor models bring in many more series \citep{bernanke2005favar}. But long-term yields, equity valuations and the dollar are usually absent from the small models used to measure the effects of policy. These are the variables that the mechanism above says carry those effects. Concern about missing variables is not new: \citet{sims1992} argued that the price puzzle arises because the Federal Reserve responds to information that small VARs do not contain. Our concern is different. Here the missing variables are the channel itself.

A simple model shows what this omission can do. Let $U_t$ be the unemployment gap, $i_t$ the policy rate and $f_t$ financial conditions, with a higher $f_t$ meaning tighter conditions:
\begin{align}
U_t &= \rho_U\,U_{t-1} + \kappa\,f_{t-1} + \varepsilon^{U}_t, \label{eq:y}\\
f_t &= \rho_f\,f_{t-1} + \lambda\,i_t + \varepsilon^{f}_t. \label{eq:f}
\end{align}
Unemployment responds to financial conditions and not directly to the policy rate. Financial conditions respond to the policy rate and to other forces, $\varepsilon^{f}_t$, such as changes in risk appetite. Suppose the economist observes only $U_t$ and $i_t$. To remove $f_t$, lag equation~\eqref{eq:y} one month, multiply it by $\rho_f$, subtract it from equation~\eqref{eq:y}, and use equation~\eqref{eq:f}. This gives
\begin{equation}
U_t = (\rho_U+\rho_f)\,U_{t-1} - \rho_U\rho_f\,U_{t-2} + \kappa\lambda\,i_{t-1} + \underbrace{\varepsilon^{U}_t - \rho_f\,\varepsilon^{U}_{t-1} + \kappa\,\varepsilon^{f}_{t-1}}_{\text{error}}. \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
The policy rate has no direct effect in this model. It appears in equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} because it stands in for financial conditions. The error is a moving average that includes past financial shocks, so a VAR in $U_t$ and $i_t$ with few lags cannot capture it. The VAR is misspecified. With the lags and samples available in practice, this can make the effects of monetary policy harder to measure. In some settings a policy shock may not be recoverable at all from a small set of variables \citep{fernandez2007}, but our argument does not depend on that.

There is a second problem, and it matters more for the size and sign of the response: the omission changes the experiment being traced. In the announcement windows, the financial conditions index used below rises about 1 unit per percentage point rise in the two-year yield. In ordinary months, its unexpected movements are only about 0.35 to 0.4 unit per percentage point. A VAR without financial conditions traces the typical move in the two-year yield, which comes with a much smaller change in financial conditions than a policy surprise does.

A small simulation shows the size of the problem. The true model is a monthly VAR in the unemployment gap, core PCE inflation, the two-year yield and the financial conditions index, with six lags and a quadratic trend. Macroeconomic variables are ordered first. We estimate it on the sample described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, which also defines the index and its nominal and $\rs$-gap versions. We generate 2,000 artificial samples of the same length (382 months) from this model. On each sample, we estimate the VAR with all four variables and again without the index. We then trace a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point, with no immediate effect on unemployment or inflation. With the index in the VAR, the move also raises the index by 0.99 unit, the amount measured in the announcement windows. Without it, the same move raises only the yield. Table~\ref{tab:sim} shows the results. These VARs are monthly and simpler than the model of Section~\ref{sec:data}, so their true responses are not the estimates reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The VAR with the index comes close to the true responses. The VAR without it does not. In the nominal case, the true unemployment response after 12 months is $+0.36$. The VAR without the index gives $-0.08$ on average, and the estimate has the wrong sign in two-thirds of the samples. In the $\rs$-gap case, it gives $+0.11$ against a true response of $+0.51$. The price effects are also understated, though less severely. More data would reduce the noise but not this error: with ten times as many months, the nominal estimate is still $-0.08$. The simulation illustrates the mechanism and does not prove it, because the true model is one in which financial conditions matter by construction. But it shows that if they do matter, a small VAR that omits them can produce the wrong-signed responses found in practice.

High-frequency surprises help identify the shock, but they do not by themselves fix how it is carried forward. Our design therefore does two things. It includes financial conditions in the system, so that they carry the effects of the surprise forward, as in equation~\eqref{eq:y}. And it measures the immediate effect of the surprise on the two-year yield and on financial conditions directly in the announcement window, where policy news is the main force at work. Finally, the measurement of $f_t$ matters. If activity responds to financial conditions relative to $\rs$, a measure that ignores $\rs$ differs from the true $f_t$ by a slow-moving error, and that error enters equation~\eqref{eq:y}.

\subsection{Financial conditions and real activity}
A separate literature asks how financial conditions are related to economic activity. Financial conditions predict activity, though unstably: asset prices help forecast output and inflation, but which ones help changes over time \citep{stock2003}. Credit spreads, and especially their excess bond premium component, are strong predictors of activity \citep{gilchrist2012}. Broad indexes summarize many such series and are used to assess the outlook \citep{hatzius2010,brave2011}. Financial conditions are also linked to the risks around growth, not only to its average \citep{adrian2019}, and their relationship with unemployment may be nonlinear \citep{kiley2020ml}. Federal Reserve staff now publish a measure of the effect of financial conditions on growth, with weights from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}.

This work has a limit that the event-study literature does not. A correlation between financial conditions and later activity need not reflect a causal effect, because financial conditions also respond to the economy. \citet{hatzius2010} address part of this problem by removing the effects of past growth and inflation from their indicators. The companion note uses the timing in a VAR for the same purpose, and finds that its index helps predict unemployment within the sample, but that out-of-sample gains are modest \citep{fcinote}.

The two literatures have complementary strengths. Work on high-frequency surprises identifies policy shocks cleanly but carries them through a small set of variables. Work on financial conditions studies a broad set of variables but does not isolate policy shocks. We bring them together. The weights in our index come from how its components respond to policy news, and the surprise is carried forward through the estimated relationship between that index and the economy.

\section{Empirical approach}\label{sec:data}
The model has three pieces. A surprise moves the two-year yield and a financial conditions index (FCI) by amounts measured in the announcement window: the vector $b$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. A daily VAR, equation~\eqref{eq:daily}, carries those two series forward. Their monthly averages then enter a monthly VAR for unemployment and inflation, equation~\eqref{eq:monthly}. Section~\ref{sec:measure} defines the FCI, Section~\ref{sec:timing} sets out the timing assumptions at each frequency, and Section~\ref{sec:midas} gives the model and how we use it. Appendix~\ref{app:data} lists the data.

\subsection{Measuring financial conditions}\label{sec:measure}
The FCI is the market index of \citet{fcinote}: a weighted sum of the 10-year Treasury yield, stock valuations and the real broad dollar. Indexes of this kind are weighted sums of financial variables \citep{hatzius2010}. They differ mainly in where the weights come from. FCI-G takes them from large macroeconomic models \citep{ajello2023}. Ours come from the event-study regressions long used to measure how asset prices respond to policy \citep{kuttner2001,bernanke2005,hanson2015,nakamura2018}.

\textbf{Policy news.} Let $\Delta X_a$ be the $5\times 1$ vector of changes in the current- and next-meeting federal funds futures and the second to fourth eurodollar futures in the window around announcement $a$. Following \citet{nakamura2018}, let $p_a$ be the first principal component of these changes, signed so that it moves with the fourth eurodollar future. Policy news is $p_a$ rescaled so that one unit moves the two-year yield $y^{2}$ by 1 percentage point:
\begin{equation}
s_a = \gamma\, p_a, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\sum_a p_a\, \Delta y^{2}_a}{\sum_a p_a^{2}}. \label{eq:ns}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Weights.} Let $\Delta A_a$ be the vector of changes in the 10-year yield, the S\&P 500 and the DXY dollar index in the announcement window. The companion note treats these changes as
\begin{equation}
\Delta A_a = \psi\, s_a + \eta_a, \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ holds the policy responses and $\eta_a$ is other news, with covariance matrix $\Sigma$. It estimates $\psi$ by regressing each element of $\Delta A_a$ on $s_a$ and a constant, and $\Sigma$ from the residuals, over 253 announcements from January 1996 to September 2026. The weights are those that recover the policy component most precisely (one-factor generalized least squares):
\begin{equation}
w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\psi}{\psi'\Sigma^{-1}\psi}. \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Because $w'\psi=1$ and $s_a$ is scaled to the two-year yield, a policy move that raises the two-year yield 1 percentage point raises the index by 1 unit. The weights are 0.61 on the 10-year yield, $-0.051$ per percent on stock valuations and 0.065 per percent on the dollar. Each of the three accounts for about a third of the index's response to policy.

\textbf{Three versions.} In month $t$, the index in version $v$ is
\begin{equation}
F_t^{v} = w_B\, B_t^{v} + w_Q\, Q_t^{v} + w_D\, D_t, \label{eq:fci}
\end{equation}
measured as a deviation from its sample mean. Here $D_t$ is 100 times the log of the real broad dollar in every version. With $y^{10}_t$ the 10-year yield, $\pi^{10}_t$ 10-year expected inflation, and $(E/P)_t$ the inverse of the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE), the other two terms are:
\begin{align}
\text{Nominal:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:nom}\\
\text{Real:}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t, & Q_t &= 100\ln \text{CAPE}_t, \label{eq:real}\\
\text{$\rs$-gap (baseline):}\quad & B_t = y^{10}_t - \pi^{10}_t - \rs_t, & Q_t &= -\frac{100}{\overline{E/P}}\left[(E/P)_t - \rs_t\right]. \label{eq:rstar}
\end{align}
In the $\rs$-gap version, stocks are measured by the gap between their earnings yield and $\rs$. The term $100/\overline{E/P}$, with $\overline{E/P}$ the sample average, converts this gap to the units of the log CAPE, so that the weight on stocks is the same in every version. CAPE is a real valuation measure, so stocks do not change between the nominal and real versions. For $\rs$ we use the New York Fed's current Laubach--Williams estimates \citep{holston2023}. These ``one-sided'' estimates use only data up to each date, with the model's parameters estimated on the full sample.

\textbf{The two-year yield.} The two-year yield is adjusted in the same steps. With $y^{2}_t$ the nominal yield and $\pi^{2}_t$ two-year expected inflation \citep[from the Cleveland Fed;][]{haubrich2012},
\begin{equation}
R_t^{\text{nominal}} = y^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\text{real}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t, \qquad R_t^{\rs\text{-gap}} = y^{2}_t - \pi^{2}_t - \rs_t. \label{eq:twoyear}
\end{equation}

\textbf{From monthly to daily.} Expected inflation and $\rs$ are not available daily, and they move slowly. We take the monthly gap between $R_t^{v}$ and $y^{2}_t$, interpolate it linearly between the middles of adjacent months, and add it to the daily yield. The daily index is
\begin{equation}
F_\tau^{v} = w_B\, y^{10}_\tau + w_Q\, 100\ln\text{CAPE}_\tau + w_D\, 100\ln\text{DOL}_\tau + o_\tau^{v}, \label{eq:dailyfci}
\end{equation}
where $\text{DOL}_\tau$ is the daily nominal broad dollar. The offset $o_\tau^{v}$ is the monthly difference between the index in equation~\eqref{eq:fci} and the average of the first three terms, interpolated in the same way. It also absorbs the difference between the real and the nominal dollar. The monthly averages of the daily series are very close to the monthly measures: the root-mean-square difference is at most 0.02 unit for the FCI and 0.04 percentage point for the two-year yield. The offsets change little from day to day, so day-to-day moves in the index come from market prices. Table~\ref{tab:measures} shows the measures used at each frequency.

\subsection{Timing assumptions and identification}\label{sec:timing}
The shock is the Nakamura--Steinsson policy-news surprise, $s_a$ in equation~\eqref{eq:ns}, from the San Francisco Fed's U.S.\ Monetary Policy Event-Study Database \citep{acosta2025}. We use the 252 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to July 2026 with all the window measures available. As in the companion note, we leave out unscheduled announcements with no change in the policy rate, and March--April 2020. The one later announcement in the database, September 2026, is left out because too few later days are available. The window runs from 10 minutes before the statement to 60 minutes after the press conference starts. When there is no press conference, it is the 30-minute window around the statement.

Identification comes from timing, at three frequencies. Table~\ref{tab:timing} summarizes the assumptions.

\textbf{The window.} The window move in the FCI is computed with the weights in equation~\eqref{eq:weights}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F_a = w_B\, \Delta y^{10}_a + w_Q\, \Delta \text{SP}_a + w_D\, \Delta \text{DXY}_a. \label{eq:winfci}
\end{equation}
These are the measures available within the day. They are also the ones used to estimate the weights, so the window move and the weights are consistent. Percent changes in the S\&P 500 equal percent changes in CAPE within a window, because earnings are fixed. The DXY index covers fewer currencies than the broad dollar used in the daily and monthly index, which we treat as a measurement difference.

We express all effects per percentage point on the two-year yield in the window. The effect on the FCI is the ratio of two regression slopes on the surprise, which is an instrumental-variables estimate with $s_a$ as the instrument:
\begin{equation}
b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ b_F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b_F = \frac{\sum_a (\Delta F_a - \overline{\Delta F})\,(s_a-\bar s)}{\sum_a (\Delta y^{2}_a - \overline{\Delta y^{2}})\,(s_a-\bar s)} = 0.99. \label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
This is close to one because the weights are normalized that way. It is not exactly one because this regression includes a constant, which the scaling in equation~\eqref{eq:ns} does not, and because it uses one fewer announcement. Expected inflation and $\rs$ move slowly, so we take the window move to be the same in every version.

\textbf{The day.} On the announcement day, the two-year yield and the FCI shift by the vector $b$. We use the window move rather than the change over the whole day, because the full-day change contains other news. On later days, the two series move as equation~\eqref{eq:daily} implies. The two series affect each other only through the daily lags, so we need no ordering between them within the day. The window fixes their joint move.

\textbf{The month.} Within each month, unemployment and inflation are set first, and markets then respond to them. So in the month of the announcement, unemployment and inflation have not moved. They respond from the next month on, through the monthly average of the daily financial series. The average depends on when in the month the announcement falls. An announcement late in a month affects few days of that month's average, and an announcement early in the month affects most of them. This is how the model takes the timing of announcements into account, as \citet{jacobson2026} recommend. The ordering is the familiar recursive one, in which slow-moving macroeconomic variables come before financial variables within the period \citep{bernanke1992,christiano1999}. It also means that the daily equations include the current month's unemployment and inflation, even though those figures are published later. We treat this as a modeling convention: the monthly values summarize economic conditions that markets observe as the month unfolds.

\textbf{The key assumptions.} Two assumptions carry the results. The first is the usual one in a VAR. Once the shock has been identified, the economy responds to a change in the financial variables in the same way whatever caused it. A policy shock identified by a Cholesky ordering or an external instrument is traced through the same estimated dynamics as every other shock, and we do the same. It has a familiar cost. Stock prices partly anticipate future activity, so a move that policy news pushes through stocks need not act on the economy like a move with another cause. This is the same problem as the information effect in any VAR with a high-frequency shock, and as in the FCI note we acknowledge it and set it aside.

The second assumption is specific to this note and has two parts. A standard VAR estimates the effect of the shock on every variable in the system. Here, the surprise reaches unemployment and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and has no direct effect on them in the announcement window. Anything else it moves, such as credit spreads or expectations, is left out. This is easier to accept when the financial variables are broad, but it is a restriction. In addition, the monthly equations see the FCI as a single index. Its weights show how the 10-year yield, stock valuations and the dollar each respond to policy news. They do not show how the economy responds to each of them. So we assume that the three affect unemployment and prices in proportion to those weights. We do not split the FCI into its parts, or into policy-driven and other movements, and we do not add credit variables to the baseline. Appendix~\ref{app:assump} relaxes both parts in turn.

\subsection{A mixed-frequency VAR}\label{sec:midas}
\textbf{Structure.} The model has a daily block and a monthly block. Let $x_\tau=(R_\tau,F_\tau)'$ hold the two-year yield and the FCI on day $\tau$, in the same version. Let $m_t=(U_t,\pi_t)'$ hold the two monthly variables. The unemployment gap $U_t$ is the unemployment rate minus the CBO estimate of its natural rate. The CBO estimate does not respond to the surprise, so the response of the gap equals the response of the unemployment rate. Inflation is $\pi_t=1200\,\Delta\ln P_t$, where $P_t$ is the price index for personal consumption expenditures excluding food and energy (core PCE).

The daily block is a VAR in the style of the heterogeneous autoregression of \citet{corsi2009}:
\begin{equation}
x_\tau = \alpha + \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_k\, \bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, m_{t(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, m_{t(\tau)-1} + \xi_\tau, \label{eq:daily}
\end{equation}
where $\bar x_{\tau-1}^{(k)}$ is the average of $x$ over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$, and $t(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$. Averages over about a day, a week, a month and a quarter capture persistence with few coefficients. Each equation has 13 coefficients with the FCI. When the FCI is left out, $x_\tau$ is the two-year yield alone. The calendar has every weekday; missing values, such as on holidays, are filled from the previous observation for up to three days.

The monthly block is
\begin{equation}
m_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, m_{t-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \bar x_{t-l} + \theta_1\, t + \theta_2\, t^2 + \zeta_t, \qquad \bar x_{t}=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{\tau\in t} x_\tau, \label{eq:monthly}
\end{equation}
with $n_t$ the number of weekdays in month $t$. Each equation has $3+4L$ coefficients with the FCI (a constant, the two trend terms, and $2L$ each for the macroeconomic and financial lags). The model is a mixed-frequency VAR \citep{ghysels2016}. In terms of the mixed-data sampling literature, the weights are equal across days within a month and unrestricted across months \citep{ghysels2007,foroni2015}. The equation contains no current-month term in $\bar x_t$, which is the timing assumption of Table~\ref{tab:timing}.

\textbf{Relation to a monthly VAR.} Suppose the daily block were replaced by a monthly equation for $\bar x_t$, and every announcement occurred on the first day of the month. With $Z_t=(m_t',\bar x_t')'$, the model would then be a standard monthly VAR with a recursive ordering, the macroeconomic variables first, and impact vector $(0,0,b')'$. The daily block adds two things to that benchmark: the position of each announcement in its month, and fast dynamics of the financial variables, estimated on many more observations. Section~\ref{sec:rob} estimates this benchmark directly.

This benchmark connects our design to the proxy VAR \citep{gertler2015,mertens2013,stock2018}. A proxy VAR estimates $Z_t=\sum_l C_l Z_{t-l}+\iota_t$, where $\iota_t$ are the monthly residuals, and uses as an instrument $z_t$ the surprises summed over the month. The effect on the FCI per unit effect on the two-year yield is then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{F,t},z_t)}{\operatorname{Cov}(\iota_{R,t},z_t)}. \label{eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
The window ratio $b_F$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact} has the same form, with three differences. It replaces monthly VAR residuals with changes measured inside the window, which do not depend on how strongly the surprise predicts monthly residuals. It sets the entries for unemployment and inflation to zero rather than estimating them. And it dates each shock to its day rather than summing shocks over a month.

\textbf{The time trend.} Every monthly equation includes a smooth quadratic time trend. Over 1994--2026, unemployment, inflation and interest rates contain slow movements that a short monthly model cannot capture. Rates drifted down along with $\rs$. Inflation was low in the 2010s and rose after 2020. The natural rate of unemployment may have shifted in ways the CBO estimate misses. Without a trend, these slow movements show up in the estimated effect of the financial variables, and trend gets mixed with transmission. A quadratic is the simplest smooth trend that allows inflation to fall and then rise. Because the trend is an assumption, Section~\ref{sec:rob} reports the main steps without it.

\textbf{Estimation.} We estimate each block by ordinary least squares. The daily block uses January 1990 to September 2026, including 2020. The monthly block uses February 1994 to September 2026, leaving out March--December 2020, which leaves 382 months. We use $L=$ 3, 6 or 12 monthly lags.

\textbf{Tracing the effect of a surprise.} The response is computed recursively, as a deviation from a path with no surprise. Let the announcement occur on weekday $d_0$ of the announcement month, and let $h=0,1,\ldots,36$ count months from then. Tildes denote responses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item In each month, unemployment and inflation come first: $\tilde m_0=0$, and for $h\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde m_h = \sum_{l=1}^{L} A_l\, \tilde m_{h-l} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} G_l\, \tilde x^{M}_{h-l}, \label{eq:irfm}
\end{equation}
with terms dated before the announcement month equal to zero, and $\tilde x^{M}_{h}$ the response of the monthly average of $x$.
\item The days of the month follow. Before day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=0$. On day $d_0$, $\tilde x_\tau=b$ from equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. On every later day, with $\tilde x^{(k)}_{\tau-1}$ the average of the responses over the $k$ days through $\tau-1$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_\tau = \sum_{k\in\{1,5,22,66\}} \Phi_{k}\, \tilde x_{\tau-1}^{(k)} + \Gamma_0\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)} + \Gamma_1\, \tilde m_{h(\tau)-1}, \label{eq:irfd}
\end{equation}
where $h(\tau)$ is the month of day $\tau$, counted from the announcement month.
\item The monthly average $\tilde x^{M}_h$ then enters equation~\eqref{eq:irfm} for later months.
\end{enumerate}
Months after the announcement month have 21 weekdays. To avoid computing a path for every date, we form six groups of announcement dates by the weekday of the month, in blocks of four days, and compute the path at each group's average day and month length. With $\omega_g$ the share of announcements in group $g$, the reported response is $\sum_g \omega_g\, \tilde m_h^{(g)}$. The response of the core price level after $h$ months, in percent, is $\sum_{j=0}^{h}\tilde\pi_j/12$. Because the model is linear, these responses do not depend on the constant or the trend.

\textbf{Relation to other methods.} In large samples, a VAR of this kind estimates the same effects as local projections, which regress each outcome directly on the shock at each future month \citep{plagborg2021}. Section~\ref{sec:rob} tests the equal weighting of days and reports a direct regression of unemployment on the daily surprises.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} To measure uncertainty, we use a bootstrap with 200 draws, with stars denoting a draw. In each draw, we resample the 252 announcements with replacement, which gives new values $b^{*}$ in equation~\eqref{eq:impact} and new group shares $\omega_g^{*}$. We also resample the residuals $\hat\zeta_t$ of equation~\eqref{eq:monthly} jointly across equations, add them to the fitted values, holding the regressors fixed, and re-estimate the monthly block. The daily block is held at its estimate. We then compute the response for each draw and report percentile intervals. Section~\ref{sec:rob} reports a fuller version that also re-estimates the daily model and rebuilds the monthly data from the model. The FCI weights and $\rs$ are held fixed throughout. We report 90 percent intervals, as recent studies in this area do \citep{bauer2023,caldara2019,miranda2021,ramey2016}. We also report 68 percent intervals, which \citet{jarocinski2020} report alongside 90 percent ones.

\textbf{Step 1: the standard approach reproduces the puzzles.} Here the nominal two-year yield is the only channel. This is the policy measure that \citet{bauer2023} use. The unemployment response depends on the number of lags. With 12 lags, it is significantly negative ($-0.27$ after 12 months), so unemployment falls after a tightening. The price level barely moves. This is the post-1994 pattern documented by \citet{ramey2016}, and the one \citet{bauer2023} find with their unadjusted surprises. Industrial production rises significantly (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ip}).

\textbf{Step 2: add financial conditions.} Adding the FCI makes the unemployment response standard, large and significant at the 90 percent level at 12, 24 and 36 months and for every number of lags. Unemployment rises 0.28 to 0.44 percentage point after 12 months and 0.6 to 0.8 after 24 to 36 months. The price level falls 0.2 to 0.5 percent after 24 to 36 months. This fall is significant only at the 68 percent level, and only with 6 and 12 lags. Industrial production does not fall.

\textbf{Step 3: real rates.} Measuring the two-year yield and the FCI net of expected inflation changes little. The companion note finds the same: after 1990, its nominal and real indexes are close. The price fall becomes significant at the 68 percent level at 12, 24 and 36 months and for every number of lags.

\textbf{Step 4: rates relative to $\rs$.} Next we measure the two-year yield and the FCI relative to $\rs$. Unemployment rises 0.36 to 0.40 percentage point after 12 months, 0.47 to 0.50 after 24 months and 0.35 to 0.43 after 36 months. These numbers are nearly the same for every lag length. The core price level falls 0.33 to 0.34 percent after 12 months, 0.53 to 0.72 percent after 24 months and 0.57 to 0.97 percent after 36 months. All of these are significant at the 90 percent level for every lag length. The unemployment response is smaller and fades sooner than with the nominal FCI. Without the FCI, the $\rs$-gap two-year yield gives a strong price effect. But the unemployment effect is then weak and depends on the number of lags (it is significant only with 3 lags).

\textbf{Comparison with \citet{bauer2023}.} Their benchmark model covers 1973--2020. It uses surprises from FOMC announcements and Chair speeches, with the predictable part removed. Per percentage point on the two-year yield, it gives an unemployment response of about 0.19 percentage point after 12 months, 0.17 after 24 months and 0.15 after 36 months. It gives a CPI decline of about 0.36, 0.48 and 0.6 percent. Our step-4 price effects (for core PCE) are similar after 12 and 24 months and larger after 36 months ($-0.57$ to $-0.97$). Our unemployment effect is about twice as large after 12 months and 2.5 to 3 times as large after 24 to 36 months. Our intervals exclude zero through 36 months. Theirs include zero after about a year and a half. Their estimates use surprises from 1988 to 2019, Chair speeches and removal of the predictable part. Ours use FOMC announcement windows alone, with no such adjustment.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:rob}
\textbf{How should the two-year yield be adjusted?} Table~\ref{tab:rob} and Table~\ref{tab:robp} keep the FCI as one of the companion note's three indexes. The $\rs$-gap FCI always uses the Laubach--Williams $\rs$. The tables change only how the two-year yield is adjusted:
\begin{itemize}
\item no adjustment (nominal);
\item minus expected inflation and the Laubach--Williams $\rs$ (baseline);
\item minus expected inflation and \citet{kiley2020}'s estimate of $\rs$;
\item minus a market-based measure of the neutral rate: the one-year rate nine years ahead (the 9--10-year forward rate), from the Federal Reserve Board's estimated yield curve \citep{gurkaynak2007}. This rate is nominal, so it replaces both the expected-inflation and $\rs$ adjustments;
\item minus the average of the two-year yield over the past 8 years.
\end{itemize}
Three results stand out.

First, financial conditions are essential for unemployment. Without the FCI, the unemployment response is weak with a model-based $\rs$. It is significantly negative with the nominal rate, the forward rate or the 8-year average. With any of the FCIs, it is positive at every horizon and significant at the 90 percent level in almost all cases.

Second, the price effect is strong whenever the FCI is measured relative to $\rs$. With the $\rs$-gap FCI, the price level falls 0.3 to 0.7 percent after 24 months. This holds for every adjustment of the two-year yield, and even with no adjustment. This fall is significant at the 90 percent level for every lag length. With the nominal or real FCI, the price effect is strong when the two-year yield is adjusted with a model-based $\rs$. It is weaker with the forward rate or the 8-year average. The forward rate also includes a term premium (the extra yield investors require to hold long-term bonds), which fell over the sample.

Third, Kiley's $\rs$ gives results close to those with Laubach--Williams, though somewhat weaker for prices. The $\rs$-gap FCI always uses the Laubach--Williams $\rs$. So these results show that how the two-year yield is adjusted matters less once the FCI is measured relative to $\rs$. They do not show that the choice of $\rs$ in the FCI is unimportant.

After 12 months, it is 0.26 to 0.31 percentage point when 2021 is dropped, 0.32 to 0.34 when the sample ends in 2022, and 0.42 to 0.44 when it ends in 2019. \textbf{Industrial production.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ip} adds industrial production to the monthly model. Without the FCI, industrial production rises after a tightening when the two-year yield is nominal or real. It does not move significantly when the two-year yield is measured relative to $\rs$. With the nominal or real FCI, it does not fall significantly. Only at the $\rs$ step does it fall: by 0.84 to 1.05 percent after 12 months, significant at the 90 percent level for every lag length. \citet{bauer2023} find about 1.5 percent. In our model, industrial production recovers within about three years. In earlier exploratory work, we also added a commodity price index. Commodity prices then rose after a tightening. Our assumptions are least plausible for prices like these, which look ahead and respond strongly to demand.

\textbf{Other surprise measures.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:shocks} replaces the Nakamura--Steinsson surprise with three others and keeps everything else the same:
\begin{itemize}
\item the surprise with the part predicted by the Fed staff's forecasts for each FOMC meeting (the Greenbook) removed;
\item the surprise of \citet{bauer2023}, with the part predicted by public data removed (their posted series, which covers 201 of our announcements through 2019);
\item the ``pure policy'' surprise of \citet{jarocinski2020}, which keeps only announcements where the two-year yield and the S\&P 500 move in opposite directions (157 announcements).
\end{itemize}
The choice of surprise series can matter for estimated effects \citep{bu2021,brennan2025}. In our design, a surprise enters only through its window moves, scaled to a 1 percentage point rise in the two-year yield, and through the timing of announcements. So this check really tests how much the FCI moves per point on the two-year yield. That ratio is 0.99 for the baseline, 1.07 for the Bauer--Swanson surprise and 1.09 for the Jaroci\'nski--Karadi surprise. The last ratio is higher partly by design: that surprise keeps announcements where stocks fall when yields rise, and stocks enter the FCI with a negative sign.

With these ratios, the effects are slightly larger than in the baseline. At the $\rs$ step, unemployment rises 0.40 to 0.45 percentage point after 12 months, and the price level falls 0.55 to 0.77 percent after 24 months. The Greenbook-cleaned surprise gives results within 0.02 of the baseline. Without the FCI, all the surprises give almost identical effects. So our results do not depend on how the surprise is cleaned. Cleaning matters here only through the ratio of the FCI move to the two-year-yield move in the window.

\textbf{A fuller measure of uncertainty.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:boot} uses 1,000 draws instead of 200. In each draw, besides resampling the announcements, it re-estimates the daily model on randomly redrawn blocks of 66 consecutive days. It also rebuilds the monthly data from the estimated model, randomly flipping the signs of the errors, and re-estimates the monthly equations. The intervals barely change. At the $\rs$ step, every $^{**}$ and $^{\dagger}$ marker is the same. Elsewhere, a few markers move between the 68 and 90 percent levels. The FCI weights and $\rs$ are still held fixed.

\textbf{Relaxing the key assumptions.} Appendix~\ref{app:assump} tests the two restrictions specific to this note and adds a monthly credit measure. First, we add the daily gap between the Baa corporate yield and the 10-year Treasury yield, and let it respond on impact to the surprise. The results barely change: at the $\rs$ step, unemployment still rises 0.28 to 0.35 percentage point after 12 months and the price level still falls 0.48 to 0.65 percent after 24 months, significant for all lag lengths. Second, we replace the FCI with its three parts, entered separately. The signs are unchanged, but the estimates are less precise, and the price result is significant at the 90 percent level only with 12 lags. A test of the proportional-weights restriction does not reject it at the 5 percent level for unemployment at any step, or for either variable at the $\rs$ step. Third, adding the monthly excess bond premium as a third monthly variable removes the wrong-signed unemployment response without the FCI, but leaves the results with the FCI almost unchanged.

\textbf{Longer sample for the monthly equations.} The baseline fits the monthly equations on 1994--2026, close to the years of the surprises (1996--2026). Some studies instead estimate the dynamics on a longer sample and apply the surprises only to the recent period. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:longu} and~\ref{tab:longp} do this. The surprises, the announcement windows and the daily model are unchanged. Only the months used to fit the unemployment and inflation equations start earlier, in 1973, 1977 or 1982. Before the daily data exist, the model uses monthly averages instead. Three splices are needed and are described in the table notes. We also run the 1973 start without the time trend.

Longer samples change the picture in four ways. First, the unemployment puzzle depends on the 1994 start. With the nominal two-year yield alone, the 1994 estimates are significantly wrong-signed at 12 months with 12 lags ($-0.27$), and the sign changes with the lag length. With the 1973 start, no point estimate is negative. But the responses are small ($+0.02$ to $+0.09$ at 12 months, $+0.10$ to $+0.16$ at 24 months) and mostly not significant. Second, the FCI still matters for unemployment in the long sample. The response has the standard sign in every sample, at every horizon and lag length, and it is significant at 12 months with 3, 6 and 12 lags. But the long-sample effects are smaller. At the $\rs$ step the response is 0.11 to 0.27 percentage point after 12 months with a 1973 start, 0.19 to 0.28 with a 1977 start and 0.21 to 0.29 with a 1982 start, against 0.36 to 0.40 in the baseline. At 36 months the 1973 effects are 0.02 to 0.08, against 0.35 to 0.43. Third, a price puzzle appears with the nominal yield in the 1973 sample. The price level rises, by 0.15 percent at 24 months with 3 lags, though not significantly. The baseline shows only a non-significant fall. Adjusting for $\rs$ removes the puzzle in both samples. At the $\rs$ step, with and without the FCI, the price level falls significantly at 24 months with 6 and 12 lags in every long sample. With the FCI the fall is 0.31 to 0.54 percent, against 0.53 to 0.72 in the baseline. In the 1973 and 1977 samples the real step also gives significant price falls, which the baseline does not. Fourth, the signs agree across starts whenever the FCI is included or the yield is adjusted for $\rs$, and the 1994 effects are larger by a factor of about 1.5 to 4, depending on the horizon. Industrial production falls in the 1973 and 1977 samples when the FCI is included (about 0.4 to 0.9 percent after 12 months). It does not fall significantly in the 1982 sample.

\textbf{A purely monthly VAR.} How much of our result comes from the mixed-frequency model, and how much from measuring the impact in the window? To find out, we estimate a monthly VAR on the same sample, with the same quadratic trend and lags: $Z_t=(m_t',\bar x_t')'$ in the notation of Section~\ref{sec:midas}, with and without the FCI, at each step. We then impose four sets of restrictions on the immediate effect of the surprise on $Z_t$, written $S$. The surprise summed over the month is the instrument $z_t$, which is zero in months without an announcement.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Standard proxy VAR.} Every element $S_i$ is estimated as in equation~\eqref{eq:proxy}, with variable $i$ in the numerator.
\item \emph{Proxy with timing restrictions.} The same, but the elements for unemployment and inflation are set to zero.
\item \emph{Window impact, announcement timing.} $S=\phi\,(0,0,b')'$, with $b$ from equation~\eqref{eq:impact}. Here $\phi=0.46$ is the average share of the month that follows the announcement, because a surprise moves only part of that month's average.
\item \emph{Window impact, first day of the month.} $S=(0,0,b')'$. This treats every surprise as if it occurred on the first day of the month, and is an upper bracket.
\end{itemize}
For the proxy cases, we report 90 percent Anderson--Rubin sets, which remain valid when the instrument is weak; they are conditional on the VAR coefficients. For the window cases, we use the bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:midas}. The proxy cases are per percentage point on the monthly average of the two-year yield, as is the first-day case. The announcement-timing case is per percentage point in the window, as in the baseline. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:monthly} gives the results.

Two findings stand out. First, the monthly proxy is weak. The first-stage $F$ statistic is 4.9 to 6.3 without the FCI and 1.7 to 6.4 with it, well below the usual threshold of 10, and it is 5.3 to 8.3 when the funds rate and the current-meeting surprise replace the two-year yield and the policy-news surprise. Many of the sets are wide or unbounded. The proxy also implies a much smaller response of the FCI, 0.13 to 0.62 unit per percentage point on the two-year yield, than the 0.99 measured in the windows. With this estimated impact, adding the FCI does not produce a standard unemployment response. At the $\rs$ step, the 12-month response is between $-0.11$ and $+0.02$. With the funds rate and the current-meeting surprise, it is negative, per percentage point on the funds rate, and significantly so at the nominal step, though the sets include zero at the $\rs$ step. So adding the FCI to a standard monthly proxy VAR is not enough.

Second, a monthly VAR with the impact measured in the window gives results close to ours. With the $\rs$-gap two-year yield and FCI, unemployment rises 0.21 to 0.23 percentage point after 12 months with announcement timing, and 0.47 to 0.51 with first-day timing. Our baseline of 0.36 to 0.40 lies between these. The price level falls 0.29 to 0.39 percent after 24 months with announcement timing and 0.62 to 0.84 with first-day timing, again bracketing the baseline of 0.53 to 0.72. All of these are significant at the 90 percent level. Without the FCI, the unemployment response is small or has the wrong sign, whichever timing we use, and it is significant at the 90 percent level at the $\rs$ step only with 3 lags. The result therefore depends on two things together: including the FCI, and measuring its response to the surprise in the window. The daily block appears mainly to refine the timing.

\textbf{The time trend.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:notrend} shows the main steps without the trend. The nominal and real steps depend much more on the trend than the $\rs$ step does. This suggests that measuring rates relative to $\rs$ does much of the work of the trend.

\textbf{Daily weights and a direct regression.} To test equal weighting of days, we replace $\bar x_t$ in equation~\eqref{eq:monthly} with a weighted average whose weight on the $d$-th weekday of a month is proportional to $1+\delta\,(2d-n_t-1)/(n_t-1)$, choose $\delta$ on a grid from $-1$ to 1, and test $\delta=0$ with an $F$ test. This check uses 12 lags, the nominal two-year yield, the $\rs$-gap FCI and no trend. The test does not reject equal weights for unemployment ($F=2.1$). It does reject them for inflation ($F=4.6$, against a 5 percent critical value of 3.9), but the gain in fit is very small: the $R^2$ rises from 0.435 to 0.443.

We also tried a method that does not need the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:timing}: a direct regression of monthly unemployment on the daily surprises \citep{chudik2022,chudik2026}. With $s_a$ the surprise on day $\tau_a$, it estimates a daily response $\beta_\ell$ from
\begin{equation}
U_t = \frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{\tau\in t}\ \sum_{a:\,\tau_a\le\tau}\beta_{\tau-\tau_a}\, s_a + \text{error}, \label{eq:mfdl}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_\ell$ piecewise linear in $\ell$ and knots at 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months. It gives wrong-signed estimates, driven largely by the 2007--09 announcements. Its intervals are also more than ten times as wide as ours, so it cannot tell our estimates apart from zero.

\textbf{Other checks.} In earlier versions of the model, we added the gap between Baa corporate and Treasury yields to the daily model without any response on impact, because no intraday credit data exist. This lowered the unemployment response by a third to a half. Appendix~\ref{app:assump} instead lets the spread respond on the announcement day, and also adds the monthly excess bond premium. The results then change little when the FCI is included. Credit is still the most important channel we leave out of the baseline \citep{gilchrist2012,caldara2019}. We also tried replacing the daily model's path for the financial variables in the first one to two months with the path estimated directly from the data. That gives larger but much less precise effects.

\section{Conclusion}
Interest-rate surprises around Federal Reserve announcements measure policy shocks well. The hard part has been tracing their effects on the economy. We keep the surprise as it is and change only how its effects are traced. Two standard parts of the transmission mechanism explain much of the post-1994 puzzle. Policy works through financial conditions as a whole. And what matters for spending and inflation is the level of rates and financial conditions relative to a slow-moving neutral rate. When we trace surprises through a broad financial conditions index measured relative to $\rs$, the effects on unemployment, prices and industrial production have the standard signs. They are precisely estimated, the unemployment effects barely depend on the number of lags, and they are similar in size to the estimates of \citet{bauer2023}.

The two parts play different roles. Financial conditions are essential for the unemployment effect, in every version and sample we try. Measuring financial conditions relative to $\rs$ is essential for the price effect. When the FCI is measured relative to the Laubach--Williams $\rs$, the price effect is strong however the two-year yield is adjusted. The unemployment effect holds up however we treat the pandemic. The price effect depends mainly on the years since 2020, when the stance of policy relative to $\rs$ moved most.

These findings complement the work on information effects and predictable surprises. Several mechanisms can operate at once. A natural next step is to trace cleaned or separated surprises through our approach. Our results have limits. They rest on the two key assumptions: policy reaches the economy through the financial variables in the model, and the economy responds to them the same way whatever moved them. They also rest on the time trend. Their baseline leaves out credit spreads, although adding a daily credit spread or the monthly excess bond premium changes little when the FCI is included (Appendix~\ref{app:assump}). And our intervals do not include uncertainty about $\rs$ or the FCI weights. The broader lesson is that the fall in $\rs$ over the past three decades is more than a fact about the level of interest rates. It also makes the effects of policy harder to measure, unless rates are measured relative to $\rs$.

\section{Relaxing the key assumptions}\label{app:assump}
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes two restrictions specific to this note. The surprise reaches unemployment and prices only through the two-year yield and the FCI, and the three parts of the FCI act in proportion to their weights. This appendix relaxes each in turn, and then adds a monthly credit measure to the monthly block. Table~\ref{tab:cc} repeats the main steps for the first two exercises. In each exercise the daily model and the monthly equations are extended with the new variables. Everything else is as in the baseline, including the quadratic trend and 200 draws.

\textbf{Adding a credit spread.} The daily model and the monthly equations now include the Baa-10-year spread: Moody's Baa corporate yield minus the 10-year Treasury yield, available daily from 1986. It is a rough measure of credit conditions. Unlike the other variables in the model, it can respond to the surprise on impact. It has no 30-minute series, so its impact is the change over the announcement day regressed on the surprise, scaled to a 1 percentage point window move in the two-year yield. The impact is $-0.25$ percentage point. A tightening narrows this spread on the day, because corporate yields rise less than the 10-year Treasury yield. This is not the widening that a credit channel would suggest. After the announcement the spread moves little: its monthly average response stays between $-0.16$ and $+0.01$ percentage point over two years.

The effects of the surprise barely change (Table~\ref{tab:cc}). At the $\rs$ step, unemployment rises 0.28 to 0.35 percentage point after 12 months, compared with 0.36 to 0.40 in the baseline, and the price level falls 0.48 to 0.65 percent after 24 months, compared with 0.53 to 0.72. All are significant at the 90 percent level for every lag length. The nominal and real steps are also close to the baseline. So leaving out this spread does not drive the results. The test is limited. The spread is crude, because it includes expected default, and the excess bond premium that purges it is available only monthly. Earlier work in this project found that credit variables matter for the monetary-shock VAR, so a better measure may behave differently.

\textbf{Entering the parts of the FCI separately.} The FCI is replaced by its three weighted parts: the 10-year yield, equity valuation (CAPE) and the real dollar, each in the same step (nominal, real or $\rs$-gap) as the FCI. The parts sum to the FCI. Each enters the daily and monthly equations with its own coefficients, so their effects on unemployment and prices are no longer tied to the weights. Their impacts per 1 percentage point move in the two-year yield are 0.32, 0.39 and 0.27 index units (together 0.99, as for the FCI).

Signs and broad size agree with the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:cc}), but precision falls. At the $\rs$ step, unemployment rises 0.48, 0.33 and 0.18 percentage point after 12 months with 3, 6 and 12 lags. The first two are significant at the 90 percent level; the third is significant only at 68 percent. The price level falls 0.30 percent after 12 months with 12 lags and 0.50 percent after 24 months, significant at the 90 percent level; with 3 and 6 lags the 24-month falls are 0.24 and 0.41, significant only at the 68 percent level at 6 lags. At the nominal and real steps, unemployment is smaller and less precise at long lags, and prices barely move. The loss of precision is expected. The three parts add $2L$ coefficients to each equation, for 382 months of data.

Table~\ref{tab:prop} tests the restriction directly. The FCI model is nested in the model with the parts, so an $F$ test applies. It does not reject for unemployment at any step ($p$-values 0.10 to 0.67). For inflation it is rejected at the 5 percent level at the nominal step with every lag length and at the real step with 12 lags. At the $\rs$ step it is not rejected at 5 percent ($p$-values 0.08 to 0.24). The tests are plain $F$ tests with many restrictions and no correction for serial correlation, so they are indicative. Taken together, the FCI weights, which come from the response to policy news, summarize how unemployment responds well. For inflation they are a worse summary when rates are not measured relative to $\rs$, which fits the main result that the $\rs$ step matters most for prices.

\textbf{Adding the monthly excess bond premium.} The excess bond premium \citep{gilchrist2012} is the part of corporate bond spreads not explained by default risk. It is monthly, so it enters the monthly block as a third variable, treated like unemployment and inflation. It depends on its own lags and on lagged monthly averages of the two-year yield and the FCI. Within a month, it is set before the financial markets move, so the daily model responds to its current and lagged values. Its response to the surprise is not set to anything: as for unemployment and inflation, it is zero in the announcement window and then evolves with the model. Table~\ref{tab:ebp} gives the results.

Without the FCI, the premium removes the wrong-signed unemployment response. At the nominal step, unemployment at 12 months is $-0.27$ with 12 lags in the baseline and $+0.17$ with the premium; at the real step it goes from $-0.20$ to $+0.06$. With the FCI, adding the premium changes little. At the $\rs$ step, unemployment at 12 months is 0.39 to 0.41, compared with 0.36 to 0.40, and the price level at 24 months falls 0.52 to 0.71 percent, compared with 0.53 to 0.72. The prices result at the $\rs$ step without the FCI is also unchanged. The premium itself barely responds to the surprise: its response is within 0.13 percentage point at 12 months in every case, and is not significant with the FCI. So the FCI and the premium are substitutes for fixing the unemployment puzzle, but the FCI does not depend on the premium. This matches the earlier finding from the monetary-shock VAR of this project, where credit variables, not the market index, removed the wrong-signed post-1994 response.
