## (1) ABSTRACT: order of moves, sentence length, numbers

Facts. 11 sentences, about 150 words, one paragraph, no numbers, no equations, no citations. Sentences are short to medium. Order of moves as printed:

- Opens with the finding/premise, not with a question: "Activity shortfalls are more costly than strong activity." [Abstract, sentence 1]
- Approach in one first-person sentence: "I consider optimal monetary policy under discretion with an asymmetric (activity shortfalls) loss function." [Abstract]
- Key assumption as its own sentence: "The model satisfies the natural rate hypothesis." [Abstract]
- Results, one per sentence: "The asymmetric loss function and resulting optimal monetary policy exacerbates shortfalls in activity." [Abstract]
- Mechanism: "The additional frequency of activity shortfalls arises from the adjustment of expectations implied by the natural rate hypothesis." [Abstract]
- Link to literature in a clause: "The shortfalls asymmetry leads to an inflationary bias, similar to results in the time-consistency literature." [Abstract]
- Policy implication: "Mandating a central bank objective with greater symmetry than the social loss function improves outcomes." [Abstract]
- Closing implication: "As a result, the analysis implies that monetary accommodation of strength in economic activity likely requires justifications other than asymmetric costs of shortfalls." [Abstract, last sentence]
- Hedge in the abstract is limited to "likely": see the last quote. No robustness or limitations sentence in the abstract. Not determined whether this is a habit or a result of having none to report.

Short declarative stacking: "Greater symmetry lowers the magnitude of activity shortfalls. Greater symmetry also reduces inflation bias." [Abstract]

INTERPRETATION: Abstract = claim, method (one sentence), assumption, then a run of one-claim sentences with plain verbs (exacerbates, lowers, reduces, improves). No numbers, no "we show that".

## (2) INTRODUCTION: framing and contribution

First paragraph, in full (Section 1, paragraph 1):
> "I examine the consequences of monetary policy approaches resulting from asymmetries in the losses associated with employment shortfalls and strength. The model assumes the natural rate hypothesis—monetary policy is neutral in the long run. The analysis demonstrates that asymmetric monetary policy approaches have unintended consequences, including exacerbating activity shortfalls and creating an inflationary bias. Mandating that central banks behave (relatively) symmetrically mitigates these unintended consequences and improves welfare. The analysis suggests that, all else equal, the asymmetry in the costs of labor market weakness and strength does not warrant monetary accommodation of strong labor markets. Other factors, such as permanent effects of strong labor markets on economic potential, may be necessary for optimal monetary policy to accommodate labor market strength."

Facts: the first paragraph is the abstract restated with the main result up front; it contains no motivation before the claim. Motivation comes in paragraph 2: "Recent research and practice at central banks motivates the analysis."

Contribution paragraph (Section 1, paragraph beginning "These results may inform discussions"), in full:
> "These results may inform discussions of monetary policy in several ways. First, asymmetric loss functions have been used to inform monetary policy discussions. The motivation for such analyses is the intuitive notion that activity shortfalls are more costly than overshoots. However, such analyses have emphasized deterministic simulations in which the asymmetric responses to shocks—and resulting shifts in expectations—are absent. The simple notion that the natural rate hypothesis may limit the ability of policy to reap the gains of accommodative policy, and can even worsen employment shortfalls, may be a useful consideration for such analyses. Second, the analysis will demonstrate that a different type of Rogoff (1985) conservative central banker can ameliorate unintended consequences of discretionary policy in the face of an asymmetric loss function. Specifically, a central bank that bases policy on a loss function that is more symmetric than the social loss function improves welfare, in a manner similar to how a central bank with a lower weight on activity improves outcomes under discretionary policy in Rogoff (1985). Finally, these results also imply that relatively symmetric policy rules improve outcomes, providing another example of how rules can improve over discretion."

Facts:
- Contribution is framed as "may inform discussions", with a First / Second / Finally enumeration. It is not framed as "the first paper to" or "we fill a gap". Not determined whether the author makes novelty claims elsewhere; in the body he does write "This result is the central new result, and its derivation is slightly more involved." [Section 2, Result 3]
- Roadmap sentence (one paragraph, 3 sentences): "Section 2 presents the model and core results. As the model is simple, section 3 discusses how the model connects with more complex models and presents thoughts on deviations from the natural rate hypothesis that may affect the results. Section 4 concludes." [Section 1, last paragraph]
- Literature is placed in the introduction through footnotes and "For example" lists, not a separate literature review section: "For example, Aaronson et al, 2019; Hotchkiss and Moore, 2022." [Section 1, footnote 2]
- The intro states the mechanism in plain prose before the model: "As part of the equilibrating process that delivers long-run monetary neutrality, expectations shift to alter the nature of expansionary supply shocks" [Section 1, paragraph 4]

INTERPRETATION: The intro leads with the answer, gives context second, and states the mechanism in words before any algebra. The contribution paragraph is hedged by "may" and speaks of what the results can do for readers, not of the author's novelty.

## (3) SENTENCE-LEVEL PROSE HABITS

Person and voice
- First person singular, present tense, for what the author does: "I use a simple static model." [Section 2, "The model"]; "I have assumed a linear Phillips curve." [Section 3, "An asymmetric Phillips curve"]; "I examine the consequences of monetary policy approaches" [Section 1]. Conclusion switches to past tense: "I considered optimal monetary policy under discretion with an asymmetric (activity shortfalls) loss function." [Section 4]
- "The analysis" and "the model" as grammatical subjects are frequent: "The analysis demonstrates that asymmetric monetary policy approaches have unintended consequences" [Section 1]; "the analysis will demonstrate that a different type of Rogoff (1985) conservative central banker can ameliorate unintended consequences" [Section 1]
- "herein" is a habitual word: "the analysis herein relies on monetary neutrality" [Section 3]; "This type of neutrality—which is not the focus herein—is unlikely to hold" [Section 3]; "the loss function herein has no such temptation" [Section 2]
- "Our" appears once, despite a sole author: "In our baseline loss function (Equation 3), the bliss point for the desired level of activity is the natural rate" [Section 2, Result 2]
- Imperative "Consider"/"Suppose" to set up an example: "Consider optimal policy for this loss function following an aggregate supply (cost-push) shock" [Section 1]; "Suppose the slope of the Phillips curve is ¼ (κ = 0.25)" [Section 2]
- Some passive/impersonal: "The asymmetry in the loss function is introduced via a specification in which losses are separable in activity shortfalls and strength" [Section 1]

Plain verbs and short declaratives
- "Output above potential raises inflation." [Section 2, after Equation 1]
- "Inflation is positively related to expected inflation." [Section 2]
- "Greater symmetry also reduces inflation bias." [Abstract]
- "Note that a coefficient of unity on expected inflation ensures the long-run neutrality of inflation (the natural rate hypothesis)." [Section 2]
- Also long noun-heavy sentences: "Because the classic results on inflation bias under discretionary policy are well understood, the analysis assumes a loss function with no inflation bias in the absence of shocks to the economy." [Section 1]

How results are stated
- Formal numbered "Result" statements, each followed by a plain-language gloss: "Result 1: Aggregate demand shocks do not impact output and inflation." followed by "This result is standard, does not depend on the degree of asymmetry in the loss function, and follows directly from Equation 4." [Section 2]
- Results labeled by status: "This result is standard." [Result 2]; "This result is the central new result, and its derivation is slightly more involved." [Result 3]
- Results are tied to the figures by number: "Results 3 and 4 are clear." [Section 2, on Figure 1]
- Results are then stated in words with an economic reading: "Rather, the inflation bias stems from the asymmetric approach to the stabilization of shocks." [Section 2, after Result 4]
- Magnitude language is qualitative; the one numerical illustration is given with parameters in-line: "the optimal weights when the social loss function is asymmetric with weights αl = 0.25, αh = 0 occurs when the central bank is assigned nearly symmetric weights below αl of αl = 0.15, αh = 0.10 (not shown)" [Section 2, after Figure 2]

How equations are introduced
- Introduced with a short lead-in naming the object, then a labeled block "Equation N", then "In Equation N, [symbol] denotes ...": "Aggregate supply is represented by a Phillips curve linking inflation to expected inflation, the output gap, and a cost-push (supply) shock, as in" [Section 2, "The model"]; "In Equation 1, π denotes inflation, y is the output gap, u is the supply shock with mean zero, and κ is a (positive) parameter." [Section 2]
- Verbal reading follows each equation: "The deviation of output from potential depends negatively on the real interest rate." [Section 2, after Equation 2]
- Cross-references use "Equation N" in full, capitalized, not "(N)": "Equation 4 and Equation 1 imply that" [Result 3]
- Role of each equation stated: "The IS curve will have no role in the optimal policy problem, as the policymaker chooses the outcomes for inflation and output." [Section 2]

- "For example, positive hysteresis from strong labor markets may provide such a justification—a subject for future research." [Section 4, last sentence]

Facts on tone: Limitations are stated as flat reports of what the model assumes or omits, usually followed by a direction in which the result would change ("would have the opposite implication", "would likely carry over", "although comparable results likely hold"). Hedges are present but are modal verbs and "likely" ("may", "likely", "at least questionable", "reasonable, albeit debatable"). The only self-deprecating wording found is "The model is stylized, and such a quantitative example are only illustrative." No sentence of the form "we apologize", "a caveat", "unfortunately", or "limitation" was found (searched by reading; the word "limitation" does not occur). Section 3 is framed as a neutral heading ("Additional considerations"), not "Limitations".

INTERPRETATION: Section 3 treats each departure from the model as a question about which way the result would move, and points to other work for the quantitative check. The author does not apologize. The place for these is a late body section plus footnotes, not the abstract or intro. Cannot tell from one theory paper whether an empirical paper by this author would use a "Robustness" section or an appendix.

Also frequent: "As a result," as a sentence opener, e.g. "As a result, optimal policy will allow all of the cost-push shock to feed through to stronger activity and will stabilize inflation at its objective." - Section 3, third subsection: "While monetary neutrality is a reasonable approximation, the literature has accumulated evidence suggesting that cyclical fluctuations have persistent or permanent effects."
- Section 4: "Activity shortfalls are more costly than strong activity."

Facts: openings are direct statements of what the model/author does or assumes. Section 3 has no opening paragraph of its own between the heading and the first subhead; Section 3 begins with the subhead "An asymmetric Phillips curve" directly.

Section closings: sections end on an implication or a pointer, not a summary. Examples:
- End of Section 2: "Changes in expectations can cause policy actions to have unintended effects, such as an exacerbation of shortfalls in activity when policies to accommodate strong activity are pursued." [Section 2, last body sentence before Figure 2]
- End of Section 3: "Putting these thoughts together, the natural rate hypothesis suggests that a shortfalls approach is costly to society in the absence of positive hysteresis, pointing to the value of additional analyses of potential positive long-run effects of strong economic activity on potential activity." [Section 3]

Conclusion structure (Section 4), facts: one paragraph of 11 sentences, about 150 words. It is the abstract restated in near-identical wording, past tense for the approach, with two added sentences at the end. Comparison:
- Abstract: "Activity shortfalls are more costly than strong activity. I consider optimal monetary policy under discretion with an asymmetric (activity shortfalls) loss function."
- Conclusion: "Activity shortfalls are more costly than strong activity. Discussions of monetary policy have emphasized this asymmetry in recent years. I considered optimal monetary policy under discretion with an asymmetric (activity shortfalls) loss function."
- New in conclusion: "Moreover, greater costs from activity shortfalls do not justify accommodating demand-driven strength in activity. It remains optimal to stabilize activity and inflation in response to demand shocks." and the closing pointer to future research quoted in part (4).
- Conclusion adds the word "counterintuitively": "Greater symmetry counterintuitively lowers the magnitude of activity shortfalls." Abstract has no such word.
- Conclusion has no limitations paragraph and no policy-recommendation list; the only forward-looking element is "a subject for future research."

INTERPRETATION: The conclusion repeats the abstract nearly verbatim, then adds one qualifying implication and one research pointer. Verbatim repetition of the abstract is a visible habit in this paper.
