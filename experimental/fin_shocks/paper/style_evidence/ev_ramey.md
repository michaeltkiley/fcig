## 1. Framing of questions and motivation

Q1 [Sec 1, opening sentence] Opens with intellectual history, not a gap in the literature.
> "At the beginning of the 20th century, economists began to recognize the importance of impulses and propagation mechanisms for explaining business cycle fluctuations."

Q2 [Sec 1, paragraph 3] The motivating question is posed as two direct questions, then answered with a first-person statement of purpose.
> "Are we destined to remain forever ignorant of the fundamental causes of economic fluctuations? Are Slutsky's "random causes" unknowable? In this chapter, I will summarize the new methodological innovations and what their application has revealed about the propagation of the leading candidates for macroeconomic shocks"

Q3 [Sec 2.1, opening] Section opens with a question about what the object is.
> "What, exactly, are the macroeconomic shocks that we seek to estimate empirically?"

Q4 [Sec 3, opening] Section opens by placing itself in the sequence, then states what the section does.
> "Having discussed the definition of macroeconomic shocks and the leading methods for identifying them, I now turn to the first of the candidate shocks that will be discussed in detail: monetary policy shocks."

Q5 [Sec 3, intro] She asks the reader's objection herself, then answers it plainly.
> "If many macroeconomists now believe that monetary policy shocks themselves contribute little to macroeconomic outcomes, why is there such a large literature trying to identify them? The reason is that we want to identify nonsystematic movements in monetary policy so that we can estimate causal effects of money on macroeconomic variables."

Q6 [Sec 3, intro] States why she picks the cases she studies.
> "I focus on these two types of shocks in part because they both imply very similar effects of monetary policy on output, despite using different identification methods and different samples."

Q7 [Sec 4, opening] Sections open with a one-sentence statement of scope.
> "This section reviews the main identification methods and summarizes existing results from the empirical literature seeking to identify and estimate the effects of fiscal policy shocks. It also presents some new results comparing several leading identified shocks."

## 2. Sentence-level habits

S1 [Sec 1, roadmap] Heavy first person ("I") for the author's own acts; roadmap sentences use the section as subject with plain verbs.
> "Section 2 begins by defining what a macroeconomic shock is. It then summarizes the many tools used for identifying macroeconomic shocks and computing impulse responses."

S2 [Sec 2.1] Explicit first-person definition of terms.
> "In this chapter, I view shocks, VAR innovations, and instruments to be distinct concepts, although identification assumptions may equate them in many cases."

S3 [Sec 2.1] First-person conditional rebuttal.
> "I would respond that these are not primitive shocks, but rather the endogenous responses of policies to a primitive shock."

S4 [Sec 3.5.1] Descriptive prose of figures: short, concrete, plain verbs (jumps, falls, rises, troughs), numbers inserted in the clause.
> "The Federal funds rate jumps up temporarily but then falls back to 0 by 6 months. This temporary blip in the funds rate, however, sets off a prolonged recession. Industrial production begins to fall in the next month and troughs 21 months later."

S5 [Sec 3.5.1] Numbers introduced by the table and then characterized in words ("very modest").
> "The first column shows the trough effect on output of a shock that raises the funds rate to a peak of 100 basis points. Even in CEE's original sample, the effects are very modest, less than a 0.5% fall."

S6 [Sec 3.5.3] Numbers stated with the test, in first person, with the estimate and standard error.
> "if I regress it on its lagged value, the coefficient is 0.31 with a robust standard error of 0.11."

S7 [Sec 3.5.3] Enumeration with "First, Second, Third" in running prose, each a flat finding.
> "First, the shock is not zero mean. The mean is 0.013 and is statistically different from zero. Second, it is serially correlated"

S8 [Sec 3.5.1] Passive appears for method choices; first person for choices she made.
> "I estimate a specification similar to Christiano et al.'s (1999) specification but use Coibion's (2012) macroeconomic variables for the first block."

S9 [Sec 3.5.2] Evaluative adjectives about the data ("odd", "puzzling") used directly.
> "An odd feature of the impulse responses shown in Fig. 2A is the robust rebound of industrial production after 2 years of recession."

Sentence length (observation): most sentences in the quoted passages are one clause or two; the longest are definitional (sec 2.1). I did not compute a statistic.

## 3. Summarizing across studies and stating own results

R1 [Sec 3.4, opening] Table introduced with a stated selection rule and a stated standardization, with its limit stated in the same sentence.
> "Rather than trying to be encyclopedic in listing all results, I have chosen leading examples obtained with the various identifying assumptions. In addition, I attempted to standardize the results by normalizing the peak of response of the federal funds rate to 100 basis points; this standardization does not control for differences in persistence of the response as I discuss later."

R2 [Sec 3.4] Cross-study result stated as a grouping, then the contrast.
> "all produce rather small effects of monetary policy shocks. Also, most are plagued by the price puzzle to greater or lesser degree. On the other hand, Romer and Romer (2004), Coibion (2012), Barakchian-Crowe (2013), and Gertler-Karadi (2015) all find larger impacts of a given shock on output."

R3 [Sec 3.5, opening] Purpose of her own estimates stated in one sentence, before results.
> "I now explore the robustness of the effects of monetary policy shocks using some of the new methods introduced in the literature to deal with both the foresight problems and the recursiveness assumption."

R4 [Sec 3.5.1] Her result is stated as a change in the headline finding; "dramatically".
> "The results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the unemployment rate."

R5 [Sec 3.5.2] Own results compared back to the literature with a plain verdict.
> "The results are similar to those found by Barakchian and Crowe (2013): contractionary monetary policy shocks appear to be expansionary."

R6 [Sec 3.5.2] Within-section mini-summary.
> "In sum, relaxing the recursiveness assumption imposed by Romer and Romer's hybrid VAR leads to several puzzles. A contractionary monetary policy shock is now expansionary in its first year and the price puzzle is very pronounced."

R7 [Sec 4.1.3] Design logic of a comparison stated up front.
> "Thus, any differences in results will be due to the identification methods rather than differences in data or implementation."

R8 [Sec 4.1.3 end, "To summarize"] Cross-shock summary with ranges.
> "To summarize, most of the aggregate analyses find government spending multipliers between 0.6 and 1.5."

R9 [Sec 7] Variance-decomposition results stated with numbers and CI, then classed as important or not.
> "The most important for both output and hours is Ben Zeev and Khan's (2015) news about IST change. [...] For example, at 8 quarters the contribution to hours is 40%. The 90% confidence interval (not shown in the table) is (25, 54)."

## 4. Robustness, sensitivity, limits (every such sentence I found in the read scope)

Observation: robustness is framed as a test whose outcome is reported; findings of fragility are stated flatly. Hedging appears in the closing interpretive paragraph, and is attached to the interpretation, not to the estimates. No apologies found.

B1 [Sec 3.1] Robustness named as the main message of a benchmark.
> "Perhaps the most important message of the chapter was the robustness of the finding that a contractionary monetary policy shock [...] had significant negative effects on output."

B2 [Sec 3.5.2, on Coibion] Sensitivity reported as a plain fact of the cited result.
> "R&R's main results [...] are very sensitive to the inclusion of the period of nonborrowed reserves targeting, 1979-82, and the number of lags"

B3 [Sec 3.5.1] Subsample instability reported without qualifier.
> "The results change dramatically and imply that increases in the federal funds rate lower the unemployment rate."

B4 [Sec 3.5.2] Alternative-specification result in one flat line.
> "In this case, the price puzzle is even worse and the initial expansionary effects on output and unemployment are no better."

B5 [Sec 3.5.2] Inference from a failed check, stated as a finding.
> "Thus, including FAVAR-type factors does not reproduce the results obtained using the recursiveness assumption."

B6 [Sec 3.5.2] Limit of her own explanation, stated as a consequence.
> "However, this means that even with the R&R shock, one is forced to make the recursiveness assumption, which does not have a solid economic basis."

B7 [Sec 3.5.2 footnote r] Reports an alternative she tried and rejected, with the reason.
> "I found that this measure sometimes behaved oddly because of the tendency of some of the variables to oscillate around zero."

B8 [Sec 3.5.2 footnote t] Robustness to lags in a one-line footnote.
> "The point estimates are similar if more lags are included."

B9 [Sec 3.5.3] Unresolved result reported as unresolved.
> "The results (not shown) were still puzzling."

B10 [Sec 3.5.3] Unresolved question asked, then candidate explanations offered, then left open.
> "Why does the Jordà method give such different estimates from the proxy SVAR? One possible explanation is the different method and sample used to estimate the impulse response function."

B11 [Sec 3.6] Summary of the robustness exercise: the headline contrast, "rosy reconciliation picture disappears".
> "This rosy reconciliation picture disappears, however, when the specifications are sub-jected to some robustness tests. My explorations have highlighted several potential issues, some of which were already noted in the literature. First, the original Christiano et al. (1999) specification, as well as many other specifications, does not hold up well in later samples."

B12 [Sec 3.6] Interpretation is flagged as her argument ("I would argue"), and hedged with "most likely" / "It is likely".
> "I would argue that the most likely reason for the breakdown of many specifications in the later sample is simply that we can no longer identify monetary policy shocks well."

B13 [Sec 3.6] Admission of what the evidence cannot settle, in a plain sentence.
> "Of course, one worries that the structure of the economy may have changed in the last few decades, but we simply do not have enough information to produce estimates with any great certainty."

B14 [Sec 3.6] A caveat is turned into a policy upside.
> "While this is bad news for econometric identification, it is good news for economic policy."

B15 [Sec 5.5] Limit of the shock measures stated, then a "more research" line.
> "My analysis shows, however, that some of the estimated shocks are not highly correlated with other versions. Moreover, many of the shocks are serially correlated or Granger-caused by other variables. This suggests that more research needs to be done to refine these shock measures."

B16 [Sec 7, last paragraph] The only place a limit is attached to the paper's own headline conclusion; it is one subordinate clause before the conclusion, which is stated in full.
> "While much more work should be done exploring the plausibility of the identifying assumptions, testing the robustness of these shock estimates, and making sure that they do satisfy the properties a shock should satisfy, these results suggest that we are indeed closer to understanding Slutsky's random shocks that drive macroeconomic fluctuations."

## 5. Openings, closings, cross-references

X1 [Sec 1, roadmap] Forward references exist only in the introduction roadmap (one paragraph per section). Example:
> "The topic of Section 3 is monetary shocks and their effects on the macroeconomy. The section summarizes the existing literature and the challenges to identification."

X2 [Sec 1, roadmap closing] Roadmap states the conclusion in advance.
> "It concludes that we have made substantial progress in understanding the shocks that drive the macroeconomy."

X3 [Sec 3.5.2] Back references are by section number, inline, short. Several appear in sec 3.5 (I counted at least 5 in the 3.5 passages read).
> "As discussed in Section 3.3.2, Romer and Romer (2004) (R&R) sought to overcome the problem of superior Federal Reserve information"
> "As discussed in Section 2.5, anticipations of future policy actions can lead to the problem of a nonfundamental moving average representation."

X4 [Sec 3.5.2] Forward reference to later content is rare in the body; the one I saw was in sec 3.4 ("as I discuss later", quoted in R1). Within-subsection transitions are mostly "Next," / "I then".
> "Next, I consider the effects of the shocks identified by Romer and Romer (2004)."

X5 [Sec 3.6, opening] Summary sections open by recalling the benchmark, and close with an evaluative judgement, not a teaser.
> "When Christiano et al. (1999) wrote their Handbook chapter, they provided what became a benchmark framework for identifying monetary policy shocks and tracing their effects."
> "Monetary policy can have big effects, but it is likely that monetary shocks are no longer an important source of macro instability."

X6 [Sec 3.6] Summary closes by posing and answering her own question.
> "What, then, are we to conclude about the output effects of monetary shocks? I would argue that the best evidence still remains the historical case studies"

X7 [Sec 7] Conclusion returns to the opening question.
> "The introduction to this chapter posed the question: Are we destined to remain forever ignorant of the fundamental causes of economic fluctuations? I would argue "no." Although we still have far to go, substantial progress has been made since Cochrane (1994) asked that question."

X8 [Sec 4.3, opening] Summary section begins by restating what the section did, then gives the findings.
> "In this section, I have summarized some of the main methods and findings concerning the effects of fiscal shocks. For both government spending and taxes, the methods that use external narrative series tend to find bigger effects on output than the more traditional SVAR method."

## 6. Tables and figures in the text

T1 [Sec 3.4] A table is introduced by naming what it summarizes, in the first sentence of the section.
> "Table 1 summarizes some of the main results from the literature on the impact of the identified monetary shock on output, the contribution of monetary shocks to output fluctuations, and whether the price puzzle is present."

T2 [Sec 3.5.1] Figures introduced by number, line style, and what they show; then the text narrates the lines.
> "Fig. 1 shows the estimated impulses for this SVAR. The solid black line and shaded areas are the point estimates and 90% bootstrap confidence bands for the system estimated over CEE's sample period"

T3 [Sec 3.5.2] Row references to a table, in prose.
> "Rows 3 and 4 of Table 2 show the trough effects and variance decompositions for the R&R VAR."

T4 [Table 2 title] Table titles can carry ownership: "My Estimates".
> "Table 2 Effects of Monetary Policy Shocks on Industrial Production: My Estimates"

T5 [Table 1 cell, column "% of output explained by shock"] Unknown entries are marked with a question mark, not omitted (Gertler-Karadi row shows "?"). Observed from the garbled table text; I cannot confirm layout.

Notes: figures here are multi-panel impulse response charts with 90% bands (stated in captions). Table 13 notes list variable order, lags and trend ("a standard VAR with four lags and a quadratic trend").

## 7. Notably absent (from what I read)

- Abstract: describes contents and ends with one unhedged claim. Quote: "It concludes that we are much closer to understanding the shocks that drive economic fluctuations than we were 20 years ago." No "we caution" or "preliminary".
- I found no apologies ("unfortunately", "we acknowledge") in the sections read; I did not search exhaustively across sections 4.1.1-4.2 and 5.1-5.4.
- No "contribution" or "novelty" claim framed as the author's own advance in the introduction; the stated job is to "summarize" and "explore".
- No "Section X will show" teasers inside body sections; cross-references are mostly backward and by number.
- Mathematics is confined to Section 2 (Eqs. 1-5) and one regression (Eq. 17); results sections are in words and numbers.

## INTERPRETATION (labeled; not quotable fact)

INTERPRETATION 1: Fragility is reported as a result ("The results change dramatically", "no better", "still puzzling"), with the author's explanation held to the end of the section and tagged with "I would argue" and "likely". The pattern is: report, then separate interpretation sentence marked as hers.
INTERPRETATION 2: The summary of a robustness exercise (3.6) openly reverses the earlier "reconciliation" ("This rosy reconciliation picture disappears") and then states what she thinks the best evidence now is, rather than ending on uncertainty.
INTERPRETATION 3: Cross-references and forward pointers are concentrated in the intro roadmap and in short "As discussed in Section X" back-pointers; body sections rarely preview results.
INTERPRETATION 4: Table/figure sentences identify the object and the quantity ("trough effect ... of a shock that raises the funds rate to a peak of 100 basis points") and then give a one-phrase verdict ("very modest").
INTERPRETATION 5: Caveat on use: this is a handbook survey chapter, so framing as "what have we learned" may not transfer to a paper presenting new estimates; the robustness-reporting tone (the part most relevant to a research note) is drawn from her own "Explorations" sections.
