## 1. Boivin, Kiley, Mishkin (2010)

### 1.1 Channels defined (Section II, pp. 5-22; Table 1 at pp. 69-70)
They split into neoclassical (perfect financial markets) and non-neoclassical (credit view). Quote (p. 5): "Monetary transmission can be categorized into two basic types, neoclassical channels in which financial markets are perfect and non-neoclassical channels that involve financial market imperfections, which are usually referred to as the credit view."

Neoclassical, grouped by the spending they affect (p. 7): "for investment, the key channels are the direct interest rate channel operating through the user cost of capital and the closely related Tobin's q channel; for consumption, the channels operate through wealth effects and intertemporal substitution effects; and for trade, the direct channel operates through the exchange rate."

| Channel | Financial variable(s) it uses | Source in paper |
|---|---|---|
| Interest-rate / user cost of capital | Real after-tax interest rate, and in traditional models a LONG-term rate: "In traditional econometric models, this link typically is formalized through direct inclusion of a long-term interest rate in the user cost formula, rather than a short-term interest rate" (p. 8); in DSGE it is the expected path of short rates. Also expected appreciation of the capital asset (house prices) | pp. 7-9, 11-12 |
| Tobin's q | Stock prices ("a direct link between stock prices and investment spending", p. 10) | p. 9-10 |
| Wealth effects | Stock and house prices; Fed staff MPC out of wealth "between 3 and 4 cents per dollar, for both housing wealth and stock market wealth" (p. 13) | pp. 12-14 |
| Intertemporal substitution | Short-term real rate; modest, "not a factor in ... MPS or FRB/US" (p. 14) | p. 12-14 |
| Exchange rate | Exchange rate, via interest differentials (UIP / portfolio balance); "earlier econometric models ... estimated sensitivities [were] small" whereas UIP models "find a larger role" (p. 15) | p. 14-15 |
| Credit view (non-neoclassical): regulation-induced credit supply (Reg Q), bank lending, bank capital, balance sheet (financial accelerator, external finance premium, household collateral/house prices, cash flow) | Credit quantities, external finance premia / corporate spreads, house prices, bank capital | pp. 15-22; Table 1 |

Table 1 (p. 69-70) column "Incorporation in policy models": interest rate/cost of capital: "Standard in large-scale models (like the MPS or FRB/US models, Fair, 2004) and DSGE models"; wealth effects: standard in large-scale and DSGE; intertemporal substitution: "Absent from traditional large-scale. Standard in DSGE models"; exchange rate: "Standard in large-scale models. Incorporated in international DSGE models"; credit channels: incorporated only for relevant periods in some large-scale models (MPS) / "Not explicitly incorporated in most" (end of the table text seen; remaining cells not transcribed).

### 1.2 What they say about the transmission set
- Abstract (p. ii): "The core channels of policy transmission – the neoclassical links between short-term policy interest rates, other asset prices such as long-term interest rates, equity prices, and the exchange rate, and the consequent effects on household and business demand – have remained steady from early policy-oriented models ... to modern [DSGE] models. In contrast, non-neoclassical channels, such as credit-based channels, have remained outside the core models."
- Section VI (p. 53): "the neoclassical channels – direct interest rate effects on investment spending, wealth and intertemporal substitution effects on consumption, and the trade effects through the exchange rate – have remained the core channels in macroeconomic modeling."
- Important for the note's a-priori set: they do NOT define a "transmission set of financial variables" as such. The set (long rates, equities, exchange rate; house prices through wealth/user cost) is what the neoclassical channels imply. The phrase "interest rates broadly construed including corporate rates" is the note's own gloss: the paper's rates are policy rate and long-term rates (user-cost), and corporate bond spreads appear under the CREDIT view (external finance premium; Section V and Figure 6, pp. 40-41: AAA/BAA minus Treasury spreads at 1, 3, 5, 10 years, plus Gilchrist-Ortiz-Zakrajsek premium). So putting corporate yields in the "rates" group is a choice not dictated by BKM; corporate spreads are in their non-neoclassical block. Worth stating when citing.
- Credit side (p. 53-54, 55): non-neoclassical channels are "sparse" in macro models; "the variation in external finance premia in such empirical models seems more important as a source of shocks driving fluctuations than as an endogenous transmission mechanism." Open question left (p. 55).
- Exchange rate de-emphasised for the US (footnote 13, p. 23): "the net effect on aggregate demand from international trade following a monetary policy innovation tends to be close to zero, as the exchange-rate induced movements in exports tend to be offset, on net, by shifts in imports ... For this reason, we do not focus on this channel much in our subsequent empirical work." Relevant tension with including the dollar as a core variable in the note: BKM list it as a core channel but argue it is of small net importance for the US.
- Expectations: "the entire expected path of interest rates, not solely the current value, influences asset prices and spending" (p. 6); "more than 1/2 of the effect of monetary policy on activity over the first year of a change in the federal funds rate reflects the expectations channel, rather than direct interest rate, wealth, or exchange rate channels" (p. 27, citing Reifschneider-Tetlow-Williams 1999 for FRB/US). Supports using the path (NS/GSS), not only the funds rate.

### 1.3 Evidence on how responses changed over time (Sections IV-V, pp. 28-52)
- Reduced-form (p. 1-2, Figure 1): output and expenditure growth negatively correlated with lagged funds rate in 1962Q1-1979Q3, positively in 1984Q1-2008Q4; they warn this is mostly policy behaviour, not transmission (p. 2).
- FAVAR (181 indicators; recursive identification; samples 1962:1-1979:9 and 1984:1-2008:12; pp. 35-38): "The effect of monetary policy on aggregate real activity seems to have become smaller in the post-1984 compared to the earlier period, and perhaps more persistent as well" (p. 38); prices respond much less post-1984; housing starts, IP, employment etc. "somewhat smaller initially" with more protracted response (p. 38).
- Expectations channel (p. 39-40): "a considerable reduction in the effect of monetary policy shock on expected inflation"; "better anchoring of inflation expectations in the period following the Volcker disinflation."
- Credit/spreads (pp. 40-41, Figure 6): "the magnitude of response of corporate spreads is somewhat smaller in the first year in the recent period while perhaps also being more persistent" (p. 41).
- Mortgage credit (pp. 23-25): after Reg Q removal and securitisation, "mortgage rates respond more quickly and persistently to changes in monetary policy. As a result, monetary policy now primarily affects housing through pricing channels rather than through credit supply restrictions" (p. 24); "these changes are to a significant degree shifts only in the short-run timing of responses."
- DSGE (pp. 42-52): a 100bp funds-rate rise gives smaller inflation, output and risk-spread responses in 1984-2008 than 1962-79 (p. 47); swapping only the policy-rule parameters reproduces the change (Figure 8, p. 47); swapping the financial-accelerator or investment-adjustment parameters does not (pp. 47-48). Conclusion (abstract): shifts "are accounted for by changes in policy behavior and the effect of these changes on expectations, leaving little role for changes in underlying private-sector behavior."
- Caveat on their own method (p. 48-49): the BGG accelerator "does not provide much information" on credit-channel change; linear model may miss non-linearity.
- Section VI (p. 53): the literature "has not suggested large changes over time in the strength of these channels" for the neoclassical set; main change is the systematic component of policy.

### 1.4 Relevance to measuring the stance through financial variables
- Opening sentence of the paper (p. 1): "understanding how monetary policy affects the economy is essential to evaluating what the stance of monetary policy is at a particular point in time. Even if a central bank's policy instrument, for example, the federal funds rate in the United States, is low, monetary policy may well be restrictive because of effects that monetary policy has had on other asset prices and quantities." This is the cleanest published justification for a financial-variables stance measure and for funds-rate equivalents. Quote it.
- Time variation matters for the note: sensitivities (response of long rates, equities, dollar, spreads per unit of funds-rate move) and the policy rule both changed; BKM find the output response smaller since the 1980s and tie it to policy behaviour/expectations, so a funds-rate-equivalent built from fixed weights from one period inherits the regime. BKM's evidence concerns real activity and inflation, not the financial-variable event-window responses; they give NO evidence on the stability of equity/exchange-rate/long-rate responses to policy surprises (high-frequency identification is not used; sample is 1962-2008). Do not cite them for stability of the financial block. UNVERIFIED whether the Handbook version adds any such material.
- They treat model-based channels as "standard in FRB/US", consistent with using FRB/US/FCI-G as the model-based yardstick.

## 2. Caballero and Simsek on financial conditions

### 2.1 Which empirical FCI (question a)
- Financial Conditions Targeting (Sep 2026 version), Sec. 2.1, pp. 6-8: "We measure financial conditions using the Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth index (FCI-G) constructed by Ajello et al. (2023). The index uses the Fed's macroeconomic models to estimate the contribution of changes in seven financial variables over the preceding three years to GDP growth over the next year. These variables are the Dow Jones stock price index, the Zillow house price index, the broad dollar index, the federal funds rate, the 10-year Treasury yield, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate, and the triple-B corporate bond yield. A positive value indicates tighter financial conditions: an index value of 1 implies a one-percentage-point drag on GDP growth over the next year." Uses the baseline (three-year) version, quarterly, 1990Q1 onward; main sample 1990Q1-2019Q4 (p. 8, App. B.1 p. 68). The rev0 (Nov 2024) draft also uses FCI-G (grep: Figure 1 and App. B).
- Chicago Fed NFCI is used only as a splice: "Since the FCI index is available from 1990 onwards, we use the Chicago Fed FCI (NFCI) for the sample period 1973-1990 when computing the IRFs of monetary policy shocks. Ajello et al. (2023) show that their index is similar in sample to the Chicago Fed FCI, and estimated IRFs are similar if we use that FCI for the full sample." (App. B.1, p. 69). The NFCI is in the data list (p. 68). Splicing is done without rescaling as far as the text shows (no scale adjustment mentioned); UNVERIFIED beyond that.
- Goldman Sachs FCI is cited only as literature (Hatzius et al. 2017, Hatzius and Stehn 2018); not used.
- FCI-plot (26 Mar 2026), Sec. 2.2 p. 8 and App. B p. 42: same FCI-G, "quarterly frequency version of the baseline FCI-G index. The baseline version computes the cumulative effect on one-year-ahead GDP growth of the three-month changes in financial conditions over the past three years." Data from the Fed FEDS Notes page. They describe all three families: "There are several well-known financial conditions indexes, such as the Goldman Sachs FCI (see Hatzius and Stehn (2018)), the National FCI by the Chicago Fed, and the FCI-Growth index (FCI-G) by Ajello et al. (2023). The FCI-G is notable for relying on the FRB/US model and other large-scale DSGE models developed by the Federal Reserve."
- Opinionated Markets (AER 2022): NO financial conditions index at all (grep for FCI/financial conditions index finds nothing relevant). Policy transmits through interest rates; the only asset price is a model "market portfolio" price q_t = q* + a_t + y_t (Eq. 8) tied to output. Data: Greenbook/Tealbook, Blue Chip, SPF-type forecasts, fed funds futures/forwards (Fig. 1-2, App. D). FCI-plot says (Intro, p. 4): "The main difference is that in our current model policy transmits through financial conditions (as we see in the data), whereas in Caballero and Simsek (2022) policy transmits through interest rates as in the textbook New-Keynesian model."
- In the theory, the "FCI" is the log aggregate asset price (market portfolio): "we interpret p_t as the model counterpart to an FCI" with y_t = m + p_t + delta_t (Eq. 7, FCT p. 17-18); footnote 2 (p. 18): "p_t is stated in price, rather than the usual rates convention of FCIs. Thus, it has the opposite sign convention of the standard FCIs, where an increase in the index typically means tightening."

### 2.2 Scaling relative to the policy rate (question b)
- I found NO funds-rate equivalent, no conversion of FCI-G into policy-rate units, and no mapping from rate changes to FCI units in any of the three papers read. The FCI stays in FCI-G units (percentage-point drag on next-year GDP growth) and is related to the rate only through (i) the VAR (FCI-G, output, inflation, 3-month rate, EBP, etc.; 12 variables, Minnesota prior; FCT Sec. 2.1 p. 8) and (ii) the stylised model (policy rate affects the asset price/FCI; output depends on FCI only).
- Stock-weight arithmetic (FCT p. 16): "The contemporaneous weight of stock prices in the FCI-G is around -0.02. Holding the other components constant, a stock price increase of 1.9% therefore lowers the index by approximately 0.038." (Note the sign convention in their VAR section, loosening = negative.) This is the only quantitative FCI-G weight cited.
- Policy-rate to FCI link in data: FCT App. B.3.5-B.3.6 (pp. 73-74): they take the Aruoba-Drechsel (2025) monetary policy shock IRF and, using the noise-shock IRF, "approximately enforce FCI_t = 0 on impact and in expectation for the first year" (McKay-Wolf counterfactual). Result: "the path of interest rates is basically unchanged ... the response [of the output gap] is essentially zero at all horizons ... in a counterfactual economy where short-term interest rates and broader Financial Conditions are disconnected, monetary policy shocks would have essentially no impact on output gaps or inflation." No numeric FCI-per-100bp elasticity was stated in the text I read (it may be in Figure 18, which I did not inspect: UNVERIFIED).
- FCI-plot treats the rate-to-FCI map qualitatively as "incomplete" (Sec. 2.2, p. 8-9) and rate guidance is "lost in translation". The FCI-plot (Sec. 4, p. 28-30, Figs 5-6) uses the FCI-G in FCI-G units on a quarterly VAR counterfactual under optimal policy and plots expected FCI paths; no rate-unit equivalent.
- Survey evidence (FCI-plot Sec. 2.5, p. 13-14, Table 1): MacroPolicy Perspectives Shadow Survey asks qualitatively "What is your expectation for broader U.S. financial conditions over the next year? (scale of 1-4, where 1=Ease, 4=Tighten a lot)". Not a quantitative index.

### 2.3 Main claims on financial conditions vs interest rates (question c)
- FCT Intro (p. 3): "Monetary policy transmission occurs primarily through financial market conditions. Businesses and consumers react to market-based interest rates, stock prices, house prices, and currency exchange rates rather than to the policy rate alone." Also FCIs "identify volatile asset prices, especially stock prices, as their main driver in the U.S. and most major economies" and "We focus on the stock market because it is the main driver of the U.S. FCI-G index over our sample" (p. 6). Figure 1 (p. 7): stock prices main driver, then exchange rate, house prices at times.
- FCT Remark 1 (p. 17-18): "the interest rate has no direct effect on output. It operates entirely through the price of the market portfolio. This feature follows from our assumptions, including log utility, but it also has empirical support when p_t is interpreted as a broad FCI."
- FCT main result: financial noise explains "up to 20%" of variance of financial conditions and output gap; announcing an FCI target as a soft near-term target (FCI targeting) improves stabilisation by "recruiting" arbitrageurs; interest-rate guidance "typically has the opposite effect" (p. 4).
- FCI-plot Abstract: "communicating expected interest rates is 'lost in translation': because the mapping from interest rates to financial conditions is inherently incomplete, interest rate projections leave substantial residual uncertainty about the central bank's intended financial conditions." Sec. 2.2 (p. 9): "fluctuations in the index are driven primarily by risky asset prices – specifically, stock prices and exchange rates, and occasionally house prices – rather than by various yields", and "This dominance of risky asset prices in driving financial conditions already suggests that the mapping from interest rates to financial conditions is incomplete."
- Evidence cited for the incomplete mapping (p. 9): Bernanke-Kuttner 2005 (equity response mostly via equity premium), Bekaert et al. 2013, Hanson-Stein 2015, Gertler-Karadi 2015, Bauer et al. 2023 (risk appetite), Boehm-Kroner 2024 (a "Fed non-yield shock" moves equities and the dollar without moving the yield curve). Dissent noted in fn. 5: Nagel-Xu 2024 (equity response mostly via yield-curve changes). FCT p. 18 also cites Bauer et al. (2026): transmission operates "primarily through risk appetite and risk asset prices."
- The VoxEU title "Financial conditions matter more than interest rates" was seen only as a snippet: UNVERIFIED.

### 2.4 What bears on comparing indexes (question d)
- They assert, without showing, that the families are similar: "Ajello et al. (2023) show that their index is similar in sample to the Chicago Fed FCI" (FCT App. B.1, p. 69); estimated IRFs similar with NFCI for the full sample. No direct comparison or common unit is done by C-S.
- Their one-line taxonomy of why indexes differ (FCI-plot p. 8): FCI-G rests on FRB/US and Fed DSGE models, incorporating "borrowing and investment effects of various interest rates, the wealth effects of stock and house prices, and the expenditure switching effects of exchange rates"; thus "a summary statistic of how recent asset price and interest rate changes are expected to influence output growth over the next year according to the Federal Reserve's structural models." Note this attributes effects to model structure, i.e., the output-equivalent lens in the note.
- Implications of their argument for the note: if risky-asset prices (equities, dollar) dominate FCI variation and are only partly policy-driven (noise explains up to 20% of FCI variance; policy only gradually offsets), then a funds-rate-equivalent of an FCI is NOT a measure of the policy stance; supports the plan's "source-agnostic tightening equivalent" caveat (decision 5). Their evidence that part of FCI movement is non-policy noise and part of policy transmits through risk premia gives the two sides of the same point.
- Sign convention differs between their model (price; opposite of standard) and their empirical FCI-G (positive = tighter); GS and NFCI also positive = tighter. Unit conventions: FCI-G in pp of GDP growth drag with a 3-year lookback; NFCI in standard-deviation units (not discussed by C-S).
- Time-horizon: FCI-G cumulates 3 years of past changes (baseline); C-S use it as a level; the "FCI-star" companion (Caballero et al. 2025) estimates an FCI analogue of r-star (FCI-gap is related to output gaps), not read.
- C-S use quarterly FCI-G, 1990-2019 (VAR); the replication note should be aware the baseline-versus-one-year-lookback choice matters ("Using the 1-year version does not alter the results", FCT p. 68).

## 3. Ten-line summary for the note
1. BKM define neoclassical channels (user-cost/interest rate with long rates, Tobin's q and wealth through stocks and house prices, intertemporal substitution, exchange rate) and non-neoclassical credit channels (Reg Q, bank lending/capital, balance sheet via external finance premium); quote abstract p. ii and p. 53 for "core channels".
2. BKM do not hand us a variable list for a stance index: long rates, equities, exchange rate (and house prices) follow from their channels; corporate yields/spreads sit in their CREDIT block (pp. 40-41), so "corporate rates under rates" is the note's choice, to be flagged (and justified by the user-cost link to private borrowing costs, not by BKM).
3. BKM say the exchange rate is a core channel in models but of small net importance for the US (fn. 13, p. 23); this bears on including the dollar and on any weight comparison with FCI-G.
4. BKM's opening (p. 1) is a ready-made justification: the funds rate can be low while policy is restrictive through other asset prices and quantities.
5. Time variation: the output and price responses to policy shocks fell after 1980s and BKM attribute it to policy behaviour/expectations, not private-sector behaviour; they provide no evidence on stability of financial-market responses (no high-frequency data, sample ends 2008), so keep the "stable weights" assumption explicit.
6. Caballero-Caravello-Simsek and Caballero-Simsek (2026) use the Fed's FCI-G (baseline, 3-year lookback, quarterly, 1990Q1+); NFCI only to splice 1973-1990 and as a claim of similarity; GS FCI not used; the AER 2022 paper uses no FCI (interest-rate based, with forward-rate and Greenbook/Blue Chip data).
7. None of the three papers converts FCI-G into funds-rate units or gives a per-100bp elasticity (only the stock weight, about -0.02 FCI-G units per 1% equity move, FCT p. 16); so item (c) of the note's contribution (funds-rate version of FCI-G) is not pre-empted by them. Figure 18 of FCT (policy-shock IRF of the FCI) not inspected.
8. Their central claim: transmission runs via financial conditions dominated by risky-asset prices (stocks, then the dollar, sometimes housing), rates are an incomplete map to FCI, and a noise component (up to 20% of variance) is only gradually offset by policy.
9. Hence a funds-rate equivalent is not a stance measure; their results support the plan's source-agnostic "tightening equivalent" wording and the market-based lens that includes equities and the dollar (cite Bernanke-Kuttner 2005, Boehm-Kroner 2024 via C-S p. 9; Nagel-Xu 2024 as dissent).
10. Caveats on my reading: BKM read in FEDS form (not the Handbook chapter); FCI-plot read in the March 2026 MIT-hosted version; FCT in the Sep 2026 version; all other C-S papers (FCI-star, multi-asset) unread.
