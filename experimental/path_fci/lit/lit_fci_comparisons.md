## 1. Arnaut and Bauer (2024), FRBSF Economic Letter 2024-07 (4 Mar 2024) -- READ
URL: https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2024/03/monetary-policy-and-financial-conditions/ (PDF link on page, 269 KB)

**Indexes compared and how.** Four, shown in Figure 1 over 1 Nov 2021 - 31 Dec 2023: Chicago Fed NFCI ("a weighted average of 105 different measures ... in money, debt, and equity markets and the banking system"), Bloomberg FCI ("averages eight indicators"), Goldman Sachs FCI ("a dynamic macroeconomic model to determine the relative weights of five underlying indicators: a policy rate, a long-term riskless bond yield, a corporate credit spread, a measure of equity valuations, and a trade-weighted exchange rate"), and the Board's FCI-G (Ajello et al. 2023; seven indicators weighted by effect on output growth in FRB/US; "considers past financial market changes instead of only current market conditions"). Comparison is a single overlay chart plus text. No correlations, no regressions across indexes, no by-group comparison across indexes.

**Common unit?** No funds-rate (or any) common unit. Only a sign/level alignment (Fig. 1 note): "To make the series comparable, the Bloomberg FCI was multiplied by -1, and 100 was subtracted from the Goldman Sachs FCI." Frequencies differ and are left as is: "The Bloomberg and Goldman Sachs series are daily, the Chicago Fed index is weekly, and the Board's FCI-G is monthly." Zero "corresponds to the average value of each index over a specific historical period, which is not necessarily a level that is 'neutral'." (So units are the indexes' own: standard-deviation-type for NFCI, GS index points, FCI-G in growth-impulse pp.) The only funds-rate link is verbal: the policy rate is compared with a hypothetical neutral rate in the intro, and they note GS's funds-rate contribution.

**Decomposition (Fig. 2, GS FCI only).** Contributions of the five GS components over two windows. Quote: "During the financial tightening from November 2021 to November 2022 ..., the substantial increase in the federal funds rate made only a small contribution. According to the Goldman Sachs model and consistent with empirical research, this is because short-term rates are not as relevant for economic activity. Instead, financial conditions tightened mainly because of higher long-term interest rates, lower stock prices, and a stronger dollar." Nov 2022 - Dec 2023: "about one-third of the earlier tightening in this FCI was reversed due to higher stock prices, a weaker dollar, and narrower credit spreads." The bar values are in an image; I did not see them. The decomposition is by variable for one index, not across indexes.

**Event-study evidence.** "we follow a similar methodology as in Bauer and Swanson (2023) and measure the surprise component of monetary policy actions using 30-minute changes in various market interest rates around the time of the announcements. Then we estimate the effects on financial conditions using event-study regressions, relating monetary policy surprises to changes in FCIs on the day of the announcement and subsequent days." Applied only to the GS FCI (Fig. 3): "a policy surprise of 0.1 percentage point leads to an increase in the FCI by about 0.07 index points on the day of the announcement and further increases over the following two weeks." Figure note: "Response to a hypothetical surprise monetary tightening of 1 percentage point. Blue shading represents a 95% confidence band." Second event study (Fig. 4): rolling 24-month regressions of GS FCI on core CPI surprises (actual minus Bloomberg consensus); "the sensitivity of financial conditions to core inflation surprises is currently at its highest level in over two decades."
- Candid notes: (i) the letter does not say which surprise measure/instrument (which of the Bauer-Swanson rates), the sample dates, number of FOMC events, or the units of "index points" in terms of standard deviations or funds rate. (ii) 0.1pp -> 0.07 points reads as 0.7 points per 1pp surprise, but that is my arithmetic, and whether it is comparable to the 1pp figure note is not reconciled in the text. (iii) Only GS is event-studied; NFCI, Bloomberg and FCI-G are not (they cite Sack and Swanson 2023 for FCI-G).

**Main findings (with numbers).** Conditions tightened before the March 2022 hike and through 2022; eased on net after late 2022 "despite the FOMC raising its policy rate more than 2 percentage points". Monetary policy surprises move GS FCI significantly and persistently (0.07 points per 10 bp). Inflation-surprise sensitivity at a 20+ year high. Conclusion: "monetary policy and macroeconomic data are clearly two important and interrelated drivers of financial market conditions."

**Limitations noted by the authors.** "Correlations between time series, for example between the policy rate and an FCI, are not particularly useful for understanding this relationship because they do not reveal causal effects." "during other episodes, it is less clear how changes in financial conditions may have been related to monetary policy--for example, during the tightening of financial conditions in fall 2023." "the complexity of financial markets makes it difficult to pinpoint the exact determinants of observed changes in FCIs." Zero is not neutral. (Short Economic Letter format; no robustness reported.)

**References (all six):** Ajello et al. 2023 (FEDS Notes); Bauer, Bernanke, Milstein 2023 (JEP 37(1)); Bauer, Pflueger, Sunderam 2023 (FRBSF WP 2023-31); Bauer and Swanson 2023 (NBER Macro Annual 37, 87-155); Daly 2023 speech (Economic Club of NY, 5 Oct); Sack and Swanson 2023 (Brookings, 18 Oct).

**Overlap with the planned note.**
- Overlap (substantial on the question, small on method): same motivation (FCIs diverge from the policy rate; compare the 4 big indexes, incl. FCI-G, GS, NFCI); same idea that asset-price responses to high-frequency surprises tell how policy feeds into FCIs; same observation that GS gives the funds rate a small weight; same HFI toolkit (Bauer-Swanson). Same episode (2021-23 tightening and 2023 easing) that the planned note would examine.
- NOT done there (so remains new): any funds-rate-equivalent or other common unit (only sign flip/level shift); levels/changes correlations and slopes; by-group comparison across indexes; episode taxonomy of divergence; NFCI/Bloomberg/FCI-G event studies; a market-based alternative index; model-implied vs event-window financial-block comparison; replication of GS. Their event study answers "does policy move the index?", the plan asks "what policy move is the index equal to, and how do indexes differ in that unit?" That is a different object.
- Risk: moderate on framing (a referee could say "Arnaut-Bauer already compared them and linked them to surprises"), low on substance. Cite and position as the event-study precursor; also use their 0.07 per 0.1pp as a cross-check on the GS replication (after confirming surprise measure and units with the authors or the source data).

## 2. Aramonte, Rosen, Schindler (2013), "Assessing and Combining Financial Conditions Indexes," FEDS 2013-39 (30 May 2013) -- READ
URL (PDF, fetched): https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2013/201339/201339pap.pdf (series page: https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2013/201339/)

**Indexes compared.** Twelve US FCIs/stress indexes with monthly-or-higher frequency and history covering the late 1990s (Table 1): Bloomberg FCI and FCI+ (daily, weighted avg., from 1994), Cleveland FSI (daily WA), Morgan Stanley FCI (daily WA, from 1995, source Bloomberg, "N/A" year established), Carlson-Lewis-Nelson FSI (weekly, FPC), Chicago Fed NFCI and ANFCI (weekly, FPC, from 1973), St. Louis Fed FSI (weekly FPC), Kansas City FSI (monthly FPC), Citi FCI (monthly WA), IMF FCI (monthly dynamic factor model; Matheson), IMF FSI (monthly WA). No Goldman Sachs FCI, no FCI-G (it did not exist).

**Common unit?** Yes but not a policy-rate unit: "All the indexes are expressed in terms of z-scores, with the exception of the Financial Stress Index of Carlson, Lewis, and Nelson (2012), which is expressed in terms of probabilities." No funds-rate equivalence; the paper never relates indexes to the policy rate. Stress and conditions indexes are mixed together (stress indexes are explicitly in scope), which matters for the plan's stress-versus-stance contrast.

**Evidence and methods (no high-frequency / event-study evidence at all).**
1. Pairwise correlations (Table 3, monthly, Jul 1995 - Jun 2012): "Pairwise correlations among the FCIs (see Table 3) range between 22% and 96%, with the majority being above 70%." Lowest: MS FCI with ANFCI 22.1, with CFSI 32.9, with NFCI 41.3; NFCI-KCFSI 95.7; NFCI-BFCI 93.8; NFCI-STLFSI 91.8; NFCI-ANFCI 67.9.
2. Summary stats (Table 2): all persistent; "in 5 cases, we are actually unable to reject the hypothesis that the FCIs have a unit root"; Sept 2008 value in worst 10% of the 1995-2012 sample for most, except MS FCI ("mainly deteriorated into October 2008").
3. Predictive regressions (levels of FCI on 1-month/1-quarter-ahead S&P 500, seven industry portfolios, residuals of six macro series), Campbell-Yogo local-to-unity inference. Finding: predictability only with the 2008 crisis in the sample. Quotes: "We find reliable predictability only when the sample includes the 2008 financial crisis, and we argue that this result is driven by tailoring the indexes to the crisis and by non-synchronous trading." Counts: 11 of 12 predict finance-portfolio monthly returns and 4 the S&P 500 in 1995-2012 (Table 5); skipping the first day of the month, 5 of 12 for finance (Table 6); for 1995-2006 "essentially, there is no predictability left" (Table 7). Conclusion: "the empirical evidence in favor of the FCIs having reliable predictive power is weak".
4. Combining (Section 3): rank each index i by adjusted R2 of robust regression of changes in the first PC of the other 11 on changes in index i (Table 14). Full sample 1995-2012: STLFSI 75.6, NFCI 65.1, BFCI+ 58.4, BFCI 57.0, Citi 44.6, KCFSI 42.2; lowest MS FCI 21.3, CFSI 24.7, IMF FCI 29.4. Then all 31 combinations of top five; best (lowest average squared deviation, Table 15) is first PC of STLFSI, BFCI, NFCI, Citi FCI ("Composite FCI"); it "follows the general pattern of the individual FCIs, but its volatility exhibits different regimes depending on whether financial conditions are loose or tight." Correlation of CFCI changes with VIX changes about 80-90% since Aug 2008.

**Aggregation observations useful to the plan.** Weights "typically assigned subjectively by the authors, although a few of the indexes use more sophisticated methods"; "the large number of FCIs is itself indicative of the uncertainty that surrounds the measurement of financial conditions, and aggregating the individual indexes can help average out model uncertainty." They assume "none of the indexes is likely to contain uniquely accurate information about financial conditions", which matches the plan's "no ground truth, not ranking" stance. Level-vs-change: they keep levels in the predictive regressions because "the authors uniformly emphasize that their FCIs are ordinal measures" but use changes to combine/compare (same split as the plan's levels-base, changes-check). Non-synchronous VIX timing is a data caution relevant to daily-frequency work.

**Limitations they note.** Single crisis in sample so threshold vs. tailoring cannot be separated ("It is difficult to empirically assess the merit of (1) and (2) above, not least because only one major financial crisis is included in the sample"); the R2 criterion is "one of potentially many"; a low R2 could mean a better measure (judged unlikely given variable overlap); robust regression; z-scores ordinal.

**Overlap with the planned note.** Overlap: multi-index comparison, MS FCI, NFCI/ANFCI, "understanding how they relate" via correlations and a principal component. Not overlapping: no policy-rate or other economic unit (z-scores only), no model-based index (FCI-G, GS), no variable-group or episode analysis, no event-study, no market-based construction, pre-2013 sample. The planned note's by-group and funds-rate-unit comparison is new relative to it. The PCA/consensus idea is the alternative "average the indexes" approach; cite it, and perhaps report the first PC of the converted indexes as a contrast. Novelty risk low. Note: ARP was a Fed Board staff paper; a Board-based reader will know it.

## 4. Summary (10 lines)
1. Arnaut-Bauer (2024) is the nearest work: overlays NFCI, Bloomberg, GS, FCI-G (Nov 2021-Dec 2023) and event-studies only the GS FCI on 30-min surprises (0.1 pp surprise -> about 0.07 index points).
2. It has no common unit: only Bloomberg x(-1) and GS minus 100; frequencies differ; zero is "not necessarily neutral".
3. Its GS decomposition shows the funds rate contributed little to the 2021-22 tightening; long yields, equities, dollar did the work.
4. It states correlations with the policy rate are not informative and uses event studies; no sample dates, N, surprise definition or units for "index points" are given.
5. ARP (2013) compares 12 FCIs/stress indexes (z-scores, 1995-2012): correlations 22-96%, mostly >70%; MS FCI is the outlier (22-47% with most).
6. ARP finds predictive power only if 2008 is in the sample and offers a first-PC composite (STLFSI, BFCI, NFCI, Citi); no event study, no policy-rate unit, no GS or FCI-G.
7. Sack-Swanson (Brookings 2023) is the closest FCI-G quantification: 25 bp policy-induced 2-year rise -> about 20 bp FCI-G on impact, about 33 bp at peak (read via summary); 2-year-yield units, not funds-rate.
8. Not found in anything read: funds-rate-equivalent FCIs, by-group comparison across indexes, or episode-based divergence analysis.
9. Ideas to adopt: ARP's levels-vs-changes split and "no index is uniquely accurate" stance; AB's surprise windows as a cross-check on the GS replication.
10. Open checks: fetch the PIMCO/Advisor Perspectives piece; confirm AB's surprise measure; read Sack-Swanson raw text; Bauer-Swanson (2023) for the surprise instrument.

## 5. Verdict on novelty risk
Novelty risk is low-to-moderate. The question ("how do the major FCIs relate, and what do they say about policy") is plainly in the territory of Arnaut-Bauer and, for older indexes, Aramonte-Rosen-Schindler, so a note must cite both and not pitch itself as the first comparison of FCIs. What neither does, as far as I could read, is express the indexes in a common policy-rate unit, compare them by variable group and episode, or confront the model-based weights with event-window responses; Sack-Swanson comes closest on the FCI-G side but in 2-year-yield units, and a practitioner piece (PIMCO via Advisor Perspectives) may be translating FCI-G into asset-price equivalents, which I could not read. The main exposure is therefore on the "funds-rate equivalent" selling point: if Goldman, a bank research desk or the Fed has circulated such conversions in unpublished or paywalled material (the 2015 GS "145 bp per 100 bp" line in the plan is secondhand), the contribution narrows to the by-group/episode comparison and the market-based alternative. Position the note as the extension of Arnaut-Bauer's event study to all four indexes in a common unit, and run a last targeted search for sell-side or Fed-staff conversions before drafting.
