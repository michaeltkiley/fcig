# Literature follow-up: funds-rate-equivalent conversions of FCIs; Sack-Swanson; Bauer-Swanson; CFL 498; Brave-Butters
Compiled 22 Oct 2026 (web search + fetched pages/PDFs, text extracted locally). Status flags: READ = full text of the relevant page/PDF obtained and the passage read; SNIPPET = search-result summary only; UNVERIFIED = not seen. Figures/charts are images and were NOT seen in any source unless stated; page numbers are not available for web pages.

## 0. Verdict (short)
No source found that converts FCI-G, NFCI/ANFCI or the GS FCI into federal funds rate equivalents AND compares several indexes in that common unit. Partial precedents exist, none doing the comparison (section 1). The single-index conversions that do exist are: (a) Goldman 2015 (GS FCI, 145 bp of index per 100 bp of funds rate; secondhand via Bloomberg/Business Standard, READ), (b) the SF Fed proxy funds rate and Morgan Stanley's 2026 index built on it (a different construct: PCA of 12 rates/spreads mapped to funds-rate levels), (c) Sack-Swanson (2023): FCI-G response to policy-induced 2-year-yield changes (inverse of the direction needed; units of the 2-year yield, not the funds rate), (d) PIMCO (30 Sep 2026): FCI-G gap expressed as 10-year-yield / equity / dollar equivalents (not funds rate). A pointer for the note: Sack-Swanson's last paragraph explicitly says their exercise "could be used to determine the policy response ... necessary to offset" a tightening of the FCI, i.e. they anticipate the conversion but do not do it.

## 1. Search for existing conversions into funds-rate / policy-rate equivalents

### 1.1 PIMCO, "Monetary Policy Through the Lens of Financial Conditions" (Macro Signposts) -- READ (PIMCO original; Advisor Perspectives copy not obtainable)
- URL: https://www.pimco.com/gbl/en/insights/monetary-policy-through-the-lens-of-financial-conditions (also /us/ and /lat/ variants). Authors Tiffany Wilding, J. R. Scott; PIMCO page dated 9/30/2026. Advisor Perspectives lists it (firm page https://www.advisorperspectives.com/firm/pimco, snippet dates 9/24, 10/2 and 10/8/26 with differing co-authors in different listings; the AP site is behind a Cloudflare check, so I could not fetch it). Cite the PIMCO page, 30 Sep 2026.
- What it does (text read in full): describes FCI-G (7 variables: fed funds rate, 10-year Treasury, 30-year mortgage rate, BBB yield, stocks, house prices, broad dollar; +1 = subtracts 1 pp from GDP growth over the following year); latest reading 2Q 2026 = -0.9 (a tailwind of 0.9 pp). Then uses the Caballero-Caravello-Simsek "FCI-star" method to get a target FCI-G consistent with closing the output gap (output estimated 1 to 1.5 pp above potential): "financial conditions were around 50 basis points (bps) too easy at the end of 2Q". Conversion: "the estimated realignment in financial conditions would be equivalent to a 70-bp increase in the 10-year Treasury yield (that passes through one-for-one to mortgage rates and corporate yields), a 9% decline in equities, a 4% increase in the broad trade-weighted U.S. dollar, or some combination", "not forecasts, but ... illustrative equivalents". Notes the 10-year rose about 80 bp since June. Figure 3 projects FCI-G from futures curves. Policy-rate content: it says the Fed need not rely only on hikes; FCI-G is explicitly NOT converted to a funds-rate equivalent.
- Units: FCI-G pp of growth (the 50 bp "gap" is, I infer, 0.5 pp of FCI-G; the text says "basis points"); equivalents are 10-year yield bp, % equity, % dollar. So: a variable-by-variable equivalent using FCI-G weights, same logic as a funds-rate equivalent but with the long rate as numeraire. Closest recent sell-side analogue to the note's idea; shows that the multiplier approach is being used in practice.

### 1.2 Goldman Sachs: "145 bp per 100 bp" and "three hikes" -- READ (secondhand, Bloomberg article via Business Standard); original GS report UNVERIFIED
- URL: https://www.business-standard.com/article/international/goldman-says-tighter-conditions-after-sell-off-equate-to-three-fed-hikes-115091200780_1.html (Bloomberg, 12 Sep 2015). Text: tightening "roughly equivalent to three 25 basis-point hikes in the ... federal funds rate". "The Goldman report presents six estimates of the historical relationship between changes in the firm's financial conditions index and the fed funds rate using the workhorse computer simulation employed by Fed staff ... and a variety of standard statistical methods. On average, a 145 basis-point change in the index equated to a 100 basis-point change in the interest rate." Uncertainty "very large" (Sven Jari Stehn). The ratio is index change in bp (GS FCI points) per bp of funds rate, i.e. 1.45 index points per 1 pp... as reported ("145 basis-point change in the index equated to a 100 basis-point change in the interest rate"); the article does not state the index change behind "three hikes", so I do not back out numbers. Single index (GS), historical regression/FRB-US ratio, not a cross-index comparison. Same article notes other regional-Fed measures "point to a tightening ... to a lesser degree" and that the dollar weight is a key difference (qualitative cross-index remark only).
- The other index weights come from Hatzius et al. (2010), p. 4 of the USMPF paper: "FRB/US ... together with Goldman Sachs modeling, were used to determine the weights" (READ).

### 1.3 Morgan Stanley (2026) -- READ (Morgan Stanley's own web article); research report UNVERIFIED
- URL: https://www.morganstanley.com/insights/articles/fed-rate-pause-fixed-income-market-impact-2026 ("Watch Out for a Fed Pause", 17 Jul 2026). Text: Martin Tobias and team "developed a financial conditions index modeled on a framework used by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco. The index incorporates 12 market variables--including Treasury yields, mortgage rates and corporate borrowing spreads--to estimate the effective level of monetary policy implied by financial conditions. Since the start of the Iran conflict, the index suggests financial conditions have tightened by an amount equivalent to four 25-basis-point rate increases." The phrase "policy rate equivalent" itself does not appear in the article (it was my brief's wording). Unit: funds-rate pp, by construction (SF Fed proxy-funds-rate method: PCA of 12 variables mapped to funds-rate levels using pre-2008 relationship). Single index; not a cross-index comparison; no FCI-G/NFCI/GS comparison.

### 1.4 San Francisco Fed proxy funds rate -- READ (page and Economic Letter)
- https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/data-and-indicators/proxy-funds-rate/ (updated 20 Oct 2026) and Choi, Doh, Foerster, Martinez (2022), "Monetary Policy Stance Is Tighter than Federal Funds Rate," FRBSF Economic Letter 2022-30: https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2022/11/monetary-policy-stance-is-tighter-than-federal-funds-rate/ . 12 financial variables, first three principal components "mapped to levels of the federal funds rate, where the mapping reflects the pre-2008 correlations"; proxy was above 5 1/4% by Sep 2022 against a funds rate near 3%; interpreted as "what federal funds rate would typically be associated with prevailing financial market conditions if these conditions were driven solely by the funds rate"; caveat: stable pre/post-2008 mapping. Units: funds-rate levels. This is the only published funds-rate-unit financial-conditions measure from a Reserve Bank, but it is its own index, not a conversion of FCI-G/NFCI/GS. (Plan already has this; confirmed.)

### 1.5 Other partial precedents found
- Chicago Fed Letter 498 (Gillet-King, Aug 2024): regresses FCI-G and NFCI (and individual variables) on the funds-rate path in past tightening cycles (section 4 below); a funds-rate-conditional benchmark, not an equivalent. READ.
- CaixaBank Research "How financial conditions are withstanding tighter monetary policy: part II" (c. Mar 2018): https://www.caixabankresearch.com/en/economics-markets/monetary-policy/how-financial-conditions-are-withstanding-tighter-monetary-policy -- READ. Standardizes changes in NFCI and ANFCI "by the total variation in the interest rate in each cycle": Feb 1994-Feb 1995 funds rate +275 bp, NFCI +0.29 points = "around 10.5 bp for each 100-bp rise in the benchmark interest rate"; Jul 1999-May 2000 +150 bp, NFCI +0.19 = "around 12.9 bp for each 100-bp increase". (Units: NFCI s.d. points x100 per 100 bp of funds rate; i.e. about 0.10 to 0.13 NFCI points per pp; the NFCI vintage then differs from today's.) Apparently the only NFCI-per-funds-rate ratio in print that I found. It is a simple raw ratio over four cycles (NFCI and ANFCI), not an equivalence in the inverse direction and not compared across indexes. (The plan's earlier mention of this should be treated as READ.)
- Hatzius, Hooper, Mishkin, Schoenholtz, Watson (2010), USMPF paper (READ, https://www.princeton.edu/~mwatson/papers/USMPF-2010.pdf): survey of FCIs. Notes the OECD FCI is normalized so that one unit = GDP effect of a 1 pp rise in the real long-term rate; Deutsche Bank FCI = weighted PCA component plus target funds rate (index in pp of GDP drag/boost); Goldman FCI as above. Raises the question of "what scale of QE or CE is expected to be equivalent ... to a step-reduction of the conventional policy rate". So output-equivalent normalizations to a rate (OECD: real long rate) are standard in the older FCI literature but not the funds rate across indexes. Section 5 of the same paper (purged index) discusses the funds rate's role in the index (p. ~27-28 of the paper); no funds-rate-equivalent conversion.
- Chung, Laforte, Reifschneider, Williams (2011), FRBSF Economic Letter 2011-03 (READ, https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2011-03.pdf): asset purchases lowered the 10-year by about 1/2 pp; "it would take roughly a 2 percentage point cut in the federal funds rate to achieve an equivalent half percentage point drop in the 10-year Treasury yield, based on patterns since 1987"; the purchase program's output support would have required cutting the funds rate "approximately 3 percentage points" in other simulations. A funds-rate-equivalent for unconventional policy: precedent for the convention, not for FCIs.
- Garcia and Skaperdas, FEDS 2017-106 (SNIPPET, abstract): shadow rate from real activity, "equivalent short-term interest rate movements", LSAP/forward guidance up to 4 pp. Precedent for funds-rate-equivalents of non-funds-rate policy. Not read in full.
- Beaton, Lalonde, Luu (2009), BoC DP 2009-11 (already in plan, READ).
- Atlanta Fed macroblog, Gospodinov, 18 Aug 2026 (READ, https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/policy-hub-macroblog/2026/08/18/monetary-policy-stance-and-financial-conditions): plots EFFR against FCI-G excluding the policy-rate contribution (GS FCI "yields similar results"), builds a standardized index of policy rate (2/3) and reserves share (1/3); an MPI shock raises FCI-G by 1.34 s.d. at peak (16 months). Units standard deviations and 'FCI-G ex funds rate'; no funds-rate equivalent.
- NIESR blog (Oct 2026?; READ, https://niesr.ac.uk/blog/financial-conditions-and-monetary-policy): UK; gilt yields +100 bp (2y) and +75 bp (10y) since 27 Feb; qualitatively discusses offsetting exogenous tightening with Bank Rate and cites Caballero-Caravello-Simsek. No conversion.
- Northern Trust ("The Fed Might be Hiking Without Tightening", 2026; READ for hike terms), RSM, T. Rowe Price "Can the Fed cut rates with financial conditions this loose?" (2024; READ for conversion terms only; shows GS FCI and other index 'thermometers' against "550 bp of Fed hikes" without a unit conversion), Bloomberg 11 Oct 2026 (via Yahoo; READ for terms): "The more tightening markets price, the tighter financial conditions become" (T. Rowe's Orchard); market-implied hikes across eight economies 539 bp, down from 717 bp. None converts an FCI into funds-rate terms.
- BIS Bulletin 80 "Monetary policy, financial conditions and real activity: is this time different?" (SNIPPET + abstract lines read; https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull80.pdf): financial conditions co-moved closely with policy rates in the current episode; not a funds-rate equivalent of an index.
- Not found / UNVERIFIED: Bloomberg Economics (no piece found converting its FCI into hikes; searches returned only the index description); Goldman 2022-2026 updates of the 145 bp rule (not found; only 2015 version seen); JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank (no conversion seen); the original Morgan Stanley report; Engen-Laubach-Reifschneider (2015) FEDS (PDF opened; no explicit "funds rate equivalent" passage found by keyword search; do not cite for that).
- Advisor Perspectives hosts several of these (PIMCO; Northern Trust "Central Banks Shrink Their Footprints", 14 Oct 2026, UNVERIFIED) but could not be fetched (Cloudflare); PIMCO original used instead.

## 2. Sack and Swanson (2023), Brookings -- READ (complete web text)
- Correct title: Brian Sack and Eric Swanson, "The impact of Federal Reserve policy on the Fed's financial conditions index," Brookings Institution commentary, 18 Oct 2023. URL: https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-impact-of-federal-reserve-policy-on-the-feds-financial-conditions-index/ . It is a short web commentary (about 3 pages) with 3 figures and 4 footnotes, NOT a BPEA paper or "Financial conditions and monetary policy transmission"; no page numbers exist, so I cite by section/figure.
- Which FCI: the Federal Reserve Board's FCI (FCI-G, Ajello et al. 2023, FEDS Notes); the text says "the Fed's FCI" and "the Board's FCI", never writing "FCI-G". Seven variables listed (funds rate, 10-year, mortgage rate, BBB yield, broad stock index, house prices, nominal broad dollar), weighted by FRB/US; "accounts not only for the effects of current movements ... but also the effects of earlier changes"; monthly; "Readings above zero indicate the degree to which current and past changes in financial conditions would be expected to restrain growth over the subsequent year, measured in units of GDP growth." Footnote 1 names NFCI, Bloomberg and GS as other prominent FCIs (not used).
- Identification: a 2-variable monthly VAR (Fed FCI, 2-year Treasury yield, monthly lags). Instrument: monetary-policy-induced movements in the 2-year yield from the Swanson-Jayawickrema (2023) high-frequency dataset (narrow windows around FOMC statements, press conferences, minutes and speeches by the Chair and Vice Chair), summed over each month; "following Bauer and Swanson (2023)" use these as an instrument in the VAR (footnote 2; cites Stock-Watson, Gertler-Karadi, Ramey). Not USMPD by name (the Swanson-Jayawickrema data are the series on which the Bauer-Swanson paper is based; whether the instrument is orthogonalized to macro news is not stated in this post).
- Quantitative results: (Fig. 2) for a policy-induced rise in the 2-year yield of 25 bp, the FCI "initially tightens by 20 basis points ... The peak effect comes about a quarter after the policy news and is 33 basis points in magnitude"; symmetric by construction; the hump is partly due to index construction (lags in FRB/US). Summary line: "the FCI moves by 4/5 of any policy-induced shift in the two-year yield, with that effect then building to more than 1 after roughly a quarter" (the 33/25 = 1.3). Units: FCI in pp of GDP growth (called "basis points" in text), yield in bp: the ratio is dimensionally "pp of growth per pp of 2-year yield". (Fig. 3) Applying this to the 2021Q4 to 2023 rise in the 2-year yield (to just above 5%), the FCI would be predicted to rise "about 3.75 percentage points at its peak" against an actual peak increase of "2.5 percentage points"; the gap is attributed to the 2-year increase reflecting growth/inflation news (footnote 3: growth news tightens FCI less, inflation news a bit less as real rates move less).
- Relevance: (1) gives the direction FCI = f(2-year yield) -- inverse gives a 2-year-yield equivalent of FCI-G (1 unit of FCI-G is equal to 1.25 pp of policy-induced 2-year yield on impact, or about 0.76 pp at the peak: my arithmetic from 20/25 and 33/25); (2) the closing paragraph: "Our exercise could be used to determine the policy response (or the policy-induced change in the two-year yield) that would be necessary to offset that tightening" -- the closest published statement of the note's idea, for the Board's FCI only; (3) the funds rate is one of the FCI's seven components so the 2-year yield is not an exogenous regressor in the index sense.
- Caveats: short piece, no standard errors in the text (Fig. 2 has bands I did not see), no sample dates, no sample size, no robustness; a single policy-induced yield measure.

## 3. Bauer and Swanson (2023), "A Reassessment of Monetary Policy Surprises and High-Frequency Identification," NBER Macroeconomics Annual 37 -- READ (NBER WP 29939, 67 pp, April 2022 version; full text; page numbers in the published volume pp. 87-155 are not matched to my text, so I cite sections)
URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29939 ; PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29939/w29939.pdf . One paragraph: The paper takes the (Swanson-Jayawickrema 2021) high-frequency monetary policy surprises (30-minute changes in interest-rate futures/yields around FOMC announcements and, newly, Fed Chair speeches, 322 FOMC announcements 1988-2019, which "doubles the number and importance of announcements") and addresses two concerns about their use as instruments. First, the surprises are predictable from macro and financial data that pre-date the announcement (Section 3, Table 1: R-squared about 16 percent on the full sample, predictors include payrolls, a commodity index, yield curve slope, stock prices, etc.; Blue Chip and Greenbook forecasts contain the same information), which they interpret via a "Fed response to news" channel (private sector underestimating how responsive the Fed is to the economy) rather than a Fed information effect; hence they orthogonalize: mps-perp = mps minus the fitted value from a regression on pre-announcement data (equation 17, Section 4.2). Second, results: (i) high-frequency asset-price responses to the surprises are largely unchanged by orthogonalization (consistent with their model; orthogonalizing is not required, and may reduce efficiency, for event-study regressions, fn. 9); (ii) macroeconomic effects in SVARs/local projections are substantially larger and more significant with the orthogonalized instrument (Section 5), and price/output/"excess bond premium" puzzles disappear, because the unadjusted surprise is endogenous; (iii) because the correction uses only public data, they do not support Fed information effects (Romer-Romer, Nakamura-Steinsson, Miranda-Agrippino-Ricco) as an important confounder. For the note: event-study (same-day) use of the unadjusted USMPD-style surprise is fine for financial-market responses (that is the use in Arnaut-Bauer and the note); orthogonalization matters for low-frequency/VAR/local-projection use, and the instrument's relevance drops after orthogonalization (first-stage F falls; Section 5). Caveat: this paper's data are the Swanson-Jayawickrema series; the USMPD database (Bauer-Swanson) is the later public version, whose exact construction I did not check here.

## 4. Chicago Fed Letter 498 (Aug 2024) -- READ (full web text; figures are images and not seen)
- Gillet and King, "How Do Recent Financial Conditions Compare to Previous Episodes of Monetary Tightening?" Chicago Fed Letter No. 498, Aug 2024. https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2024/498 (PDF /-/media/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2024/cfl498.pdf).
- What it does: identifies 14 tightening cycles since 1954 (average 4.0 pp, 23 months); for each financial variable (10-year, 30-year mortgage, SLOOS C&I standards, loan growth, S&P 500, Case-Shiller, AAA and BAA spreads) regresses changes on current and lagged funds-rate changes (equation with Δ1, Δ3, Δ4, Δ8 lags; Bai-Perron break), projects the path implied by the observed 2022-24 funds-rate path, and tests whether actual values lie outside 95% bands. Findings: long rates, lending standards, loan growth tighter than predicted; equities and house prices about as predicted; credit spreads much narrower than predicted (AAA -50 bp, BAA -53 bp); "little evidence" of weak pass-through. Explicitly "not causal"; conditional only on the funds rate path (expectations omitted).
- FCI comparison (Figure 5, section "Results from financial conditions indexes"): the same regression applied to FCI-G (available from 1990) and NFCI (from 1971): "The FCI-G indicates that financial conditions have been considerably tighter than the model-based expectations over most of the post-2021 period, while the Chicago NFCI indicates that they have been much looser." Explanations: (1) FCI-G weights long-term rates heavily (above predicted); NFCI puts little weight on risk-free rate levels but much on corporate credit spreads and short-term funding spreads (NFCI "risk" and "credit" subindexes show large gaps; "leverage" subindex, with more weight on long Treasury rates, is in line); (2) sample differences: NFCI is more volatile in 1970s-80s; on the FCI-G sample (post-1990) the NFCI is closer to the model (orange line); (3) different purposes: FCI-G = restraint on real economy; NFCI = financial stability/stress. Footnote 10: conditioning on the future funds-rate path removes the remaining NFCI gap. Footnote 9: FCI-G weights from medium-run GDP effects.
- Quantification in a common unit: NO. Each index is compared with its own regression-based benchmark in its own units (FCI-G pp of growth; NFCI s.d.); the text gives no numerical gaps for the indexes (maybe in the figure; not seen), and nothing is converted into funds-rate equivalents. It is the reverse mapping (index conditional on funds rate) and the unit is the index's own; the funds-rate path is the conditioning variable. Closest in spirit to the note's by-group diagnosis: it attributes the FCI-G/NFCI difference to rates vs credit spreads.
- Also read: Chicago Fed Insights (Gillet and Brave, June 2024), "What Does the NFCI Tell Us About Future Economic Growth?" https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-insights/2024/nfci-future-economic-growth (READ): cross-correlations of NFCI (weekly, last week of month) with the monthly FCI-G, 1990-2024 and as-released (ALFRED) 2011-24; NFCI leads FCI-G by about 1-2 months (Pearson, rank correlations; weaker for as-released); renormalized NFCI (median/MAD scaling, linear transform). No common unit; states NFCI "lack of direct interpretability" in growth terms. Uses the same pair of indexes; relevant for the revision/real-time point (NFCI full history revised weekly; example: on 1 Apr 2020 NFCI = 0.05 as-released but the threshold was in fact crossed on 13 Mar 2020).

## 5. Brave and Butters (2011, 2012) on the NFCI -- READ (2012 IJCB full PDF, 49 pp; 2011 Economic Perspectives PDF, text extracted, notes read)
- 2012: S. Brave and R. A. Butters, "Diagnosing the Financial System: Financial Conditions and Financial Stress," Int. J. Central Banking 8(2), June 2012, pp. 191-239. https://www.ijcb.org/sites/default/files/journal/v8n2/ijcb-v8n2-diagnosing-financial-system-financial-conditions-and-financial-stress.pdf . Nothing on how the NFCI maps to policy-rate changes. The only policy statement: footnote 13 (p. 229): "Brave and Genay (2011) provide some evidence that during the recent crisis, traditional and non-traditional monetary policy actions contributed to the recovery in financial conditions as measured by the NFCI and ANFCI in a joint model of Federal Reserve policy and financial conditions." The funds rate level is not among the indicators (indicators are spreads such as Fed Funds/overnight repo spreads, 2Y/10Y swap-Treasury spreads, etc.; per the earlier note, rate levels have almost no weight).
- 2011: S. Brave and R. A. Butters, "Monitoring Financial Stability: A Financial Conditions Index Approach," Chicago Fed Economic Perspectives 35(1), 2011, pp. 22-43 (PDF https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/economic-perspectives/2011/1qtr2011-part2-brave-butters-pdf.pdf). Note 1 (p. 37): "Hatzius et al. (2010) also construct a similar version of their index of financial conditions and relate it to changes in the federal funds rate over time. We have found very similar results to theirs; our adjusted FCI is significantly correlated with measures of monetary policy, though we have not documented this here." Cites Brave and Genay (2011) for the relation of crisis-period monetary policy to the adjusted FCI. Quantitative NFCI-to-funds-rate relation: none in either paper; the only numeric NFCI-per-funds-rate ratios found elsewhere are the CaixaBank ones (1.5).
- Implication: the NFCI's creators claim only a statistically significant correlation with policy measures, undocumented; the note's conversion would be new for the NFCI.

## 6. Summary (10 lines)
1. No source converts FCI-G, NFCI/ANFCI or the GS FCI into federal funds rate equivalents and compares them; the note's core comparison appears to be new (search-based, not proof).
2. Closest single-index funds-rate conversions: Goldman 2015 (GS FCI: 145 bp of index per 100 bp of funds rate; "three hikes"; via Bloomberg, "very large" uncertainty), SF Fed proxy funds rate and Morgan Stanley 2026 (four 25 bp hikes since the Iran conflict; PCA of 12 variables mapped to funds-rate levels).
3. PIMCO (30 Sep 2026, READ at pimco.com; AP copy not fetchable) converts an FCI-G gap (50 bp, FCI-G units) into a 70 bp 10-year yield, 9% equity or 4% dollar equivalent: variable-by-variable equivalents with FCI-G weights, but not funds-rate units and only FCI-G.
4. Sack-Swanson (Brookings commentary, 18 Oct 2023, not a paper): Fed's FCI (FCI-G) rises 20 bp on impact and 33 bp at peak for a 25 bp policy-induced 2-year yield rise (HFI instrument, 2-variable VAR); their last paragraph proposes using it to find the policy response that offsets an FCI tightening, but they do not do it and use only the Board index.
5. Bauer-Swanson (2023): surprises are predictable from pre-announcement macro/financial data (R-squared about 16%); orthogonalize for VAR/local-projection work; event-study asset-price responses are unchanged; no strong Fed information effect.
6. Chicago Fed Letter 498 compares FCI-G and NFCI against regression-based benchmarks conditional on the funds-rate path; each in own units; the gap (FCI-G tight, NFCI loose) is explained by weights (long rates vs credit/funding spreads), sample (NFCI back to 1971) and purpose; no common unit, no funds-rate equivalent.
7. Chicago Fed Insights (June 2024) shows the NFCI leads the FCI-G by 1-2 months in correlation terms; revisions and release lags matter for real-time comparison.
8. Brave-Butters (2011, 2012) contain no NFCI-funds-rate mapping, only a statement of an undocumented significant correlation (2011 note 1, p. 37) and a pointer to Brave-Genay (2011); CaixaBank (2018) gives the only NFCI-per-funds-rate ratios seen (about 0.10 to 0.13 NFCI points per pp in 1994-95 and 1999-2000 tightenings).
9. Precedents for the funds-rate-equivalent convention for other policies: Chung et al. 2011 (LSAP equal to about 3 pp funds-rate cut; a 2 pp funds-rate cut needed for 1/2 pp lower 10-year), Garcia-Skaperdas 2018 (abstract only), Beaton-Lalonde-Luu 2009.
10. Verdict: no existing source already does a funds-rate-equivalent comparison across indexes. Single-index or different-unit pieces (Goldman, Morgan Stanley/SF Fed, PIMCO, Sack-Swanson, CFL 498) should be cited as precedents and contrasted; the Goldman 145 bp ratio and the Sack-Swanson 20/33 bp-per-25 bp response are useful cross-checks for the note's GS and FCI-G conversions (note the three use different instruments, units and horizons).
