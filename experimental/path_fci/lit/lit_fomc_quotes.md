# Verified official quotes on financial conditions (indexes) in the policy context

Compiled 22 Oct 2026 for the note in `NOTE_PLAN.md` (work item 2). No other file was modified; nothing committed.

## How verification was done
Each source below was downloaded in full (federalreserve.gov, newyorkfed.org, chicagofed.org, stlouisfed.org, dallasfed.org), converted to text (HTML stripped, PDFs via pdftotext), and each sentence quoted here was copied programmatically from that text, not from search snippets. Speaker attributions for FOMC transcripts come from the speaker label immediately preceding the passage. Minor edits: page headers/footnote numbers removed; "[page break]" marks where a transcript page header interrupted a sentence. Search-engine summaries were used only to find documents. Dates are meeting/speech dates; for the three Monetary Policy Reports and the two Chicago Fed pieces and the St. Louis Fed article only the month was captured.

All items in the main list are VERIFIED. Items I could not open and read are in the last section and are excluded.

Labels: NAMED = a specific index (FCI-G, Goldman Sachs, Chicago Fed/NFCI, Bloomberg, St. Louis, KC Fed) is named or clearly identified; GENERAL = "financial conditions indexes" without a name, or "financial conditions" used as a policy concept.

## Key findings in one view
- **Indexes named in FOMC/Fed sources**: Goldman Sachs FCI (Dudley 2010; transcripts June 2017, Sept 2017, Jan 2018, May 2018), Chicago Fed (transcripts May 2013, Jan 2018), Bloomberg (May 2018), St. Louis and KC Fed (2015, 2017, 2018), FCI-G (minutes Jan 2024; Kugler 2024, 2025; Cook 2025). NFCI is named in a Fed source only in Chicago Fed publications (2024); I found no FOMC minutes sentence naming "NFCI" or "Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index".
- **The FOMC minutes (2022-2026) never name Goldman Sachs or NFCI**; they use "financial conditions indexes" (May 2022, Mar 2023, Jun 2023, Sep 2023) and the staff FCI-G (Jan 2024).
- **Different signals / which index**: Powell 20 Mar 2024 ("many different financial conditions indicators ... different answers"); Chicago Fed Letter Aug 2024 (FCI-G tighter than model, NFCI much looser; weights explain); minutes Jan 2024 (broad indicators eased, FCI-G restrictive); Yellen Apr 2010 (FCIs slightly positive vs negative real funds rate); Dudley 2017 (policy rate and financial conditions diverge); Bullard transcripts 2017 (index weights questioned; indexes highly correlated); Potter May 2018 (Bloomberg includes money-market spreads, GS does not); Bullard 2023 article (many indexes, different components and weights).

### A1. Dudley (NY Fed President), speech, "Comments on Financial Conditions Indexes: A New Look after the Financial Crisis", Chicago Booth US Monetary Policy Forum (2010-02-26)
- URL: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/speeches/2010/dud100226
- Index usage: NAMED: Goldman Sachs FCI (GSFCI); FCIs generally
- Status: VERIFIED
> After all, I have long been a proponent of using a financial conditions framework to think about the economic outlook and monetary policy. When I was working as the chief U.S. economist at Goldman Sachs, we built one of the earliest financial conditions indexes, the Goldman Sachs Financial Conditions Index (GSFCI).

> Most important, monetary policy works its magic through its effect on financial conditions; it does not operate directly on real economic variables.

> In particular, most financial conditions indexes are ad hoc and incomplete.

> The endogenous components of financial conditions should be removed in the construction of a financial conditions index.

> I think FCIs have value as heuristic devices in highlighting what is important.

> Third, as the authors note, the resulting FCI is complex; it is difficult to estimate, update and to communicate what the results mean.

Note: Dudley both names the GS index (which he co-built) and critiques FCIs in general. Direct support for "which index" and communication complexity.

### A23. Dudley (NY Fed President), speech, "The Importance of Financial Conditions in the Conduct of Monetary Policy" (2017-03-30)
- URL: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/speeches/2017/dud170330
- Index usage: GENERAL + footnote to GS index (Dudley-Hatzius, 2000)
- Status: VERIFIED
> Nearly 20 years ago—when I was working in the private sector—my colleagues and I introduced an index of financial conditions.

> Focusing on the United States, financial conditions can be broadly summarized by five key measures: short-and long-term Treasury rates, credit spreads, the foreign exchange value of the dollar, and equity prices.

> In the conduct of monetary policy, the FOMC does not have targets for either specific components of financial conditions or for financial conditions more broadly.

> If financial conditions moved predictably with the policy rate, then there would be no need for the FOMC to focus on financial conditions. In such a world, by adjusting the level of the federal funds rate, the FOMC could be fully confident that it could generate the appropriate financial conditions necessary to support achievement of full employment and price stability.

> Moreover, at times, we have observed cases in which financial conditions did not move in the same direction as the monetary policy stance.

> Let me highlight two recent divergences which indicate that we need to keep a separate eye on the evolution of financial conditions.

Note: Key for the note: policy rate and financial conditions can move apart; Dudley (an FOMC member) says the FOMC must watch financial conditions separately.

### A33. Chicago Fed Letter No. 498, Gillet and King (Chicago Fed staff) (2024-08)
- URL: https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2024/498
- Index usage: NAMED: NFCI vs FCI-G, divergent signals
- Status: VERIFIED
> For this purpose, we turn to two measures of broad financial conditions, the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago’s National Financial Conditions Index (NFCI; see Brave and Butters, 2011 ), and the Federal Reserve Board’s Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth (FCI-G) Index ( Ajello et al., 2023 ).

> The FCI-G indicates that financial conditions have been considerably tighter than the model-based expectations over most of the post-2021 period, while the Chicago NFCI indicates that they have been much looser.

> The FCI-G index places a lot of weight on the levels of longer-term interest rates. As noted earlier, these have generally been above what historical experience would have predicted over this cycle. In contrast, the aggregate NFCI places relatively little weight on the level of risk-free rates but substantial weight on corporate credit spreads, as well as on highly correlated series, such as spreads in short-term funding markets.

> The FCI-G is meant to capture how financial conditions are likely to stimulate or restrain macroeconomic activity. The NFCI, on the other hand, is meant to speak more to financial stability concerns, so it places a greater emphasis on measures of risk aversion, fragility, and illiquidity in financial markets. Indeed, that index does well in predicting financial crises.

Note: Not an FOMC source, but a Fed source documenting that two Fed indexes give opposite readings and why (weights). Strong for the note.

## E. Strongest quotes for the note's motivation
1. **B22: Powell, 20 Mar 2024**: "there are many different financial conditions indicators, and you can kind of ... see different answers to that question." The Chair himself says indicators disagree. Best single motivation quote.
2. **A33: Chicago Fed Letter 498, Aug 2024**: FCI-G "considerably tighter than the model-based expectations ... while the Chicago NFCI indicates that they have been much looser", with the weights explanation. Documents the disagreement between two Fed indexes (not FOMC, but an official Fed publication).
3. **A28: FOMC minutes 31 Jan 2024**: "Indicators of broad financial conditions eased over the period, but the staff's Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth index remained restrictive." FCI-G named in minutes; shows divergence.
4. **A24: FOMC minutes 4 May 2022**: "financial conditions, as measured by many financial conditions indexes, had tightened by historically large amounts since the beginning of the year." FCIs used in the policy narrative at the start of the 2022 cycle.
5. **B19/B20: Powell Dec 2022 / Feb 2023**: "it is important that overall financial conditions continue to reflect the policy restraint that we're putting in place" - financial conditions as the transmission target, supporting a funds-rate-equivalent framing.
6. **A18: Laubach (staff), 31 Jan 2018**: FCIs from Goldman Sachs and the Chicago Fed "give the same message" and easing "would call for raising the federal funds rate at a somewhat faster pace" - the closest FOMC-room precedent for a funds-rate equivalent of an FCI.
7. **A1/A23: Dudley 2010 and 2017**: "monetary policy works its magic through its effect on financial conditions" (2010); policy rate and conditions can diverge (2017). Dudley co-built the GS FCI.
8. **A11/A13/A15: Bullard in 2017 transcripts**: questions the GS index weights; says the FCI may not signal what it did; notes high correlation across indexes.
9. **A32: Bullard (2023)**: many indexes with different components and weights; unclear that an index adds over components.
10. **A29-A31: Kugler 2024 and 2025, Cook 2025**: Governors use FCI-G by name to describe the stance; **B24/B25: Warsh 2026**: "hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive" - the Chair's stance judgment rests on financial conditions, not the funds rate.
