## 2. Hatzius et al. (2010): what it says (page numbers = USMPF report pages)
**Why FCIs (section 2.1, pp. 5-9).** An FCI "summarizes the information about the future state of the economy contained in these current financial variables." Ideally it measures *financial shocks*, exogenous shifts in conditions, distinguished from "the endogenous reflection ... of past economic activity" (p. 5, with footnote 1: purge the FCI of endogenous predictive content). Policy works by altering financial conditions (neoclassical and credit channels, drawing on Boivin-Kiley-Mishkin); but conditions are also hit by non-policy shocks, so an FCI "can serve as one guide to the effective stance of policy, after taking into account all the other factors," like a Taylor rule (p. 8). Examples: the 2004-06 "Greenspan conundrum" (conditions easy while rates rose) and late 2008 (extremely restrictive even at a zero funds rate). They acknowledge FCIs lack micro foundations and are "vulnerable to the Lucas critique," but argue reduced-form indexes are the practical tool, especially at the zero bound (pp. 8-9). Footnote 2 offers a taxonomy: financial shocks related to policy vs other; Section 5.2 shows most predictive power is *not* from the policy rate.

**Two families and two readings (section 2.3, pp. 9-13).** Construction: weighted-sum (weights from effects on GDP via large models, VARs or reduced-form demand equations) vs principal components (or PCA plus the policy rate). Reading: "Some FCIs can be interpreted as summarizing the impact of financial conditions on growth, others ... as measuring whether financial conditions have tightened or loosened" (p. 10). Examples that are growth-impact indexes with a growth-neutral zero: Deutsche Bank ("percentage point drag or boost to GDP," negative/positive), Macroeconomic Advisers (response functions inverted so the index "shows the combined effects of current and past changes ... on real GDP growth", 38 quarters of lags: an FCI-G precursor), OECD (one unit = GDP effect of a 1 pp rise in the real long rate). Levels-type: Bloomberg (z-score against its 1991-mid-2007 average), Kansas City (relative to long-run average), GS. "Neutral" appears in the text as "about neutral" for the historical average (p. 13 and 5.2).

**GS FCI (p. 11-12).** Weighted sum of a short-term bond yield, a long-term corporate yield, the exchange rate and a stock variable; "The FRB/US model, together with Goldman Sachs modeling, were used to determine the weights"; long yield = 10-year swap plus CDX since 2005, Moody's A before; set to 100 on 20 Oct 2003; "exhibits a noticeable downward trend because it uses levels." No stated zero or neutral point.

**Their new FCI (section 4, pp. 17-22).** 45 indicators, unbalanced-panel PCA. Each series transformed, standardized, regressed on current and two lags of real GDP growth and GDP-deflator inflation (eq. 2); the residual is factored. Footnote 13: "we measure financial conditions relative to the setting that would be typical at a particular stage of the business cycle" (a 250 bp Baa-Treasury spread can be restrictive in expansion and accommodative in recession). One-factor index weights proportional to the loadings. Funds rate deliberately excluded. Evaluated by predictive regressions (eq. 1) of 2- and 4-quarter growth in GDP, payroll employment, IP and the unemployment rate on lags of the activity variable and the index.

**Results used here (section 5, pp. 22-27).** Purging helps in the early 1990s and the last decade; purged vs unpurged differ most in the mid-1970s/early 1980s and 2009. End-2009: unpurged index "essentially neutral" (conditions near average), purged index still a drag conditional on the pace of recovery. Purging the funds rate barely changes forecast performance: "pure monetary shocks contributed relatively little to the prediction of future economic activity," though policy mattered in specific episodes (1987, 2008-09). Predictive performance is unstable over time. Footnote 12: leverage levels are not captured.

**Dudley's comments (pp. 56-61).** Taylor-rule equilibrium rate is not constant: higher productivity growth or higher stock prices raise the equilibrium real funds rate; "Higher stock prices meant that financial conditions were easier. This needed to be offset by a somewhat higher fed funds rate target." Financial conditions have become more volatile relative to the funds rate. This is the 2010 ancestor of the r*/FCI* idea.

## 3. Relation to our work [inference unless marked]
1. **VAR with the unemployment gap first.** Same logic as eq. (2): isolate the index's own innovations from feedback of activity. Differences: ours is dynamic (12 lags, one activity variable, impulse responses) and purges the *innovation*, leaving the level unpurged; theirs purges each indicator on current + 2 lags of GDP growth and inflation and then summarizes. Our level is like their unpurged index and like GS; the analogue of their purged index is ANFCI (purged on activity and inflation). Their finding that purged and unpurged diverge in 1975-82 and 2009 suggests checking our index around those dates.
2. **Weights.** Weighted-sum family, as GS. Our weights are neither FRB/US simulations nor PCA loadings; they come from observed responses to policy news and take the growth link from the VAR. Note A should say plainly that policy-news weights answer "what does policy do to conditions", while GS/FCI-G weights answer "what do conditions do to growth", and the VAR supplies the second step.
3. **Neutral.** In 2010 "neutral" already has the statistical sense (average); FCI-G/DB/MA have a growth-neutral zero; Dudley (2010) has the level-neutral sense (equilibrium rate moving with conditions). FCI* (Caballero et al.) formalizes the third. The market index offers a flow (growth/activity-neutral zero) and an r*-gap level (level-neutral in spirit).
4. **Funds rate.** They exclude it (and test purging it); GS includes it at 4.4%; FCI-G includes it; our index has none of the policy rate directly (weights on 10-year, CAPE, dollar, with the policy move in the unit). Their 5.2 result (pure monetary shocks add little to predicting activity) sits alongside Note B (the 2-year alone gives a wrong-signed unemployment response post-1994 unless the FCI is included) and the MP project's finding (credit variables, not the index, fix the sign). Worth a discussion paragraph rather than a ranking.
5. **Evaluation.** Their eq. (1) design (activity h quarters ahead on own lags plus the indicator) is the template for the Note A box (unemployment a year ahead, out-of-sample ratios).
6. **Caveats they already give:** Lucas critique; instability over time; selection bias from fitting to the crisis; leverage levels not captured. Our caveats should cite them rather than present these as new.
